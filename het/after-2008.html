Economics After 2008: The Revival of Political Economy

The financial crisis of 2008 sent economists back to questions that the textbooks had set aside: instability, inequality, power, and the limits of the planet. This closing topic looks at how the profession responded, at the return of distribution to the center of debate, and at what the history of economic thought offers for the problems of today.

1. The crisis and the profession

In September 2008 the investment bank Lehman Brothers failed, and the world financial system came close to collapse. Output and trade fell faster than at any time since the Great Depression. Visiting the London School of Economics that November, Queen Elizabeth II reportedly asked why nobody had seen the crisis coming. A group of British economists replied in a letter in 2009 that it had been a failure of “the collective imagination of many bright people” to understand the risks to the system as a whole.

The crisis exposed the limits of the dominant macroeconomic models, which had little room for banks, debt, or default, and of the belief that financial markets price risk efficiently (Topics 14 and 16). It revived interest in thinkers who had stressed instability: Keynes, Kalecki, Minsky, and Godley (Topics 12 and 15), and, from a different direction, the Austrians (Topic 14). In the following decade, the slow recovery, the debate over austerity, the eurozone crisis, the COVID-19 recession of 2020, the inflation of 2021–23, and climate change kept those questions open.

Mainstream economics also changed. It became far more empirical, using large administrative datasets and natural experiments; it added financial frictions and unequal households to its macroeconomic models; and it took inequality, market power, and behavioral evidence more seriously. Critics argue that these changes leave the core of the neoclassical framework untouched.

2. The return of inequality

The crisis also revived questions of distribution that the classical economists had placed at the center of the discipline. Thomas Piketty’s Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013), built on work with Anthony Atkinson, Emmanuel Saez, and Gabriel Zucman, assembled two centuries of tax records to show that inequality of income and wealth fell between 1914 and the 1970s and has risen since in most rich countries. This challenged the optimism of Simon Kuznets, who had suggested in 1955 that inequality falls naturally in the later stages of growth (Topic 13); for Piketty, the mid-century decline was the product of wars, depression, and policy, such as progressive taxation and strong unions.

Piketty summarized his argument in two “laws.” The first is an accounting identity: capital’s share of income α equals the rate of return on capital r times the ratio of capital to income β. The second says that in the long run β tends toward the net saving rate s divided by the growth rate g. When growth slows while saving does not, capital accumulates relative to income; and when r exceeds g, inherited wealth grows faster than the economy, and the past tends to “devour the future.”

The book became a bestseller and brought the language of Ricardo and Marx, of rent, capital, and class shares, back into public debate. Critics questioned whether the return on capital stays high as capital accumulates, whether housing explains most of the rise in capital, and whether r > g is the main mechanism. Post-Keynesians and Marxists added that distribution is set by power and institutions, not by a law of accumulation.

3. The critical traditions today

The final chapters of Hunt and Lautzenheiser trace the traditions that kept classical questions alive. Clarence Ayres (Topic 10) saw economic progress as a struggle between technology and “ceremonial” institutions. Post-Keynesians built on Keynes, Kalecki, and Joan Robinson (Topic 15), and Sraffians on Sraffa’s revival of classical price theory (Topic 13). Harry Braverman’s Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974) revived Marx’s analysis of the labor process, arguing that twentieth-century management, from Frederick Taylor’s “scientific management” onward, deskilled work to increase control over workers. Hunt and Lautzenheiser’s last chapter surveys a wider revival of research since the 1960s:

  • Value and profit. Anwar Shaikh’s work on the transformation problem and on how closely labor values track prices, and his national accounts in Marxian categories with E. Ahmet Tonak (1994); the “new interpretation” of value of Gérard Duménil and Duncan Foley; studies of the profit rate by Duménil and Dominique Lévy (1993) and Robert Brenner (1998). See Topic 7.
  • Stagnation and empire. Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy’s Monopoly Capital (1966) and Harry Magdoff’s The Age of Imperialism (1969). See Topics 11 and 13.
  • Work, schooling, and the media. Braverman on the labor process; Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis’s Schooling in Capitalist America (1976); Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky’s Manufacturing Consent (1988).
  • Long waves and growth. The social structures of accumulation framework, which links long waves of growth to the institutions that support them; Edward Nell’s “transformational growth”; Heinz Kurz and Neri Salvadori’s Sraffian Theory of Production (1995); and Duncan Foley and Thomas Michl’s Growth and Distribution (1999).
  • Alternatives to capitalism. John Roemer’s A Future for Socialism (1994), a model of market socialism, and Michael Albert and Robin Hahnel’s The Political Economy of Participatory Economics (1991), a model of socialism without markets.

4. Pluralism and the teaching of economics

Students, too, pushed for change. After 2008, groups such as Rethinking Economics (founded in 2012) and the International Student Initiative for Pluralism in Economics (2014), whose open letter was signed by student groups from some thirty countries, called for teaching that includes the history of economic thought, competing schools, and the real economy. Within the mainstream, the CORE project’s free textbook The Economy (2017) began with inequality, history, and institutions rather than with the supply-and-demand diagram. Ha-Joon Chang’s Economics: The User’s Guide (2014) argues that there are at least nine schools of economics, each with its strengths and blind spots, and that students should know them all. That is also the argument of this course.
