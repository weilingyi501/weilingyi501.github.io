Chicago and Vienna: The Neoliberal Turn

Two free-market traditions that waited out the Keynesian decades, and then shaped economic policy from the 1970s onward. They agree on the virtues of markets, and disagree on almost everything else.

1. From stagflation to Thatcher and Reagan

By the late 1960s the postwar boom was slowing. In 1971 the United States ended the convertibility of the dollar into gold, and the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates collapsed. Oil prices quadrupled in 1973–74 and doubled again in 1979. Rich economies now suffered high inflation and high unemployment at the same time, stagflation, which the simple Keynesian models of the day, with their stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment, could not explain.

Free-market economists had been waiting for this moment. In 1947 Friedrich Hayek gathered liberal economists and philosophers, including Milton Friedman, George Stigler, Frank Knight, and Ludwig von Mises, at Mont Pèlerin in Switzerland to found a society devoted to defending the market against planning and the welfare state. In the 1970s their ideas moved from the margins to the center: Hayek received the Nobel Prize in 1974 and Friedman in 1976. Paul Volcker’s Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply from 1979, Margaret Thatcher came to power in 1979 and Ronald Reagan in 1981, and the policies later summed up as the Washington Consensus (deregulation, privatization, free trade, and free capital flows) spread across the world.

2. Two traditions, two cities

The Austrian school began with Carl Menger’s Principles in Vienna in 1871 (Week 8) and continued with Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk’s theory of capital and interest (Week 9). In the 1920s and 1930s Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek made it a force in the debates over socialism and the business cycle. Both left Austria before the Nazi annexation of 1938: Mises eventually settled in New York, and Hayek moved to the London School of Economics in 1931 and later to the University of Chicago (1950–62), though to its Committee on Social Thought rather than the economics department. After Mises, the tradition continued through students such as Israel Kirzner and Murray Rothbard, and later at George Mason University.

The Chicago school grew out of the University of Chicago’s economics department. Its first generation in the 1920s and 1930s, Frank Knight, Jacob Viner, and Henry Simons, combined neoclassical price theory with a liberal suspicion of concentrated power, public or private. Its second generation, from the late 1940s, was led by Milton Friedman and George Stigler and included Ronald Coase, Gary Becker, and later Robert Lucas and Eugene Fama. Between 1974 and 2013, a striking number of Nobel prizes in economics went to economists associated with Chicago.

E. K. Hunt, in his account of contemporary orthodoxy, treats the two as “separate but similar schools” that share a commitment to individualism, voluntary exchange, and laissez-faire, and that differ mainly in method: the Austrians are rationalists, Friedman’s Chicago is empiricist. Hunt regards that methodological difference as minor. The comparison below suggests that the differences go further, even where the policy conclusions converge.

4. Friedman and the natural rate

In the 1960s many Keynesians read the Phillips curve, the observed negative relation between unemployment and inflation, as a menu: accept a little more inflation, get a little less unemployment. In his 1967 presidential address to the American Economic Association (published 1968), Friedman argued that this trade-off holds only while people are surprised. There is a natural rate of unemployment set by real factors. If policy holds unemployment below it, workers and firms come to expect higher inflation, build it into wages and prices, and inflation keeps rising. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical. Edmund Phelps made a similar argument at the same time.

inflation = expected inflation − β × (unemployment − natural rate)

Expectations:

Under adaptive expectations, people expect this year’s inflation to equal last year’s. Each year the government buys lower unemployment with a further rise in inflation, so inflation accelerates. When stagflation arrived in the 1970s, Friedman’s argument looked like a successful prediction, which is exactly the test his methodology prescribes.

5. Rational expectations and the Lucas critique

Robert Lucas and other new classical economists in the 1970s went further. If people form expectations rationally, using all available information including knowledge of the policy rule, they cannot be systematically fooled even in the short run. Switch the interactive above to “Rational”: a predictable attempt to hold unemployment down produces only inflation. Lucas’s critique of 1976 added that statistical relationships estimated under one policy regime will shift when the regime changes, so Keynesian econometric models could not be used to evaluate new policies. Macroeconomics was rebuilt on “microfoundations”: optimizing households and firms with rational expectations.

The response, new Keynesian economics, kept rational expectations but added sticky prices and wages, which restored a short-run role for monetary policy. By the 2000s the two had merged into a “new consensus” that guided central banks until the financial crisis of 2008 (Week 14).

6. The Austrian theory of the business cycle

The Austrian account starts from the interest rate. In Hayek’s version, the “natural” rate reflects how willing people are to save, that is, to postpone consumption. When banks or a central bank push the market rate below it, investment in long, capital-intensive projects looks profitable even though people have not saved enough to complete them. Resources are drawn into “higher stages” of production far from consumers. When the credit expansion stops, or consumer demand reasserts itself, many of these projects turn out to be unsustainable: the boom has produced malinvestment, and the bust is the painful process of liquidating it.

The policy conclusion is the opposite of Keynes’s and of Friedman’s: do not prevent the liquidation with cheap money or public spending. Hayek and Keynes argued over this directly in the early 1930s. Most economists judged that Keynes won that debate during the Depression, but the Austrian theory found new audiences after the dot-com bubble of 2000 and the housing bubble of 2008.

7. Chicago beyond macroeconomics

  • Regulatory capture. George Stigler (1971) argued that regulation is usually acquired by the industry it regulates and run for its benefit, a theory of government failure to set beside the theory of market failure.
  • The Coase theorem. Ronald Coase (1960) showed that if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, private parties can resolve externalities like pollution without taxes or regulation. Coase himself stressed that transaction costs are rarely low, but the result became the basis for tradable pollution permits.
  • Human capital and “economic imperialism.” Gary Becker applied the logic of rational choice to education, discrimination, crime, and the family.
  • Law and economics, and antitrust. Aaron Director, Richard Posner, and Robert Bork reshaped American antitrust law around a narrow “consumer welfare” standard, a long way from Henry Simons’s earlier hostility to monopoly.
  • Efficient markets. Eugene Fama’s efficient markets hypothesis held that asset prices reflect all available information, an idea that became central to finance and was badly tested in 2008.

8. Critics and consequences

Hunt’s criticism applies to both schools. By reducing all economic life to voluntary exchange between individuals with given “initial endowments,” they leave out how those endowments came about, and the difference between owning means of production and having only one’s labor to sell. A wage bargain is voluntary, but the alternatives open to each side are very different. Hunt also questions their claim to be value-free; Joan Robinson made the same point, arguing that every economist has political views and that honesty requires stating them.

Defenders point to the end of the high inflation of the 1970s, to the failures of central planning that Mises and Hayek predicted, and to the economic opening of China and India. Critics point to slower growth than in the golden age, rising inequality, the weakening of labor, repeated financial crises, and the bailouts of 2008, in which governments rescued the financial system on a scale neither school would have endorsed.

Discussion: Was neoliberalism a victory of ideas, or did the ideas win because the conditions of the 1970s, and the interests of those who gained from deregulation, favored them? How would each school we have studied answer that question?
