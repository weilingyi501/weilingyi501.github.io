Ricardo and Malthus: Rent, Gluts, and the Corn Laws

Two friends who disagreed about almost everything: how rent is formed, whether profits must fall, whether trade should be free, and whether a market economy can produce too much.

1. War, grain, and class conflict

During the wars with revolutionary and Napoleonic France (1793–1815), imports of grain to Britain were disrupted and prices soared. Farmers brought poorer land under the plough, and landlords’ rents rose. When peace returned and grain prices fell, Parliament, dominated by landowners, passed the Corn Law of 1815, which barred imports of wheat until the domestic price reached 80 shillings a quarter. (“Corn” in British usage meant grain in general.)

The Corn Laws set the economic interests of Britain’s three great classes against one another:

LandlordsReceived rent. They gained from high grain prices, and in Ricardo’s and Malthus’s accounts spent much of their income on luxuries, servants, and the arts.
Industrial capitalistsReceived profits. High grain prices forced them to pay higher money wages, cutting profits; they saved and invested to accumulate capital.
WorkersReceived wages, near subsistence. They spent everything on necessities, above all bread.

These were years of open conflict: Luddite machine-breaking (1811–16), the Peterloo massacre in Manchester (1819), when cavalry charged a crowd demanding parliamentary reform, and the long campaign of the Anti-Corn Law League, which ended with repeal in 1846. David Ricardo, a stockbroker turned economist and Member of Parliament, took the side of the industrial capitalists. Thomas Robert Malthus, a clergyman and the first professor of political economy in England, defended the landlords. They debated in print and in a long, friendly correspondence until Ricardo’s death in 1823.

2. Ricardo’s theory of rent

Ricardo’s theory of differential rent (1815, 1817) rests on three assumptions: land differs in fertility; competition equalizes the rate of profit among capitalist farmers; and the least fertile land in use, the margin of cultivation, pays no rent. On every better plot, competition among farmers for land drives rent up until it absorbs the extra produce that the plot yields above the marginal plot. Rent is therefore not a reward for any productive effort of the landlord; it arises from differences in fertility and from the fact that land is privately owned.

3. The falling rate of profit and the stationary state

The model above contains Ricardo’s theory of distribution in miniature. Capital accumulation raises the demand for labor; wages rise above subsistence for a while; population grows; more food is needed; poorer land is brought into cultivation. At the new margin, the same capital yields less, so the profit rate, which competition makes the same everywhere, falls. Rents on all the better land rise. Wages return to subsistence, but the subsistence wage, measured in effort and land, has become more expensive.

As this continues, the profit rate approaches zero and accumulation stops: the stationary state. The only beneficiaries are the landlords. Hence Ricardo’s case against the Corn Laws: importing cheaper grain from abroad would halt the move to poorer land at home, keep profits up, and let accumulation continue. As he put it, the interest of the landlord is “always opposed to the interest of every other class in the community” (Essay on the Influence of a Low Price of Corn on the Profits of Stock, 1815).

Ricardo’s pessimism did not come true in Britain, mainly because agricultural productivity rose and cheap grain flowed in from the Americas and Eastern Europe after repeal. But his argument that rent is an unearned income, and his method of building a model of the whole economy from a few assumptions, shaped economics for the next two centuries. Marx (Week 7) kept the falling profit rate but moved its cause from agriculture to mechanization.

4. Comparative advantage

In chapter 7 of the Principles (1817), Ricardo showed that two countries can both gain from trade even if one of them is more productive in everything. What matters is not absolute advantage but comparative advantage: each country should specialize in the good it produces at the lowest opportunity cost.

5. Malthus on population

Malthus first wrote his Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) against the radical optimism of William Godwin and Condorcet, who believed society could be made far more equal and prosperous. Population, Malthus argued, tends to grow geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8 …) while food supply grows at best arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4 …). Any improvement in the condition of the poor would therefore be eaten up by population growth, held in check only by “misery and vice”: famine, disease, and war. In later editions he added “moral restraint,” late marriage and abstinence, as a gentler check.

Two conclusions followed that made Malthus influential and controversial. Wages could not stay above subsistence for long. And poor relief was self-defeating, because it encouraged the poor to have more children; this argument helped shape the harsh New Poor Law of 1834 (Week 5).

The “Malthusian trap” describes most of human history fairly well: before the nineteenth century, gains in output were largely absorbed by larger populations. Since then, world food production has grown faster than population, thanks to fertilizers, new crop varieties, and mechanization, and birth rates have fallen as incomes rose. But Malthus’s question has returned in new forms: limits to fossil energy, soil, water, and the climate (Week 14). Marx and Engels attacked the population principle, arguing that unemployment and poverty come from capital’s demand for labor, not from a law of nature.

6. Gluts, effectual demand, and Say’s law

Malthus asked whether a capitalist economy could produce more than it could sell, a general glut. Commodities sell at their natural prices only if there is enough effectual demand: spending by people willing and able to pay. Workers spend all their wages on subsistence; landlords spend their rents on comforts and services; capitalists, driven by the passion to accumulate, try to invest their profits. But as accumulation proceeds, especially with labor-saving machinery, capitalists receive more profit than they can profitably invest, demand falls short, and unsold goods pile up. Malthus concluded that landlords’ unproductive spending was useful, because it kept demand up.

Ricardo, following Jean-Baptiste Say, denied that a general glut was possible. People produce in order to buy: every sale puts money in someone’s hands that will be spent on something else. There could be too much of a particular good, but not too much of everything. This became known as Say’s law, often summarized as “supply creates its own demand.” Ricardo’s view prevailed for over a century. Keynes, writing in 1936, regretted that it had, and praised Malthus as an early theorist of effective demand (Week 11).

7. Ricardo on machinery

In the third edition of the Principles (1821), Ricardo added a new chapter, “On Machinery,” in which he changed his mind. He had believed that introducing machinery benefited all classes. He now argued that replacing workers with machines could reduce the gross product available to pay wages, so that “the opinion entertained by the labouring class, that the employment of machinery is frequently detrimental to their interests, is not founded on prejudice and error.” It was a rare admission by a founder of economics, and a question that returns with every new technology, from the power loom to artificial intelligence.

Discussion: Ricardo argued that a tax on imported grain benefits one class at the expense of all others. Can you think of a present-day policy that is debated in similar terms? Who are the “landlords” today?
