Imperialism, Dependency, and the World-System

A century-long line of argument about capitalism as a world system. It began with the question of why capitalist powers seized colonies, continued with the question of why former colonies stayed poor, and arrived at the study of the world economy as a hierarchy of core and periphery.

1. The age of empire

Between the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 and the First World War, European powers divided almost all of Africa among themselves and extended their rule across much of Asia and the Pacific. By 1914 the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world’s land. Britain had also become the world’s banker: on the eve of the war, its income from foreign investment was large enough to cover a persistent deficit in its trade in goods. Germany, the United States, and Japan rose as industrial rivals, and the great powers raced to build navies.

Neoclassical economics, meanwhile, was building a theory of competitive markets populated by small firms and rational individuals (Topics 8–9). The thinkers of this topic argued that the picture missed what mattered most about their world: concentrated corporate and financial power, and the expansion of capital across the globe by force. Hunt treats them together in chapter 13.

2. Hobson: underconsumption and empire

John A. Hobson (1858–1940), an English liberal journalist and economist, argued in Imperialism: A Study (1902), written after the Boer War, that empire was driven not by national glory but by a domestic economic problem. Income in Britain was so unequally distributed that the rich could not spend it all and workers could not afford to buy what they produced. The resulting surplus of savings sought profitable outlets abroad, and investors pressed the state to secure colonies to protect their investments. Imperialism benefited a narrow class of financiers at the cost of the nation as a whole. Hobson’s remedy was reform at home: redistribute income, raise wages, and domestic demand would absorb the surplus. Keynes later credited Hobson as a forerunner of the theory of effective demand (Topic 12).

3. Hilferding: finance capital

The Austrian Marxist Rudolf Hilferding (1877–1941), later twice German finance minister, argued in Finance Capital (1910) that capitalism had changed since Marx. Industry was now organized in cartels and trusts, and the great banks, which financed industry through long-term credit and share issues, had fused with it into finance capital. Finance capital wanted protective tariffs to raise cartel profits at home, and it pushed the state to secure foreign markets, raw materials, and fields of investment abroad. Free trade, the creed of competitive capitalism, gave way to protection, militarism, and empire.

4. Luxemburg: accumulation and the noncapitalist world

Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919), a leading figure of German and Polish socialism, argued in The Accumulation of Capital (1913) that a purely capitalist economy faces a persistent problem of realization: who buys the extra goods that accumulation produces, if workers are paid less than the value they create and capitalists keep investing? Her answer was that capitalism has always depended on markets, land, labor, and resources outside the capitalist system: peasant economies, colonies, and precapitalist societies, which it absorbs and transforms, often by force. Militarism and colonial conquest were not accidents but conditions of capitalist expansion. Her reading of Marx’s reproduction schemes was disputed even by fellow Marxists, but her emphasis on capitalism’s relation to its noncapitalist outside has influenced theories of development, ecology, and social reproduction (Topics 22–23).

5. Bukharin and Lenin: imperialism as a stage of capitalism

Nikolai Bukharin’s Imperialism and World Economy (written 1915) described a world economy in which national economies had become “state capitalist trusts” competing for markets and raw materials, so that war between them was built into the system. Vladimir Lenin’s Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (written 1916, published 1917) drew on Hobson, Hilferding, and Bukharin and summarized the new stage in five features:

  1. the concentration of production into monopolies;
  2. the merger of bank capital with industrial capital into finance capital, controlled by a financial oligarchy;
  3. the growing importance of the export of capital, as opposed to the export of goods;
  4. the formation of international monopolist associations that divide the world market among themselves;
  5. the completed territorial division of the world among the great powers.

Since the world was already divided, any change in the relative strength of the powers had to lead to struggles to redivide it. The First World War, in Lenin’s view, was exactly that. Unlike Hobson, he did not think reform could end imperialism: it was the logic of monopoly capitalism itself. Super-profits from the colonies also allowed capitalists to buy off a “labor aristocracy” at home, which Lenin used to explain why many socialist parties supported the war.

6. After empire: structuralism

After 1945 most colonies won independence, but the gap between rich and poor countries did not close. The first answers came from Latin America. Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer (1950) argued that the terms of trade of primary-commodity exporters tend to deteriorate against those of manufacturing exporters, because productivity gains in industry are kept as higher wages and profits in the core while those in primary production are passed on as lower prices. Under Prebisch, the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA) called the world economy a system of center and periphery and advocated industrialization behind protection (Topics 13 and 19). These “structuralists” were reformers, not Marxists, but they supplied the vocabulary for what followed.

7. Dependency theory

By the 1960s, import substitution had produced industry in Latin America but not the independence or equality that had been hoped for. A more radical answer, dependency theory, argued that underdevelopment is not an original state that poor countries will grow out of, but a product of how they were incorporated into the world economy.

  • Paul Baran’s The Political Economy of Growth (1957) argued that the economic surplus of the periphery was drained abroad by foreign capital or wasted by local elites, rather than invested.
  • Andre Gunder Frank’s “The Development of Underdevelopment” (1966) and Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America (1967) argued that Latin America had been capitalist since the Spanish conquest, integrated as a satellite of the metropolis, and that its regions were most underdeveloped where their ties to the world market had once been strongest.
  • Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto’s Dependency and Development in Latin America (1969) offered a more nuanced view: “dependent development” was possible through alliances between the state, local capital, and multinational firms, but it was shaped by those alliances and by class conflict within each country. Cardoso later became president of Brazil (1995–2002).
  • Theotonio dos Santos’ “The Structure of Dependence” (1970) defined dependence as a situation in which one country’s economy is conditioned by the development and expansion of another. Samir Amin’s Accumulation on a World Scale (1970) and Walter Rodney’s How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972) extended the argument to Africa.

Critics came from several directions. The Marxist Bill Warren (Imperialism: Pioneer of Capitalism, 1980) argued that capitalism was in fact industrializing the periphery. Robert Brenner (1977) criticized Frank and Wallerstein for locating capitalism in market exchange rather than in class relations of production. And the rapid industrialization of South Korea and Taiwan seemed to show that a peripheral position was not destiny, though the East Asian cases relied on exactly the kind of state-led development dependency theorists had called for (Topic 19).

8. World-systems analysis

Immanuel Wallerstein’s world-systems analysis, beginning with The Modern World-System (1974), took the whole capitalist world-economy, rather than the nation, as the unit of analysis. Wallerstein dated it to the “long sixteenth century” (about 1450–1640), when a single division of labor came to link Western Europe, Eastern Europe, and the Americas. The world-economy is divided into a core, a periphery, and a semi-periphery between them, distinguished not by what they produce but by the kinds of production they specialize in:

CoreMonopolized, high-profit, technologically leading activities; strong states; most of the world’s wealth.
Semi-peripheryA mix of core-like and peripheral activities; both exploited and exploiting; the zone of upward and downward mobility.
PeripheryHighly competitive, low-profit activities; weaker states; the great majority of the world’s population.

The approach was developed at the Fernand Braudel Center at Binghamton University, founded in 1976 and named after the French historian whose work on capitalism and the Mediterranean inspired it. Giovanni Arrighi’s The Long Twentieth Century (1994) traced successive systemic cycles of accumulation, each led by a hegemonic center (Genoa, the Dutch Republic, Britain, the United States). Each cycle begins with expansion in trade and production and ends with a phase of financial expansion, which Arrighi read as a sign of the leading power’s decline. Arrighi and Jessica Drangel (1986) found a trimodal distribution of income per person across countries: the world’s population clustered around three income levels, and very few countries moved between the clusters. Christopher Chase-Dunn and others have tested the framework with quantitative and long-run comparative data.

9. Unequal exchange

The theory of unequal exchange was set out by the Greek-French economist Arghiri Emmanuel in L’échange inégal (1969; English translation Unequal Exchange: A Study of the Imperialism of Trade, 1972), published together with a critical exchange with Charles Bettelheim. Emmanuel built on Marx’s transformation of values into prices of production (Topic 7) and applied it to the world economy.

His starting point was an asymmetry. Capital moves across borders, so competition tends to equalize the rate of profit internationally. Labor does not move freely, so wages stay very different between countries, and they are set largely by history, institutions, and class struggle, not by productivity alone. When goods are sold at prices of production that include an equal profit rate, the low-wage country’s products sell below their labor value and the high-wage country’s above it. Trade at market prices then transfers value from the low-wage country to the high-wage one, even if both use the same techniques and no one is cheated.

  • Unequal exchange in the broad sense arises from differences in the organic composition of capital. It is Marx’s ordinary transformation, which happens within any single country too.
  • Unequal exchange in the strict sense, Emmanuel’s real subject, arises from differences in wages between countries with equal profit rates. It is specific to the world economy.

Emmanuel’s model: same technique, different wages

Each country produces its export with 100 hours of labor and constant capital worth 100 hours, so each good contains 200 hours of value. Wages are measured in hours of value paid per 100 hours worked. Capital is mobile, so both earn the same rate of profit.

Emmanuel drew a sharp political conclusion: workers in rich countries share in the gains from unequal exchange, so international solidarity between workers of the core and the periphery cannot be taken for granted. Samir Amin (Unequal Development, 1973; The Law of Worldwide Value, 1978) integrated unequal exchange into a theory of accumulation on a world scale, stressing cases where productivity in the periphery’s export sectors is close to that of the core while wages are far lower.

The theory was controversial from the start. Bettelheim argued that wages differ because productivity and the development of the productive forces differ, so the transfer is not the cause of poverty but its consequence. Paul Samuelson (1976) dismissed the theory as a confusion. Others noted that Emmanuel’s mechanism differs from the Prebisch–Singer thesis (section 6), which concerns falling relative prices of primary goods, not wage differences with equal techniques. The debate fed into dependency theory and world-systems analysis, and later into theories of ecologically unequal exchange (Topic 22).

Discussion: If wages differ between countries mainly because productivity differs, is there still a transfer of value? What would Emmanuel, Bettelheim, Ricardo, and a neoclassical trade economist each say?
