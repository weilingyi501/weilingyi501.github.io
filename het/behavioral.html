Behavioral Economics

Neoclassical theory assumed that people maximize utility with stable preferences and the ability to process all relevant information. Behavioral economics asks how people actually decide, using psychology and experiments, and finds systematic departures from that ideal.

1. Simon: bounded rationality

Herbert Simon (1916–2001) was a political scientist, psychologist, computer scientist, and economist at Carnegie Mellon University. In Administrative Behavior (1947) and “A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice” (1955), he argued that real decision makers have bounded rationality: limited information, limited time, and limited ability to compute. Instead of optimizing, they satisfice, a word he coined from “satisfy” and “suffice”: they search until they find an option that is good enough, then stop. Organizations, rules of thumb, and routines exist partly to economize on scarce attention. Simon received the Nobel Prize in 1978. His work was more influential in management, organization theory, and artificial intelligence than in economics, where the optimizing model remained dominant for decades.

Simon’s skepticism about “economic man” echoed Veblen’s mockery of the “lightning calculator of pleasures and pains” (Topic 10), and his attention to organizations and routines influenced Nelson and Winter’s evolutionary economics (Topic 20).

2. Early anomalies

John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern’s Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944) gave economics a precise theory of choice under risk: expected utility. A rational person weighs each outcome’s utility by its probability. Almost at once, experiments found people violating it. In the Allais paradox (1953), the French economist Maurice Allais showed that most people prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble with a small chance of nothing, yet reverse their preference when both options are made less likely by the same proportion, which expected utility theory does not allow. Daniel Ellsberg (1961) showed that people dislike ambiguity, unknown probabilities, as distinct from risk.

3. Kahneman and Tversky: heuristics and biases

The Israeli psychologists Daniel Kahneman (1934–2024) and Amos Tversky (1937–1996) began working together in 1969. In “Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases” (Science, 1974), they showed that people judge probabilities with mental shortcuts that work well most of the time but lead to systematic errors:

  • Representativeness: judging how likely something is by how much it resembles a stereotype, while ignoring base rates.
  • Availability: judging frequency by how easily examples come to mind, so vivid events such as plane crashes seem more common than they are.
  • Anchoring: estimates are pulled toward an arbitrary starting number, even one known to be random.

The point was not that people are stupid, but that their errors are predictable, and predictable errors can be built into economic models.

4. Prospect theory

“Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk” (Econometrica, 1979) became one of the most cited papers in economics. It replaced expected utility with three ideas:

  • Reference dependence: people evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually the status quo, not as final levels of wealth.
  • Loss aversion: losses hurt more than equal gains please, by a factor of roughly two.
  • Diminishing sensitivity: the difference between $100 and $200 feels larger than between $1,100 and $1,200, for both gains and losses. People are therefore risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses.

Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in 2002, shared with Vernon Smith; Tversky had died in 1996, and the prize is not awarded posthumously. Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) popularized the distinction between fast, intuitive “System 1” and slow, deliberate “System 2” thinking.

5. Thaler and the economics of everyday life

Richard Thaler, who began as a skeptic among Chicago-trained economists, collected “anomalies” in a column he began in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 1987. His contributions include mental accounting, the habit of treating money differently depending on which mental account it belongs to; the endowment effect, the tendency to value something more once we own it (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler, 1990, using coffee mugs); and work on self-control and present bias, which explains why people save too little for retirement even when they intend to save more. Thaler received the Nobel Prize in 2017.

6. Nudges and policy

Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein’s Nudge (2008) argued for “libertarian paternalism”: designing the setting in which people choose, the “choice architecture,” so that they are more likely to choose well, without forbidding any option. The best-known example is automatic enrollment in workplace pension plans, which raised participation dramatically because few people opt out of a default. Britain created a Behavioural Insights Team, the “nudge unit,” in 2010, and many governments followed. The World Bank devoted its World Development Report 2015 to “Mind, Society, and Behavior.”

7. Behavioral finance and experiments

Robert Shiller showed that stock prices move far more than the dividends they are supposed to forecast (1981), and his Irrational Exuberance (2000) warned of the dot-com bubble and, in its second edition (2005), of the housing bubble. He shared the Nobel Prize in 2013 with Lars Peter Hansen and with Eugene Fama, whose efficient markets hypothesis (Topic 14) he challenged. Vernon Smith pioneered experimental economics, testing market institutions in the laboratory, and found, somewhat in tension with the behavioral program, that competitive markets often reach equilibrium even when individual traders are far from perfectly rational.

8. Critics and limits

  • Ecological rationality. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer argues that simple heuristics are often smart, not biased: in an uncertain world, a rule of thumb can outperform complex optimization.
  • The individual, not the system. Critics from institutionalist, post-Keynesian, and Marxian traditions note that behavioral economics mostly corrects the individual in the model and leaves the theory of markets, power, and distribution untouched. Nudges ask individuals to choose better within existing institutions rather than changing the institutions.
  • Replication. Some well-known findings in social psychology have failed to replicate, and the size of nudge effects in published studies has turned out to be smaller once unpublished trials are counted.
  • Absorption. Behavioral ideas such as present bias and loss aversion have been absorbed into mainstream models as extra parameters, which some see as success and others as domestication.
Discussion: If people’s choices are shaped by defaults and framing, who should decide how choices are framed? Is a nudge more or less respectful of people than a rule or a tax?
