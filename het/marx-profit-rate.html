Marx on Profit and Crisis

Why Marx expected the rate of profit to fall as capitalism develops, what might stop it, and how modern economists have tried to measure and test the idea.

1. Volumes 2 and 3 of Capital

Marx published only the first volume of Capital (1867) in his lifetime. Friedrich Engels assembled the second (1885) and third (1894) from Marx’s manuscripts. Volume 3 contains the two arguments of this week: how labor values are transformed into prices of production when profit rates equalize (worked through in the Topic 3 module), and the “law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall.”

The idea was not new. Smith had expected profits to fall as capital accumulated and competition intensified; Ricardo had blamed the falling profit rate on diminishing returns in agriculture and rising rents (Topic 4); Mill had expected a stationary state. Marx’s innovation was to locate the cause inside production itself, in the way capitalist competition drives mechanization. He wrote in a period of recurring crises, including the panics of 1857 and 1866, and Engels edited Volume 3 during the Long Depression of 1873–96.

2. The rate of profit

Recall Marx’s division of the value of output into constant capital (C, means of production used up), variable capital (V, wages), and surplus value (S). The rate of profit is surplus value relative to all capital advanced:

r = S / (C + V) = (S/V) / (C/V + 1)

The second form divides top and bottom by V. It shows that the profit rate depends on two ratios:

  • The rate of surplus value (S/V), or rate of exploitation: surplus value produced per dollar of wages. It rises if the working day is longer, work is more intense, or productivity in wage goods rises faster than real wages.
  • The organic composition of capital (C/V): the value of means of production relative to wages. It rises with mechanization, as each worker operates more and more machinery and materials.

3. The tendency to fall

Competition pushes each capitalist to adopt labor-saving machinery to cut costs. As this spreads, C/V rises. Only living labor produces surplus value, so if the rate of surplus value does not rise fast enough to compensate, the rate of profit falls. Marx called it a “tendency” because other forces work against it. Experiment with the race between mechanization and exploitation:

4. Counteracting tendencies

In Volume 3, Marx listed forces that slow or reverse the fall:

Counteracting tendencyHow it raises the profit rate
Raising the intensity of exploitationLonger hours or faster work raise S/V.
Depressing wages below the value of labor powerLower wages raise S and lower V.
Cheapening the elements of constant capitalProductivity growth in the industries that make machines and materials lowers the value of C, even if the physical quantity of means of production per worker rises.
Relative surplus populationA reserve army of unemployed workers holds wages down and supports labor-intensive branches.
Foreign tradeCheaper imported inputs and wage goods, and higher profits on capital invested abroad.
Increase of share capitalCapital that earns only interest or dividends stays out of the calculation of the general profit rate.

The third item is the crux of the debate. If technical progress cheapens machines as fast as it multiplies them, the value composition C/V need not rise at all.

5. A modern formulation

Modern Marxian economists usually measure the profit rate with national accounts data rather than labor values. Dividing total profit Π by the capital stock K, and multiplying and dividing by output Y:

r = Π / K = (Π / Y) × (Y / K)

The profit share Π/Y reflects the balance of power between capital and labor. The capital–output ratio K/Y plays the role of the organic composition. It is not fixed. In the long run it tends toward the ratio of net investment to output divided by the growth rate of output:

(K/Y)* = (net investment / Y) / (growth rate of Y)

For example, if net investment is about 3% of output and output grows about 2.5% a year, the capital–output ratio settles near 1.2. If growth slows while firms keep investing the same share of output, capital piles up relative to output and the profit rate falls.

6. Three theories of crisis

Later Marxian economists developed three main accounts of why profitability, and with it investment and employment, periodically collapses:

TheoryMechanismMain channel
Profit squeezeRapid accumulation drains the reserve army of labor; workers gain bargaining power; wages rise faster than productivity.Falling profit share
UnderconsumptionRising exploitation holds wages down; consumption lags behind output; factories run below capacity.Rising K/Y through idle capacity
Rising organic compositionTechnical change is biased toward capital; capital per worker rises faster than labor productivity.Rising K/Y through mechanization

The three are not mutually exclusive. Many historians of the postwar economy argue that a profit squeeze in the late 1960s, combined with slowing productivity, helped end the golden age (Topic 13), while the decades of wage restraint that followed raised the profit share but left demand dependent on debt (Topic 15).

7. Objections and evidence

  • The Okishio theorem (1961). Nobuo Okishio showed that if capitalists only adopt techniques that cut costs at current prices, and the real wage stays constant, the new equilibrium profit rate cannot fall. On this view, the profit rate falls only if real wages rise or competition forces firms into techniques that lower profitability overall. Defenders of Marx reply that the theorem compares equilibria and ignores the dynamics of competition, fixed capital, and the time it takes for capital to be devalued.
  • Measurement. Results depend on choices: which industries count, how fixed capital is valued, whether profit is measured before or after taxes and interest. Studies for the U.S. and other rich economies commonly find a fall in the profit rate from the mid-1960s to the early 1980s and a partial recovery in the neoliberal decades that followed, driven more by the profit share than by the capital–output ratio.
  • Is it a theory of crisis at all? A slow trend cannot by itself explain sudden crises. Most modern Marxian accounts combine a long-run squeeze on profitability with short-run financial and demand mechanisms, much as Minsky and Keynes did from a different starting point (Topics 12 and 15).
Discussion: If the profit rate is driven mainly by the profit share rather than by mechanization, is that still Marx’s law? What would change about its political implications?

8. After Marx: the transformation and value debates

Marx’s transformation of values into prices of production (Topic 3 module) left the inputs valued at labor values rather than at prices. The debate over how to finish the job, and whether it matters, has run for more than a century.

  • Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz (1907) transformed the inputs as well. He showed that a consistent solution exists, but that, in general, only one of Marx’s two equalities can hold: total surplus value can equal total profit, or total value can equal total price, but not both.
  • Paul Sweezy’s The Theory of Capitalist Development (1942) introduced Bortkiewicz’s work to English-speaking readers and gave the first systematic account of Marxian economics in English. Sweezy put particular weight on underconsumption as a source of crisis.
  • Ian Steedman’s Marx After Sraffa (1977) drew a radical conclusion from Sraffa (Topic 13): if we know the physical conditions of production and the real wage, we can calculate prices and the rate of profit directly, so labor values are redundant. With joint production, he argued, labor values can even be negative while profits are positive.
  • Anwar Shaikh replied in several papers. In 1977, he showed that repeating Marx’s own procedure, using each round’s prices as the next round’s inputs, converges to Bortkiewicz’s prices. In “The Poverty of Algebra” (1981), he argued that Steedman’s logic, followed through, would make prices of production redundant too, and that “algebra is no substitute for logic.” He also defended the distinction between value produced in production and value realized in exchange.
  • The “new interpretation,” proposed by Gérard Duménil (1980), Duncan Foley (1982), and Alain Lipietz (1982), equates the value of the net product with its price and measures the wage as money actually paid. With these definitions, both of Marx’s equalities hold.
  • G. A. Cohen argued in “The Labour Theory of Value and the Concept of Exploitation” (1979) that the charge of exploitation does not need the labor theory of value: it rests on the simpler fact that workers, and not owners, produce the product.

These essays were collected with others in The Value Controversy (1981), edited from a 1978 London conference. Empirical work followed. Shaikh and E. Ahmet Tonak’s Measuring the Wealth of Nations (1994) recast national income accounts in Marxian categories, and Shaikh found that labor values track market prices closely in input–output data. Gérard Duménil and Dominique Lévy’s The Economics of the Profit Rate (1993) and Robert Brenner’s The Economics of Global Turbulence (1998) traced the long fall and partial recovery of profit rates in the rich economies.
