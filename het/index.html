History of Economic Thought

From mercantilism to the economics of the twenty-first century: how economists have explained value, distribution, and the stability of capitalism, and why their answers changed with the times.

About the course

Economic ideas are answers to the problems of their time. Adam Smith wrote as commerce was dissolving the old agrarian order; Ricardo and Malthus argued over the Corn Laws during the Industrial Revolution; Marx wrote amid the factories of Manchester and the revolutions of 1848; the marginalists wrote during the Long Depression; Keynes wrote during the Great Depression; and the monetarists rose with the stagflation of the 1970s. This course follows each school of thought through the historical conditions that produced it, the questions it tried to answer, and the debates it started.

Three questions run through the whole course:

ValueWhat determines the prices of commodities: labor, utility, or the costs of production?
DistributionHow is the social product divided among wages, profits, and rents, and is that division just?
StabilityDoes a market economy correct itself, or does it generate gluts, crises, and unemployment?

Learning goals

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Trace the major schools of economic thought from mercantilism to the present and place each in the economic, political, and social conditions that produced it.
  2. Compare how different schools answer the same questions about value, distribution, and the stability of markets.
  3. Work through key models by hand, including Ricardo’s natural price, Marx’s rate of profit, the marginal utility rule, and the Keynesian theory of effective demand.
  4. Use historical ideas to analyze present-day problems such as inequality, trade, financial crises, and climate change.

Readings

Main text

E. K. Hunt and Mark Lautzenheiser, History of Economic Thought: A Critical Perspective, 3rd ed. (M. E. Sharpe, 2011).

Companion texts

Robert Heilbroner, The Worldly Philosophers.

Linda Yueh, What Would the Great Economists Do? (Picador, 2018).

Ha-Joon Chang, Economics: The User’s Guide (Pelican, 2014), chapter 4.

Frank Stilwell, Political Economy: The Contest of Economic Ideas, 3rd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2012).

Primary sources

Short selections from Smith, Ricardo, Malthus, Mill, Marx, Jevons, Marshall, and Keynes, including chapters 12 and 24 of The General Theory.

Looking ahead

Jorgen Randers, 2052: A Global Forecast for the Next Forty Years (2012).

Peter Zeihan, The End of the World Is Just the Beginning (2022).
