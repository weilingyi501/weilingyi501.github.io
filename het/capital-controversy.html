The Golden Age and the Capital Controversy

How postwar economics combined Keynes with neoclassical theory, and how a debate between two Cambridges exposed a problem at the center of the neoclassical theory of distribution.

1. The golden age, 1945–73

The quarter-century after the Second World War was the fastest period of growth that Western Europe, North America, and Japan had ever seen. The Bretton Woods system (1944) fixed exchange rates to a dollar convertible into gold and allowed controls on capital flows. Governments committed themselves to full employment; welfare states and labor unions expanded; wages rose roughly with productivity. Former colonies won independence and pursued planned industrialization, and the Cold War turned economic performance into a contest between capitalism and socialism.

Economics changed with it. It became more mathematical, more American, and more confident that it could manage the economy. Two very different research programs grew up in two Cambridges: at MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, around Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow; and at the University of Cambridge in England, around Joan Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Piero Sraffa, and Luigi Pasinetti.

2. The neoclassical synthesis

In 1937 John Hicks condensed Keynes’s book into the IS–LM diagram: one curve for the goods market (investment equals saving), one for the money market (liquidity preference equals money supply). Paul Samuelson’s textbook Economics (1948) taught a generation that Keynesian policy could secure full employment, after which the familiar neoclassical results about markets and prices would hold. He called this the neoclassical synthesis.

What the synthesis kept from KeynesAggregate demand determines output in the short run; fiscal and monetary policy can stabilize it; unemployment can persist when wages are slow to adjust.
What it left out, according to post-KeynesiansFundamental uncertainty, the instability of investment and finance, and the claim that a market economy has no automatic tendency toward full employment even in the long run.

3. Growth theory: Harrod–Domar and Solow

Roy Harrod (1939) and Evsey Domar (1946) extended Keynes to growth. If the capital–output ratio is fixed, the economy grows steadily only if saving and investment keep capital growing exactly in step with the labor force. Any deviation tends to widen: the economy balances on a “knife-edge.”

Robert Solow’s growth model (1956) removed the knife-edge by letting firms substitute capital for labor along an aggregate production function, Y = F(K, L). The capital–labor ratio adjusts until the economy reaches a steady state, and long-run growth per person comes only from technological progress. The model became the foundation of mainstream growth economics. It also depends on treating K as a single quantity of “capital,” and that is where the trouble began.

4. What is “capital”?

The neoclassical theory of distribution (Week 9) says that, in competitive markets, the wage equals the marginal product of labor and the rate of interest (or profit) equals the marginal product of capital. More capital per worker lowers its marginal product, so a lower interest rate goes with more capital-intensive methods. For this story to work, “capital” must be a quantity that can be measured before we know the interest rate.

In 1953 Joan Robinson asked in what units capital is measured. Real capital is a collection of different goods: trucks, machines, buildings, software. To add them up we need prices. But the price of a machine includes the profit earned on the capital used to make it, so the value of the capital stock changes when the profit rate changes, even if not a single machine changes.

Ten trucks and five machines

Each truck needs $10,000 of labor and $10,000 of capital inputs; each machine needs $10,000 of labor and $20,000 of capital inputs. Price = labor cost + capital cost × (1 + profit rate).

The same physical capital stock has a different “quantity” at each profit rate. If we need the profit rate to measure capital, we cannot then explain the profit rate by the marginal product of that capital without arguing in a circle.

5. Sraffa and the wage–profit frontier

Piero Sraffa’s Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities (1960), a short book he had worked on since the 1920s, revived the classical approach of Ricardo. Given the technique of production, prices and one distributive variable can be determined once the other is known. If the profit rate is set, for instance, by the interest rate or by class conflict, the wage follows, and vice versa. Nothing in the system requires utility or marginal products.

For each technique there is a wage–profit frontier: the highest wage compatible with each profit rate. Higher profits mean lower wages; the shape of the curve depends on how capital is used across sectors. If every sector uses capital in the same proportion, the frontier is a straight line and prices are proportional to labor. Otherwise it bends. When firms can choose among techniques, they pick whichever gives the highest wage, or equivalently the lowest cost, at the going profit rate.

6. Reswitching

This example appeared in Paul Samuelson’s 1966 article “A Summing Up,” in which he conceded the point. Two techniques produce one unit of the same good using only labor, applied at different dates:

  • Technique A: 7 hours of labor, applied two years before the output is ready.
  • Technique B: 2 hours applied three years before, plus 6 hours applied one year before.

Wages paid in advance must earn the going profit rate r until the output is sold, so in units of the wage the costs are

cost A = 7(1 + r)²     cost B = 2(1 + r)³ + 6(1 + r)

Which technique is cheaper?

The line shows how much B costs relative to A at each profit rate. Shaded: the range where B is cheaper.

Setting the two costs equal gives 2(1 + r)² − 7(1 + r) + 6 = 0, with roots 1 + r = 1.5 and 1 + r = 2. So the techniques switch at r = 50% and switch back at r = 100%:

Profit rateCheaper techniqueWhat the neoclassical story would conclude
below 50%AA is chosen at low interest rates, so A must be the more capital-intensive technique.
50% to 100%BB is chosen at higher interest rates, so B must be less capital-intensive.
above 100%A againBut now A is chosen at an even higher interest rate, so A must be less capital-intensive than B.

Both conclusions cannot be true. The ranking of techniques by “capital intensity” depends on the profit rate, so there is no general, profit-independent sense in which one technique uses more capital than another. A fall in the interest rate need not lead firms to use more capital per worker, and the downward-sloping demand curve for capital that the neoclassical story needs is not guaranteed.

Try this: move the slider from 0% to 150% and watch which technique is chosen. Notice that technique A uses less total labor (7 hours against 8), yet it loses in the middle range.

7. Who won, and does it matter?

On logic, the English Cambridge won. Samuelson accepted that reswitching is possible and that aggregate capital cannot in general be measured independently of distribution. Yet the aggregate production function stayed at the center of growth theory, macroeconomics, and empirical work. Defenders replied that it remains a useful approximation, or that a full general-equilibrium model with many capital goods does not need an aggregate K. Critics replied that the approximation hides the question of how income is distributed between wages and profits.

Empirically, reswitching may be rare. Studies of input–output data, such as Ochoa’s (1989) work on the U.S. economy, found measured wage–profit curves to be close to straight lines, which is also why labor values track market prices closely (see Week 3). There is an irony here that Hunt emphasizes: the condition that makes reswitching unlikely is the same condition that makes the labor theory of value work well.

Discussion: If a theory is logically flawed but works well as an approximation, should economists keep using it? What would you need to know to decide?

8. Kuznets: measuring growth and inequality

Much of the golden age’s economics rested on a new kind of data. Simon Kuznets (1901–1985), a Russian-born American economist, built the first official estimates of U.S. national income in the 1930s, the ancestor of today’s GDP accounts, and later compiled long-run statistics of growth across countries; he received the Nobel Prize in 1971. In his 1955 presidential address to the American Economic Association, “Economic Growth and Income Inequality,” he suggested that inequality first rises as a country industrializes and workers move from farms to cities, and then falls: the inverted-U Kuznets curve. Kuznets warned that his evidence was thin, calling the paper perhaps “5 per cent empirical information and 95 per cent speculation.” Its optimistic message, that growth would reduce inequality by itself, nonetheless became a staple of the Cold War era. Thomas Piketty (Week 14) would later argue that the mid-century fall in inequality came from wars, depression, and policy, not from a natural stage of growth.

9. Monopoly capital

Marxian economics survived the golden age mainly outside the universities. Paul Sweezy (1910–2004), who left Harvard in the late 1940s, founded the magazine Monthly Review with Leo Huberman in 1949. His Theory of Capitalist Development (1942) had been the first systematic account of Marxian economics in English. Paul Baran (1909–1964), at Stanford, wrote The Political Economy of Growth (1957), arguing that underdevelopment in the periphery was the product of its relation to the advanced capitalist countries.

Together they wrote Monopoly Capital (1966), published after Baran’s death. They argued that the economy was now dominated by a few giant corporations that avoid price competition. Rising productivity with stable prices produces an economic surplus, the difference between what a society produces and the costs of producing it, that tends to rise over time. The problem is how to absorb it: capitalists’ consumption and investment are not enough. Left to itself, they wrote, “the normal state of the monopoly capitalist economy is stagnation.” Stagnation is held off by the sales effort (advertising, model changes, planned obsolescence), by government spending, especially on the military, and by imperialism. Harry Magdoff’s The Age of Imperialism (1969) documented the dependence of U.S. corporations on raw materials and markets abroad. Hunt treats their work as a central part of the revival of critical political economy in the 1960s (ch. 19).

Discussion: Baran and Sweezy wrote at the peak of the golden age. Does their stagnation thesis look better or worse in the light of the slow growth since 2008?

10. Development economics

The golden age also produced development economics, as newly independent countries asked how to industrialize. W. Arthur Lewis (1954) described a dual economy in which a modern sector grows by drawing labor from a traditional sector at a near-subsistence wage, so profits and investment rise while wages stay low until the surplus labor is absorbed. Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer (1950) argued that the terms of trade of primary-commodity exporters tend to decline against those of manufacturing exporters, a case for industrialization behind protection. These ideas returned in later debates about East Asia, China, and global value chains, and they connect to questions of unequal exchange (Week 10).
