Utility, Reform, and Dissent

Between Ricardo and Marx, political economy split. Radicals turned the labor theory of value into a demand for justice; utilitarians and their allies turned to utility, abstinence, and the defense of property; and John Stuart Mill tried to hold the tradition together.

1. Reform and unrest, 1820–1848

After Ricardo’s death in 1823, Britain’s industrial transformation accelerated. Trade unions became legal with the repeal of the Combination Acts in 1824, though their activities remained restricted. The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to more of the middle class but not to workers. The Factory Act of 1833 limited the hours of children in textile mills. The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, the “New Poor Law,” ended most outdoor relief for the able-bodied poor and sent them to workhouses deliberately made harsher than the worst paid work. The Chartist movement (1838–48) gathered millions of signatures for votes for working men. The Irish famine of 1845–52 killed about a million people. And in 1848, revolutions spread across Europe, the year of both Mill’s Principles and Marx and Engels’s Manifesto.

In this setting, the labor theory of value became politically charged. If labor creates value, as Ricardo said, why should profit and rent take a share of it? Economists divided over how to answer.

2. The utopian socialists

The first socialists did not start from Ricardo’s theory of value. They started from the misery of the early factory towns and the conviction that society could be redesigned along rational, cooperative lines. Marx and Engels later called them “utopian” because they appealed to reason and to the goodwill of the powerful rather than to the struggle of a working class, but they took many of their criticisms of capitalism from them.

Robert Owen (1771–1858), a Welsh-born cotton manufacturer, took over the mills at New Lanark, Scotland, in 1800. He shortened hours, refused to employ young children, opened a school and a company store selling goods near cost, and still made a profit. In A New View of Society (1813–16), he argued that character is formed by circumstances, so a better environment would make better people. When reformers and governments ignored him, he founded the cooperative community of New Harmony, Indiana (1825–27), which failed within two years. Back in Britain, Owenism became a mass movement: cooperative stores, the National Equitable Labour Exchange (1832), where goods were traded for “labour notes” measured in hours of work, and the short-lived Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (1834).

Henri de Saint-Simon (1760–1825), a French aristocrat who renounced his title during the Revolution, divided society into productive “industriels” (workers, manufacturers, bankers, scientists) and idle nobles, clergy, and officials. He wanted society organized for production and run by experts. His followers, the Saint-Simonians, attacked inheritance and promoted banks, railways, and canals; several became leading financiers and engineers of the Second Empire, and the Suez Canal owed much to their plans.

Charles Fourier (1772–1837), a commercial clerk in Lyon, denounced the waste and deceit of commerce and proposed that people live in self-governing communities of about 1,600, the phalanstère, where work would be varied and freely chosen so that it became “attractive.” Fourier argued that the condition of women was the best measure of social progress. Dozens of Fourierist communities were set up in the United States in the 1840s, and Brook Farm in Massachusetts converted to Fourierism in 1844.

What united these thinkers was the belief that competition and private property produce poverty in the midst of plenty, and that cooperation and planning could end it. Their ideas fed both the cooperative movement and, through Marx and Engels, the socialist movement.

3. The Ricardian socialists

A group of radical writers drew socialist conclusions from Ricardo. William Thompson, an Irish landowner and cooperative socialist, argued in An Inquiry into the Principles of the Distribution of Wealth (1824) that labor is the sole source of value, so laborers have a right to the whole product of their labor; profit and rent are deductions taken by those who own the means of production. Thompson also wrote, with Anna Wheeler, one of the earliest arguments for women’s political equality (1825). Thomas Hodgskin, in Labour Defended against the Claims of Capital (1825), argued that capital is not productive in itself: tools and machines are the product of past labor and produce nothing unless living labor uses them. These ideas fed into the early labor movement and the cooperative movement associated with Robert Owen, and Marx read them closely.

4. Bentham and the calculus of happiness

Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure.Jeremy Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. 1

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), a philosopher and legal reformer, argued that the right action or law is the one that produces “the greatest happiness of the greatest number.” Pleasures and pains could, he believed, be measured and added up. In his felicific calculus, the value of a pleasure or pain depends on its intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (how soon it comes), fecundity (whether it leads to more pleasure), purity (whether it is free of later pain), and extent (how many people it affects).

Bentham was a reformer, not a defender of the status quo: he campaigned for prison reform, the codification of law, and the extension of the vote, and he opposed laws against homosexuality in essays unpublished in his lifetime. But his calculus raised problems that economics has never resolved. Can a pleasure really be measured on a scale? Can one person’s pleasure be added to another’s? If utility is the measure of value, then the distribution of wealth that gives the most total utility might call for far more equality than the rich would accept, a radical implication that later utilitarian economists avoided by abandoning comparisons between people (Week 8).

5. Say: utility and the three productive agents

Jean-Baptiste Say (1767–1832), whose Treatise on Political Economy (1803) popularized Smith in France, argued that value comes from utility, not labor: things are valuable because they satisfy wants. Production is the creation of utility, and three “productive agents” contribute to it: human industry (labor), capital, and natural agents (land). Each receives its share of the product as a reward for its contribution: wages, interest, and rent. Say also gave a prominent place to the entrepreneur, who combines the agents of production. On this view, profits and rents are not deductions from labor’s product but payments for real contributions, an answer to the Ricardian socialists before they wrote. Say is best known today for Say’s law (Week 4).

6. Senior: abstinence, the wage fund, and the Poor Law

Nassau William Senior (1790–1864), the first Drummond Professor of Political Economy at Oxford, gave the defense of profit its most influential form. In his Outline of the Science of Political Economy (1836), he argued that profit is the reward for abstinence: the capitalist could have consumed his wealth but chose to save it, and that sacrifice deserves payment just as labor does. Critics, from Marx to Hunt, replied that the largest fortunes involve the least sacrifice, and that what the capitalist “abstains” from is consuming wealth that others produced.

Senior also helped establish the wage-fund doctrine: at any time, capitalists have a fixed fund set aside to pay workers, so the average wage equals that fund divided by the number of workers. If unions raised some wages, others would have to fall or workers would lose their jobs; only a smaller population or more capital could raise wages generally. As a member of the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws, he was a principal author of its 1834 report, which led to the New Poor Law. In 1837 he argued against shortening the factory working day on the ground that a mill’s whole net profit was produced in the last hour of the day, an argument Marx ridiculed in Capital as “Senior’s ‘Last Hour’.”

7. Bastiat and the harmony of interests

The French journalist and deputy Frédéric Bastiat (1801–1850) was the most popular free-trade writer of his day. In Economic Sophisms (1845), he mocked protectionism with a mock petition from the candle makers asking parliament to shut out the unfair competition of the sun. In “What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen” (1850), he introduced the parable of the broken window: the glazier’s gain from a broken window is seen, but the shoe or book the owner would otherwise have bought is not.

In Economic Harmonies (1850), written against the socialists of 1848, Bastiat argued that all exchange is an exchange of services, and that capitalists, landlords, and workers each render services to one another, so that their interests are in harmony, not conflict. Interest is the reward for the service of lending. In 1849–50, he debated Pierre-Joseph Proudhon on whether interest is legitimate. Hunt treats Bastiat as the purest form of utilitarian economics, in contrast with Mill’s eclectic blend; Marx dismissed him as a “vulgar” economist who defended capitalism rather than analyzing it.

8. John Stuart Mill

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), raised by his father James Mill in Bentham’s circle and famously educated in Greek at three, wrote the most influential economics textbook of the nineteenth century, Principles of Political Economy (1848). Mill kept much of Ricardo’s framework but drew more reformist conclusions:

  • Production versus distribution. The laws of production, Mill argued, are like laws of nature; but the distribution of wealth “is a matter of human institution solely,” and society can change it.
  • Property and its origins. He wrote that the social arrangements of modern Europe began from a distribution of property that was “the result, not of just partition, or acquisition by industry, but of conquest and violence.”
  • Socialism. Mill took socialist ideas seriously. If the choice were between communism with all its chances and the existing society with all its sufferings and injustices, he wrote, the difficulties of communism would be “as dust in the balance.” But he argued that private property had never been given a fair trial, and he favored reforming it, with cooperatives of workers, heavy taxes on inheritance, and public control or taxation of natural monopolies.
  • The stationary state. Unlike Ricardo, Mill welcomed an end to the growth of capital and population, so long as it allowed more leisure and culture: “a stationary condition of capital and population implies no stationary state of human improvement.”
  • Changing his mind. In 1869 Mill publicly abandoned the wage-fund doctrine, and in the same year published The Subjection of Women, influenced by Harriet Taylor Mill, arguing for women’s legal and political equality.
Discussion: Say and Senior said that profit rewards a contribution (capital) or a sacrifice (abstinence). The Ricardian socialists said it is a deduction from what labor produced. What evidence, if any, could settle this disagreement?
