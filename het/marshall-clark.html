Marshall, Clark, and the Neoclassical Theory of Distribution

How the marginal revolution became a complete system: supply and demand in every market, and a theory in which each factor of production is paid what it contributes.

1. The Gilded Age and the new profession

By the 1880s, industry in the United States, Britain, and Germany was dominated by large firms: railways, steel, oil, chemicals. Giant trusts formed to fix prices, and the U.S. Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890. Labor conflict was fierce. In 1886, a bomb at a rally for the eight-hour day in Chicago’s Haymarket Square led to the execution of anarchist leaders after a trial widely seen as unjust. Socialist parties grew across Europe.

Economics, meanwhile, was becoming an academic profession. The American Economic Association was founded in 1885, and Alfred Marshall persuaded Cambridge to create a separate degree in economics in 1903. The new professionals built on the marginalism of the 1870s (Topic 8) and extended it from the theory of consumer demand to the theory of production and distribution, which is where the politics lay: if wages, interest, and rent are each the reward of a productive contribution, the socialist charge of exploitation has an answer.

2. Marshall’s scissors

Alfred Marshall (1842–1924) dominated British economics for a generation. His Principles of Economics (1890), revised through eight editions, helped rename the field “economics” and taught generations of students its toolkit: supply and demand curves, elasticity, consumer surplus, the representative firm, and external economies. Marshall kept mathematics in footnotes and appendices; he believed economic reasoning should be translated into plain English and illustrated with real examples.

On the old dispute between labor (cost of production) and utility theories of value, Marshall answered that both matter:

We might as reasonably dispute whether it is the upper or the under blade of a pair of scissors that cuts a piece of paper, as whether value is governed by utility or cost of production.Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890), Book V, ch. 3

His great innovation was time. Which blade matters more depends on the period considered. In the very short market period, supply is fixed and demand decides the price. In the short run, firms can produce more with existing plants, at rising cost. In the long run, firms can enter and build new capacity, and price returns to the cost of production. Marshall analyzed one market at a time, holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus), an approach called partial equilibrium, in contrast to Walras’s general equilibrium.

3. Clark and marginal productivity

The American John Bates Clark (1847–1938) extended marginal analysis from consumption to production. In The Distribution of Wealth (1899), he argued that competitive markets pay each factor of production the value of its marginal product: the extra output from employing one more unit of it. Employers hire workers until the last worker adds just enough output to cover the wage; they borrow capital until the last unit of capital earns just the interest rate. Clark drew an ethical conclusion:

It is the purpose of this work to show that the distribution of the income of society is controlled by a natural law, and that this law, if it worked without friction, would give to every agent of production the amount of wealth which that agent creates.John Bates Clark, The Distribution of Wealth (1899), preface

If everyone gets what they create, there is no exploitation. A puzzle followed: if every factor is paid its marginal product, do the payments add up to exactly the total product? Philip Wicksteed (1894) showed that they do when the production function has constant returns to scale, a result that follows from Euler’s theorem.

4. Böhm-Bawerk on capital and interest

The Austrian Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk (1851–1914), several times Austria’s finance minister, gave the theory of interest its classic marginalist form in Capital and Interest (1884–89). Production is roundabout: it is more productive to first make tools, nets, and machines, and then use them, but roundabout methods take time. Interest exists because present goods are worth more than future goods, for three reasons: people expect to be better supplied in the future; they systematically underestimate future wants; and present goods can be invested in more productive, roundabout methods. In Karl Marx and the Close of His System (1896), he argued that Volume 3 of Capital contradicted Volume 1, opening the long debate over the transformation problem (Topic 7).

5. Pareto and Pigou: efficiency and externalities

  • Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923), Walras’s successor at Lausanne, rebuilt demand theory on rankings of preferences rather than measurable utility. He defined an allocation as efficient (Pareto optimal) when no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. The criterion avoids comparing people’s utilities, but it is silent on distribution: an allocation in which one person owns everything can be Pareto optimal. Pareto also found that the upper tail of income distributions followed a regular pattern, the “Pareto law.”
  • A. C. Pigou (1877–1959), Marshall’s successor at Cambridge, wrote The Economics of Welfare (1920). He showed that markets can fail when private and social costs diverge, as with smoke from a factory chimney, and proposed taxing such activities, a Pigouvian tax. He also argued, from diminishing marginal utility, that transferring income from rich to poor would, other things equal, raise total welfare.

Together, these ideas form the core of welfare economics: competitive markets are efficient under ideal conditions, and departures from those conditions justify specific interventions. Coase (Topics 14 and 18) would later challenge Pigou’s remedy.

6. Critics

  • Veblen (Topic 10) mocked Clark’s attempt to find marginal productivity everywhere, imagining it applied to “a gang of Aleutian Islanders slushing about in the wrack and surf with rakes and magical incantations for the capture of shell-fish” (1909).
  • Monopoly. The theory assumes perfect competition, but the economy of the trusts was anything but competitive. In 1933, Joan Robinson and Edward Chamberlin independently developed theories of imperfect and monopolistic competition in which factors are paid less than the value of their marginal product.
  • Capital. The theory needs a quantity of capital whose marginal product sets the interest rate. The Cambridge capital controversy of the 1960s showed that capital cannot in general be measured independently of the interest rate (Topic 13).
  • Distribution and justice. Even if each factor is paid its marginal product, the distribution of income depends on who owns the factors. Hunt argues that marginal productivity theory naturalizes the existing distribution of property and treats ownership as if it were a contribution.
Discussion: Clark claimed that, without friction, everyone receives what they create. What would you need to observe to test that claim, and how does it compare with Marx’s claim (Topic 6) that profit is unpaid labor?
