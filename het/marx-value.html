Marx: History, Value, and Surplus Value

How Marx turned the classical labor theory of value into a theory of exploitation, and why he thought capitalism was one stage in a longer history.

In this module
  1. Marx, Engels, and the factory age
  2. Historical materialism
  3. The commodity and its value
  4. The puzzle of profit
  5. The working day (interactive)

1. Marx, Engels, and the factory age

Karl Marx (1818–1883) trained in law and philosophy in Germany and became a radical journalist. In Paris in 1844 he met Friedrich Engels, the son of a textile manufacturer, whose The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845) described Manchester’s mills and slums: fourteen-hour days, child labor, cholera, and cellar dwellings. Together they wrote The Communist Manifesto (1848) on the eve of revolutions across Europe. When those revolutions failed, Marx settled in London, where he lived for the rest of his life, often in poverty and supported by Engels.

Marx spent years in the Reading Room of the British Museum studying Smith, Ricardo, and the British government’s own factory inspectors’ reports. The first volume of Capital, subtitled “A Critique of Political Economy,” appeared in 1867. Marx treated the classical economists with respect, especially Ricardo, but argued that they had mistaken the laws of one historical system for the laws of nature.

2. Historical materialism

Marx’s theory of history, which he called the “materialist conception of history,” starts from production. To live, people must produce, and they produce in definite social relations. Two concepts carry the argument:

  • Forces of production: the means of production (tools, machines, land) and human labor power with its skills and knowledge.
  • Relations of production: the relations of ownership and control under which production happens, such as slave and master, serf and lord, worker and capitalist.

Together they form the economic structure, the “base,” on which law, politics, and ideas, the “superstructure,” rise. History moves through a succession of modes of production: in Western Europe, from ancient slavery to feudalism to capitalism. When existing relations of production come to hold back the further development of the productive forces, an era of social revolution begins.

It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but, on the contrary, their social being that determines their consciousness.Karl Marx, Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859)

The philosopher G. A. Cohen, in Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defence (1978), reconstructed this view with the tools of analytical philosophy. He argued that the productive forces tend to develop throughout history (the “development thesis”), and that relations of production exist and persist because they promote that development: they are explained functionally, by their effect on the forces. Cohen’s defence started a long debate over whether historical materialism is a testable theory or a philosophy of history.

3. The commodity and its value

Capital opens with the commodity, “the elementary form” of wealth in capitalist societies. A commodity has a use value, its usefulness, which comes from its particular physical qualities; and an exchange value, the quantity of other commodities it trades for. Use values are qualitatively different and cannot be compared; exchange presupposes that commodities have something in common that can be measured. Marx’s answer, following Ricardo, was labor, but labor of a specific kind:

  • Abstract labor: not the particular work of a tailor or a weaver, but human labor in general, the expenditure of human effort that all commodities share.
  • Socially necessary labor time: the time required to produce a commodity “under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity of labour prevalent at the time.” A slow weaver does not create more value; when power looms halved the time needed to weave cloth, the value of hand-woven cloth halved too.

Money is the commodity in which all others express their value. In a capitalist economy, money circulates not to buy useful things (C–M–C) but to make more money: M–C–M′, where M′ is larger than M (Week 1). Where does the increment come from?

4. The puzzle of profit

If commodities exchange at their values, nobody gains in exchange: one person’s gain from buying cheap is another’s loss. So profit cannot come from cheating in the market, as the mercantilists thought. Marx’s solution is the distinction between labor and labor power. What the worker sells is not labor but the capacity to work for a period of time. Like any commodity, labor power has a value: the labor time needed to produce the necessities that maintain the worker and the worker’s family. Marx added that this includes a “historical and moral element”: what counts as necessary depends on the customs and the struggles of a particular society.

The capitalist pays the value of labor power, the wage, and gets to use it for the whole working day. If the worker can produce the value of the wage in part of the day, the rest of the day produces surplus value, which the capitalist keeps. No one has been cheated in the market, yet the worker has worked part of the day without pay. That is Marx’s concept of exploitation: a technical term for the appropriation of unpaid labor, not a claim that individual capitalists are unusually wicked.

5. The working day

Necessary and surplus labor

The two buttons correspond to Marx’s two ways of raising surplus value:

  • Absolute surplus value: lengthen the working day, or intensify work within it. Marx devoted a long chapter to the struggle over the working day, from employers who stretched shifts to fifteen hours to the British Factory Acts, including the Ten Hours Act of 1847, which limited the hours of women and children in textile mills.
  • Relative surplus value: raise productivity in the industries that produce workers’ consumption goods. When food and clothing take less labor to produce, the value of labor power falls, necessary labor shrinks, and surplus labor grows, even with the same working day and the same standard of living for workers. This is the method that dominates once the length of the day is limited by law and by struggle, and it ties the capitalist’s profit to technical change.
Try this: press “Relative” twice. Workers’ real consumption is unchanged; what happens to the rate of surplus value? Now consider: if real wages rise, but more slowly than productivity, can the rate of surplus value still go up?

6. Constant capital, variable capital, surplus value

Marx divided the capital advanced into two parts according to their role in creating value:

Cconstant capital: means of production (machines, materials). Their value is transferred to the product, not increased.
+
Vvariable capital: wages. Buys labor power, which creates new value.
+
Ssurplus value: the new value created beyond V.

value of output = C + V + S    rate of surplus value = S / V    rate of profit = S / (C + V)

Only V is “variable” because only living labor can produce more value than it costs. Machines matter enormously for productivity, but in Marx’s accounting they do not create surplus value. This distinction drives the two arguments of Week 7: the transformation of values into prices of production, and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall as C grows relative to V.

How large is the rate of surplus value today? Anwar Shaikh and Ahmet Tonak, in Measuring the Wealth of Nations (1994), reworked U.S. national accounts into Marxian categories and estimated that the rate of surplus value rose over the period they studied, 1948 to 1989. Such estimates depend on contested choices, for example which workers count as productive, but they show that Marx’s categories can be measured.

7. Primitive accumulation

Where did the first capitalists get their capital, and why were there workers with nothing to sell but their labor power? Classical economists told a story of the thrifty and the idle. Marx, in the last part of Volume 1, called this a fairy tale. He traced the origins of capitalism to primitive accumulation: the enclosure of common lands that drove English peasants off the land (Week 1), laws that punished vagrancy and forced the dispossessed into wage work, the plunder of the Americas, the slave trade, and colonial rule in Asia. Capital, he wrote, came into the world through force, not saving. Historians still debate the relative weight of these forces in the rise of capitalism, a debate that connects to theories of imperialism and the world-system (Week 10).

Discussion: Is “exploitation” in Marx’s sense compatible with workers being paid exactly what the market says their labor power is worth? What would a neoclassical economist (Week 9) say instead about where profit comes from?
