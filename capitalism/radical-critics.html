Contemporary American Capitalism and Its Radical Critics

The silence of the 1950s ended with the civil rights movement, the war in Vietnam, and women’s liberation. Out of these movements came a new generation of critics who asked whether America’s problems were separate failures or parts of one system.

1. Three movements

Civil rights. Brown v. Board of Education (1954), the Montgomery bus boycott, the sit-ins, and voter registration drives broke legal segregation. Many activists concluded that law was not enough. By Hunt’s figures the average Black salary fell from 61 percent of the white in 1950 to 55 percent in 1962.

Vietnam. Hunt retells the history from the French return after 1945 through the Geneva Accords of 1954 and the elections that were never held, to the half-million American soldiers of 1968. The Pentagon Papers showed how far official explanations had departed from what officials knew. Critics asked whether anticommunism alone could explain such a war.

Women’s liberation. The movement drew on women’s experience in civil rights and antiwar work, and on the gap between the ideal of the housewife and the fact that growing numbers of women worked for low pay. Hunt distinguishes liberal feminists (such as the National Organization for Women), separatists, and Marxist feminists. Most of the last group thought that abolishing class was a necessary, though not sufficient, condition of women’s liberation; a minority put the two struggles on an equal footing.

2. Imperialism

Paul Baran’s The Political Economy of Growth (1957) argued that poor countries have an economic surplus large enough for development, but that landlords, merchants, and foreign firms waste it or take it abroad. Harry Magdoff’s The Age of Imperialism (1969) documented the importance of foreign markets, investment, and raw materials to American corporations. Their lives are described in The Sixties Generation.

3. Racism and sexism

Baran and Sweezy (1966) asked who benefits from racial division: employers who can play groups against each other, landlords in crowded districts, households with cheap servants, and white workers protected from competition. Hunt sets beside them the argument, developed by Michael Reich, that racism lowers the wages of white workers as well because it weakens their bargaining power. Radical economists make a similar argument about sex.

Two theories of discrimination

Employers earn a surplus of 100 per worker, to be divided by bargaining. In the bargaining view (Reich), division among workers reduces the power of all of them, and discrimination moves part of the wage from one group to the other. In the taste view (Becker 1957), a discriminating employer gives up profit to satisfy a prejudice.

4. Alienation and the environment

Erich Fromm extended Marx’s alienation from production to consumption and the self: people come to see themselves as commodities to be sold on a personality market, and buying becomes compulsive. Environmental critics argued that capitalism must grow or fall into depression and that firms profit by shifting costs onto others. Hunt adds that pollution control itself became a business for the polluters. He reads the energy crises of 1973 and 1979 as results of oil-company power more than of scarcity, and inflation as the price of holding off depression by debt: it moves income from workers and pensioners to capital and leaves a chain of credit that could snap.

5. Liberal and radical critics

Liberals and radicals often describe the same evils. Hunt locates their difference in two places. Liberals treat the problems as separate and the state as neutral; radicals treat them as connected results of private ownership and profit, and doubt that a government financed by the wealthy will act against them. His evidence is the rising cost of campaigns and the growth of political action committees, from 608 in 1974 to about 4,500 in 2000.

The new left, with Students for a Democratic Society at its center, distrusted party dogma and fell apart in 1969. In the 1970s many of its members turned to studying Marx, worked through institutions such as the Union for Radical Political Economics (founded in 1968), or joined socialist groups that merged in 1982 into the Democratic Socialists of America. Hunt ends by arguing that socialism, freed from its association with Soviet-type governments, has better long-run prospects than before.

6. Beyond Hunt

  • Discrimination. Arrow and Becker expected competition to erode it. William Darity, Patrick Mason, and the stratification economists argue that groups have material interests in preserving it. Reich’s evidence that white workers lose remains debated.
  • The women’s wage gap. Hunt reports about 73 percent for full-time workers in 2000. In the Census series for full-time, year-round workers it was 80.9 percent in 2024, down from 82.7 in 2023. Claudia Goldin links what remains largely to parenthood and to jobs that reward long hours.
  • The oil shocks. Most accounts give more weight than Hunt does to OPEC and to rising demand, along with corporate power.
  • Money and politics. Gilens and Page (2014) find that policy follows the preferences of affluent Americans and organized business. Other scholars reply that the groups usually agree.
  • Intersectionality. Black feminists, from the Combahee River Collective (1977) to Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989), went beyond the question of which oppression is primary.
  • Pollution. Hunt thought there was little doubt that pollution was much worse in the 1990s than in 1970. For the six common air pollutants the opposite is true: by the EPA’s estimate, their combined emissions in the United States fell 79 percent from 1970 to 2024. Carbon dioxide is a different matter.

Slips to watch for in the book

Hunt describes OPEC as a cartel formed by Arab countries. It was founded in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela.

Question to think about: Hunt says liberals see separate problems where radicals see one system. Choose a current problem and write one paragraph from each point of view.

7. Readings
