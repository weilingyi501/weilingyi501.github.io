The Transition to Early Capitalism

Capitalism is so familiar that it can seem to have no beginning. This topic defines it, follows the long breakup of the manorial economy from the eleventh century to the seventeenth, and introduces one of the great debates in economic history: what caused the transition from feudalism to capitalism?

1. What is capitalism?

Hunt defines capitalism by three features found together, so that eighteenth-century England, early twentieth-century Japan, and the United States today, for all their differences, share one economic system:

  1. Market exchange is everywhere. Most people get what they need by buying it with money. They depend on a vast number of strangers, but the dependence appears to them only as prices.
  2. Classes are divided by ownership. A small class owns the means of production. A large class owns little but its ability to work and must sell it for a wage. Between them stands a middle class of small owners and independent professionals, and beneath them those who have neither property nor work.
  3. Profit decides what is produced, how, where, and for whom.

Each feature existed somewhere before capitalism. Markets, hired labor, and profit seeking are ancient. What is new is a society organized by all three.

2. Fields, towns, and trade

Hunt starts the story in the fields. From about the eighth century, and widely by the eleventh and twelfth, the three-field system began to replace the two-field system in northern Europe. Instead of leaving half the land fallow each year, farmers left one third. With the same arable land, the area sown rose from one half to two thirds, an increase of a third. (Hunt, citing Lynn White, gives a gain of up to 50 percent. White’s own argument is that output could rise by a third or by a half, depending on how the fallow was plowed and whether wasteland could be brought under cultivation.) Oats fed horses, which plowed and hauled faster than oxen. More food supported more people, and more of them lived in towns.

Towns brought trade. Hunt insists that trade was not an outside force that happened to strike Europe. Farming had first produced the surplus and the towns that made it possible. Long-distance commerce, quickened by the Crusades and by contact with the Arab world, fed the great fairs of Champagne and then permanent commercial cities. Merchants developed bills of exchange, bookkeeping, and commercial law. To secure supplies for distant markets, they began to advance materials and then tools to craftsmen. In this putting-out system, the worker at the end of the process no longer sold cloth to the merchant. He or she sold labor power. Capitalist textile industries in Flanders and Florence were torn by class conflict in the towns; England’s industry, Hunt argues, avoided it by scattering its workers through the countryside.

3. The crisis of the manor

Markets entered the manor as well. Lords wanted cash, and peasants who could sell small surpluses paid money in place of labor services (commutation). Then came catastrophe. The Black Death of 1348–49 and its recurrences cut England’s population from perhaps 4 million to 2.5 million by the early fifteenth century, on Hunt’s figures. Labor became scarce and wages rose. Lords and parliaments tried to force the old terms back, as in the English Statute of Labourers (1351), and peasants fought back: the Jacquerie in France (1358), the English rising of 1381, and the German Peasants’ War of 1524–25, in which as many as 100,000 may have been killed.

4. The making of a working class

After 1500 population grew again, and several forces separated people from the land. English lords enclosed common fields and turned them to sheep. Rents rose, and debts ruined smallholders. Guilds closed their ranks. In some areas, Hunt writes, three-fourths to nine-tenths of tenants were forced off the land. Those who lost their livelihood faced harsh laws against vagrancy. Marx called this process primitive accumulation (Topic 7), and Hunt follows him in stressing that a class of people with nothing to sell but their labor was made, often by force. He names four main sources of the first accumulations of capital: trade, putting-out, enclosure, and the price inflation. To these he adds colonial plunder, piracy, and the slave trade.

Silver from the Americas added the price revolution. Prices rose by 150 to 400 percent in the sixteenth century, depending on the region. Rents and wages lagged behind, so the gains went to those who sold goods and paid wages and fixed rents.

The price revolution

A farmer or manufacturer sells output worth 100 and begins by paying 45 in wages and 30 in rent, leaving 25 as profit. All prices then rise, but wages and rents rise more slowly. Output and employment stay the same.

5. The first mercantilism

The new monarchies that unified England, France, and Spain drew on merchants for money and support. Their early economic policy, bullionism, tried to keep gold and silver inside the country. Later mercantilists aimed instead at a favorable balance of trade, with trading monopolies, navigation laws, and regulation of industry. Hunt’s point is that this was not simply a policy of merchants. It carried the paternalist idea that authority is responsible for the general welfare into a new age, and that created the conflict taken up in Topic 3. (For the trade theory, see Mercantilism and the Physiocrats.)

6. Beyond Hunt: the transition debate

PositionArgument
Trade dissolved feudalismPaul Sweezy (1950), drawing on Henri Pirenne: feudalism was conservative, and long-distance trade broke it from outside.
Feudalism broke from withinMaurice Dobb (1946): lords squeezed peasants too hard, and peasants fled or resisted. In eastern Europe, trade strengthened serfdom instead.
PopulationM. M. Postan and Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie: long waves of population growth and decline drove rents, wages, and tenure.
Class structureRobert Brenner (1976): the same plague had opposite effects in England and eastern Europe. What mattered was the balance of power between lords and peasants. In England it produced capitalist tenants who had to compete to keep their leases.
World economyImmanuel Wallerstein (1974): capitalism began as a European world-economy in the long sixteenth century, with coerced labor in its periphery.

Hunt calls improvements in farming the original force behind the whole chain of events, and he rejects accounts that begin with trade. On property and class he relies on Dobb.

On enclosure, J. R. Wordie (1983) estimated that about 45 percent of England was already enclosed by 1500, only about 2 percent was enclosed in the sixteenth century, and about 24 percent in the seventeenth, with the parliamentary enclosures coming later. His figures are debated, but they cast doubt on a sixteenth-century peak, where Hunt places it.

Slips to watch for in the book

Hunt dates Dutch independence to 1690; Spain recognized it in 1648. He lists the telescope among the instruments of the Age of Exploration, but it appeared only in 1608.

Question to think about: The Black Death made labor scarce across Europe. Why did serfdom fade in the West and harden in the East?
