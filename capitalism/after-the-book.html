After the Book: Property and Prophets since 2003

Hunt’s last edition appeared in 2003. This topic is not in the book. It uses his method, setting institutions beside the ideas that defend and attack them, to study what has happened since.

1. Using Hunt’s method

The book’s method can be put as four questions to ask of any period:

  1. How is production organized, and who owns the means of production?
  2. Who receives the surplus?
  3. Which ideas present this as natural or just, and where did they come from?
  4. Who criticizes it, and in the name of which values?

In his foreword Robert Pollin points out that when he first read the book in 1975, the collapse of Soviet socialism and the retreat of Keynesian social democracy still lay ahead. He describes the ideology that took their place, neoliberalism, as a dressed-up classical liberalism.

2. Neoliberalism

From the late 1970s governments freed capital movements, privatized public firms, weakened unions, and lowered top tax rates. Scholars describe it in different ways. David Harvey (2005) calls it a project to restore class power. Quinn Slobodian (2018) shows that its founders wanted to build strong international rules that would protect markets from democracy, which is far from the minimal state. Defenders stress falling inflation, growth in Asia, and the decline of extreme poverty in the world, much of it in China, whose policies fit the label poorly.

3. Finance and 2008

The subprime crisis of 2007–08 revived the questions of Topic 12. Hyman Minsky’s theory, in which stability leads borrowers and lenders to take more risk, explained it better than models that left finance out. Governments rescued banks and for a time returned to Keynes. Occupy Wall Street put the “1 percent” into political language in 2011, and Thomas Piketty’s Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013) and Capital and Ideology (2019) brought the history of property and its justifications to a wide audience. The second book asks Hunt’s question across the world.

4. Platforms and concentration

The largest firms are now technology platforms. Thomas Philippon (2019) and De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger (2020) find rising concentration and markups in the United States. Others reply that superstar firms are simply more productive (Autor and coauthors 2020). Shoshana Zuboff (2019) writes of “surveillance capitalism,” and a new antitrust movement cites the trustbusters of Topic 9. Gig work raises again the question of putting-out in Topic 2: who is an employer?

5. Climate

Climate change is the largest external cost in history. William Nordhaus treats it as a problem of price, with a carbon tax as remedy. Nicholas Stern has called it the greatest market failure the world has seen. Andreas Malm (2016) traces it to the factory owners’ choice of steam as a way to control labor, and degrowth writers such as Jason Hickel question growth itself. (See the HET modules on environmental and ecological economics.)

6. The return of the state

Tariffs, subsidies for chips and clean energy, pandemic spending, and China’s state-led growth have ended the confidence that markets alone should guide investment. Some see a return to mercantilism, some a new developmental state. Nationalist movements attack global markets while defending property at home, and democratic socialists have returned to electoral politics for the first time in decades. The dispute between Bernstein and Luxemburg is open again.

7. Assessing the book

Three criticisms are worth discussing at the end of the course:

  • Balance. Hunt writes as a critic of capitalism and says so. His opponents would say he presents defenses in order to refute them. Does he state the best case for markets?
  • Scope. The story runs from Europe to the United States. Slavery in the Americas, colonial subjects, and the thinkers of the South appear mostly as objects. Cedric Robinson’s Black Marxism (1983) and the literature on racial capitalism would begin it elsewhere.
  • Function and truth. To show that an idea serves a class is not to show that it is false. When is the origin of an idea relevant to judging it?
Question to think about: Write chapter 15 of Property and Prophets. Which institutions would you choose, and who are today’s prophets?
