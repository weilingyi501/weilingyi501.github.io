Contemporary American Capitalism and Its Defenders

After 1945 American capitalism delivered growth, and with it a new confidence. Hunt sets out the postwar structure of corporate power and inequality and then goes through the ideas that justified it. Nearly all of them, he finds, are older ideas in modern dress.

1. Power and inequality

In 1962 there were about 420,000 manufacturing firms in the United States, and the assets of the twenty largest roughly matched those of the smallest 419,000. Merger waves in the 1960s and 1980s carried concentration further. Wealth was still more concentrated than income. Taxes did little to change the picture. The income tax is progressive on paper, but Hunt argues that taxes as a whole increase inequality. He cites Musgrave’s study of 1964, in which the poorest families paid a third of their income in taxes, more than most families above them. He adds figures for the 1980s: the federal rate on the top 1 percent fell from 50.1 to 28.8 percent while the rate on the poorest tenth rose from 11.5 to 14.6.

How progressive is the system?

The chart shows the share of income paid by households at different incomes under a stylized system. The income tax is a flat rate above an exemption. The payroll tax stops at a cap. The sales tax falls on consumption, and the share of income consumed falls as income rises.

2. Free-market liberalism, popular and academic

Neoclassical theory became highly mathematical, which made it stronger as theory and weaker as persuasion. A popular version grew up beside it. Business associations and foundations such as the Foundation for Economic Education spread a simple doctrine: free markets always do better than government, and the real dangers are unions and the welfare state. The “supply-side” economics of the Reagan years was, for Hunt, only a new name for the old case for a small state.

3. Countervailing power and people’s capitalism

John Kenneth Galbraith’s American Capitalism (1952) accepted that power was concentrated and argued that one power calls forth another. Strong unions and big retailers balance strong manufacturers. (Galbraith later moved well beyond this position.) Massimo Salvadori and others spoke of people’s capitalism: so many people own shares that everyone is becoming a capitalist. Hunt replies that shareholding is wide but very shallow.

4. The managerial ethic

Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means (1932) showed that in the largest corporations ownership had separated from control. By the 1950s this had become a defense. Managers, no longer driven by profit, balanced the interests of shareholders, workers, customers, and the public. Carl Kaysen wrote in 1957 of the “soulful corporation.” Executives said much the same in public lectures, and the Committee for Economic Development and the U.S. Information Agency spread the message. Unlike the free marketeers, the CED accepted big government, Keynesian policy, and cooperative unions. The reader will recognize the Christian paternalist ethic once more, with the manager in the place of the lord.

5. Anticommunism

Hunt treats anticommunism as an ideology of capitalism because of how it worked. It used one word for two things: the Soviet system, and any movement that sided with labor, wanted equality, or criticized corporations. By moving from one meaning to the other, it made domestic criticism look like treason. He follows its use from the Haymarket trial of 1886, when the word was “anarchist,” through the Red Scare after the First World War to McCarthyism, which ruined tens of thousands of careers, and abroad to American support for the overthrow of governments such as those of Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954), and Chile (1973).

6. Criticisms of the defenses

Hunt ends by reporting criticisms. Oskar Lange (1938) and Fred Taylor showed that the efficiency claimed for competitive markets could in theory belong to a socialist economy as well. J. de V. Graaff’s Theoretical Welfare Economics listed the demanding conditions needed for the welfare theorems. Against the managerial view, studies found that managers pursue profit much as owners do, and the record of corporate crime, from Edwin Sutherland’s research to the savings-and-loan scandals, did not suggest a soul. Of anticommunism, critics said that it called every critic a communist, gave a selective picture of communist countries, ignored the democratic ideal of socialism, and excused as bad or worse abuses by friendly dictatorships.

7. Beyond Hunt

  • Shareholder value. Within a few years of Kaysen, Milton Friedman (1970) declared that business’s only social responsibility is profit, and agency theory (Jensen and Meckling 1976) treated managers’ independence as a problem. The managerial ethic revived as “stakeholder capitalism.”
  • Measuring inequality. Piketty, Saez, and Zucman (2018) find that the top 1 percent’s share fell in mid-century and rose steeply from about 1980. From 1960 to 2019 its share of income after taxes and transfers rose by 6 points in their data, but by only 1 point in Auten and Splinter’s. Hunt’s claim that the postwar boom reduced inequality little is disputed, since most series show a marked compression by mid-century.
  • Communism. Hunt writes that millions of peasants died in collectivization, that conservatives greatly exaggerated the number, and that several thousand of Stalin’s rivals were imprisoned or killed. Archives opened after 1991 did lower the highest Cold War estimates. They also show terror far beyond what he describes: 681,692 recorded executions in 1937–38 alone, some 5.5 to 7 million or more deaths in the famines of the early 1930s, and about 1.7 million recorded deaths in camps, colonies, and prisons. The point about two meanings of a word can be judged separately from his account of Soviet history.
  • Planning. The socialist calculation debate (Mises, Hayek, Lange) is treated more fully in the HET module on Chicago and Austrian economics.

Slips to watch for in the book

The Haymarket bomb exploded on May 4, 1886, and not on May 1. Eight men were convicted and seven sentenced to death; four were hanged, one killed himself, two had their sentences commuted, and three were pardoned in 1893. Galbraith’s American Capitalism first appeared in 1952 and Graaff’s Theoretical Welfare Economics in 1957; Hunt gives the dates of later editions.

Question to think about: Compare the soulful corporation of the 1950s with today’s corporate social responsibility. Are they the same ideology?
