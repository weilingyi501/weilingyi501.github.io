Imperialism and Revolutionary Socialism

Between 1875 and 1914 a handful of powers took control of most of the world. Imperialism split the socialist movement more deeply than any question of theory. This topic covers the record and the three most influential explanations of it.

1. The record

India. Hunt begins with the East India Company’s conquest of Bengal in 1757. He cites estimates that Britain took between £500 million and £1,000 million out of India by 1815. Officials made fortunes quickly, and over the longer run British policy discouraged Indian manufactures and encouraged raw-material exports. Railways built after the 1850s, with a 5 percent return guaranteed to British investors by Indian taxpayers, opened the interior.

Africa. In 1800 Europeans held little beyond the coast. By the First World War they controlled about 93 percent of the continent, by Hunt’s count. He singles out Leopold II’s Congo, where rubber was collected by terror, and the British South Africa Company of Cecil Rhodes, whose expansion he blames for the Boer War (1899–1902).

Asia and the Pacific. France took Indochina, the Dutch completed their control of the East Indies, and Britain took Burma, Malaya, and much else. China was forced open by the Opium Wars and divided into spheres of influence.

The United States conquered a continent from its native peoples, then took Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, where a long war for independence was suppressed. It secured the Panama Canal Zone in 1903, and sent marines to Nicaragua, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic.

2. Hobson

J. A. Hobson’s Imperialism (1902), written after he reported on the Boer War, located the “taproot” of imperialism in underconsumption. Income was so unequally divided that the rich saved more than could be invested profitably at home. The surplus sought outlets abroad, and investors called on the state to protect them. Empire did not pay the nation as a whole; it paid a small group. Hobson’s remedy was reform. If income were distributed more equally, home demand would absorb the product and the motive for empire would fade.

Hobson’s taproot

National income is 100. The top tenth saves a large part of its share, everyone else very little. Home investment depends on the growth of consumption. Saving that finds no outlet at home must go abroad or cause a slump.

3. Imperialism divides the socialists

The Fabians split over the Boer War. Shaw’s faction argued that advanced powers were entitled to manage peoples who could not manage themselves. In a vote of the membership 45 percent wished to condemn imperialism and 55 percent to approve or ignore it, and eighteen members resigned. Many German revisionists approved of colonies or took no position; Hunt quotes Bernstein on the higher right of the higher civilization. To revolutionary socialists, this showed where evolutionary socialism led.

4. Luxemburg

In The Accumulation of Capital (1913), Rosa Luxemburg asked where the demand comes from that allows capitalists to realize surplus value they intend to accumulate. Workers spend their wages, and capitalists consume a part. She found the missing buyers in noncapitalist societies. Capitalism needs them and destroys them, so it uses up the conditions of its own growth. She wrote with power about how natural economies were broken, and about military spending, which both protects empires and supplies a steady, state-guaranteed demand.

Hunt praises her insight into military spending, decades ahead of its time, but finds the theory faulty: to sell abroad a society must also buy, so noncapitalist trade does not by itself add net demand. Her mistake, he thinks, was to focus on underconsumption when the real force was the search for investment outlets, and he credits Lenin with the correction.

5. Lenin

Lenin’s Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism, written in 1916, drew on Hobson and Hilferding. He listed five features: monopoly; the merger of bank and industrial capital into finance capital; export of capital; division of the world among cartels; and division of territory among powers. Because capitalist countries grow at unequal rates, any division becomes outdated and leads to war. Hunt stresses Lenin’s difference from Hobson: the driving force is the export of capital, not of goods. Lenin added a political argument. Extra profits from the colonies raise wages at home and blunt the workers’ revolutionary will.

6. Beyond Hunt

  • Where did capital go? A large part of British capital before 1914 went to the United States, the settler dominions, and Latin America; the new African colonies received little. Critics such as D. K. Fieldhouse use this against Hobson and Lenin. Defenders reply that Lenin’s theory concerned rivalry over the whole world, formal colonies being one part.
  • Did empire pay? Davis and Huttenback (1986) find that taxpayers lost while a small group of investors gained, close to Hobson’s view.
  • Other explanations. Schumpeter (1919) blamed precapitalist elites, and Gallagher and Robinson (1953) the “imperialism of free trade,” informal where possible, formal where needed. Cain and Hopkins stress the “gentlemanly capitalism” of the City of London.
  • Colonial voices. Dadabhai Naoroji’s theory of the drain (1901) came before Hobson’s book. W. E. B. Du Bois’s “The African Roots of War” (1915) preceded Lenin’s.

Slips to watch for in the book

Hunt says the British South Africa Company came to control South Africa completely. Its territory was to the north, in what became Rhodesia (Zimbabwe and Zambia). He says Afghanistan was put under the Indian government; after the war of 1878–80 Britain controlled its foreign affairs. His remark on the suppression of Black South Africans “today” was written before the end of apartheid in 1994.

See also the HET module on imperialism and the trade module on theories of imperialism.

Question to think about: Hobson thought redistribution at home would end imperialism; Lenin thought it impossible under capitalism. What evidence from the twentieth century bears on this dispute?
