The Conflict in Mercantilist Thought

Between about 1500 and 1776, English economic thought was pulled in two directions. The state took over the Church’s duty to care for the poor and regulate economic life. At the same time merchants, philosophers, and Protestant preachers were building a new view of human beings, in which self-interest is natural and even virtuous.

1. Medieval roots of mercantilist policy

Hunt looks for the origins of mercantilist policy in the Middle Ages. English kings from Edward I to Henry VII promoted the wool trade, limited foreign merchants, and tried to balance the interests of lords, merchants, and laborers. Edward III fixed wages and prices during the French war, which Hunt reads as a bargain that protected laborers in return for an obligation to work. (Other historians read the labor laws after the Black Death as an attempt to hold wages down.) Richard II faced the revolt of 1381 and passed a Navigation Act in the same year. Policy in the mercantilist age grew out of this older concern with order and welfare as well as out of the interests of merchants.

2. The state takes over from the Church

When Henry VIII broke with Rome and dissolved the monasteries, an organized system of charity went with them, just as enclosure and slumps in the cloth trade were creating large numbers of the unemployed. The state stepped in:

LawWhat it did
Act of 1531Separated the “deserving” poor, who were allowed to beg, from the “undeserving.”
Act of 1536Made each parish responsible for its poor, through a fund of voluntary contributions.
Statute of Artificers (1563)Regulated apprenticeship, hiring, and wages, with maximum rates set by magistrates.
Poor rate (1572)Accepted that the poor must be supported from compulsory taxes.
Poor Law (1601)Recognized a right to relief, administered and paid for by each parish, with help at home for the old and sick, work for the unemployed, and punishment for vagrants.
Statute of Monopolies (1624)Outlawed monopolies except for inventions and those that would help the balance of payments; abuses continued.

Hunt reads these laws as the Christian paternalist ethic in secular form. He also notes the other side. The Crown wished to protect laborers and to keep them in their place; the maximum wages of the Statute of Artificers protected employers, whose own class enforced them, and probably fell in real terms as prices rose. He argues that concern for employment ties most mercantilist proposals together, from export promotion to patents of monopoly.

3. The rise of individualism

Merchants and manufacturers often found the web of regulation a burden on their own trade. A new philosophy gave their complaint a general form. Thomas Hobbes’s Leviathan (1651) portrayed people as driven by appetite and aversion, always seeking their own advantage. Hobbes drew the conclusion that they need an absolute sovereign. Others accepted the psychology and drew the opposite conclusion: if self-interest is universal, policy should let it work.

By the later seventeenth century, Dudley North was arguing that trade flourishes best without special privileges, and that laws conferring them serve a few at the expense of all. In 1714 Bernard Mandeville published The Fable of the Bees: or, Private Vices, Publick Benefits. Greed, vanity, and luxury, the old sins, turn out to keep the hive busy and prosperous. The moral world of Topic 1 has been turned upside down.

4. Protestantism

The first reformers were conservative in economic matters. Luther defended the ban on usury and called on the princes to crush the peasants in 1525. Still, the Protestant idea that each believer stands before God alone and is justified by faith weakened the Church’s power to judge economic conduct. Motives counted more than acts, and each conscience could judge its own. Calvinist preachers praised diligence in one’s calling and condemned waste. A person who worked hard and did not spend would accumulate, and could do so with a clear conscience.

5. Beyond Hunt: religion and capitalism

  • Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–05): anxiety about salvation led Calvinists to methodical work in a calling, an “elective affinity” with capitalism.
  • R. H. Tawney, Religion and the Rise of Capitalism (1926): the influence ran both ways, and Puritanism adapted to the commercial classes. Hunt refers readers to both, and his own account rests mainly on Christopher Hill.
  • Evidence. Becker and Woessmann (2009) find that Protestant counties in nineteenth-century Prussia were richer mainly because they were more literate. Cantoni (2015) finds no effect of Protestantism on the growth of 272 cities of the Holy Roman Empire between 1300 and 1900. The settings differ (counties and cities), so the two results do not directly contradict each other.
  • Albert Hirschman, The Passions and the Interests (1977): early arguments for commerce claimed that the calm pursuit of interest would tame more dangerous passions such as glory and lust for power. The case was political before it was economic.
Question to think about: Mandeville said that private vices create public benefits. How is this different from the claim Adam Smith would make sixty years later?
