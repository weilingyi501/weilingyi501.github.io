Socialist Protest Amid the Industrial Revolution

Industrial capitalism was built on hard lives: long hours, child labor, crowded cities, and the loss of old skills and rights. The first socialists answered it with ideas drawn from both older traditions, the liberal claim that people are equal and the Christian claim that we are responsible for one another.

1. The social costs

Industrialization requires a society to give up consumption now in order to build capital. Hunt asks who made the sacrifice. He argues that real wages fell in the second half of the eighteenth century, that any gain in the first decades of the nineteenth was slight at best, and that relative to other classes the poor without doubt fell far behind. Beyond income, he describes the loss of a way of life: the clock and the factory bell replaced the varied rhythm of earlier work, skills were broken into simple tasks, and the worker became an attendant of the machine. Pauper children were indentured to mill owners for years. Cities grew faster than water, sewers, or housing, and cholera and typhoid followed.

The law offered little help. The Combination Acts of 1799–1800 outlawed unions. The Speenhamland system, which topped up wages from the parish rates, had real faults, Hunt concedes: it pulled wages down and tied workers to their parishes. What matters to him is what replaced it in 1834, the workhouse, where relief was meant to be less attractive than the worst-paid work.

Engels’ pause

Robert Allen (2009) estimates that between 1780 and 1840 output per worker in Britain rose about 46 percent while the real wage rose about 12 percent. If hours and employment are held constant, labor’s share must fall. Choose the starting share, or change the wage growth to see what keeping pace would have required.

2. Two kinds of early socialism

Hunt sorts early socialists by their relation to classical liberalism.

Thomas Hodgskin (1787–1869) stayed inside it. He kept competition and the market but rejected the capitalist. What is called capital, he argued in Labour Defended (1825), is the present and past labor of other workers. Owners receive profit because they have power over labor, not because capital produces anything. Remove that power, and free exchange among working producers would be just. Hunt sees him as an ancestor of market socialism.

William Thompson (1775–1833), who also wrote with Anna Wheeler on the rights of women, went further. In his Inquiry (1824) he listed five evils that competition would cause even among equal workers: it makes every worker a rival of every other, oppresses women by confining unpaid domestic work to them, produces crises through the anarchy of the market, leaves the sick and old insecure, and turns knowledge into a trade secret. His alternative was cooperative communities in which property, work, and child-rearing were shared.

3. Robert Owen

Owen was a successful manufacturer whose mill at New Lanark became famous for shorter hours, schooling, and decent housing. He insisted that character is formed by circumstances, so a society that degrades its workers should not blame them. In practice he was a paternalist: his “silent monitor,” a colored block hung by each worker, displayed the previous day’s conduct to everyone. In his plans for an ideal society, private property and profit would disappear, and self-governing cooperative villages would replace them.

Hunt uses Owen to define socialism as an intellectual tradition. It joined the liberal idea that all people are equal to the older idea that each is the keeper of the other, and it removed from the second the father at the head of the table.

5. Beyond Hunt: the standard of living

  • Optimists and pessimists. The debate began with Engels (1845) and Macaulay, and continued in the twentieth century with T. S. Ashton and Max Hartwell (optimists) against E. P. Thompson and Hobsbawm (pessimists).
  • Wages. Charles Feinstein (1998) found that real wages rose very little before the 1840s. Gregory Clark found faster growth. Allen calls the gap between output and wages “Engels’ pause.” Nicholas Crafts (2020), with newer data and a different price index, finds a smaller gap: output per worker up about 38 percent and real earnings up about 18 percent.
  • Bodies. Average heights and urban life expectancy stagnated or fell in the second quarter of the nineteenth century, evidence that income alone misses.
  • Speenhamland. Karl Polanyi (1944) made it central to his account of market society. Later historians, including Mark Blaug (1963) and Fred Block and Margaret Somers (2003), argue that the Royal Commission of 1834 greatly exaggerated its harms. Hobsbawm disagreed with the revision.

Slips to watch for in the book

Hunt gives 1652 as the year of Winstanley’s death and February 24, 1797, as the date of Babeuf’s execution. The accepted dates are 1676 and May 27, 1797.

Question to think about: Thompson thought competition would do harm even in a society of equal worker-owners. Which of his five evils seems strongest to you, and could a market be designed to avoid it?
