Corporate Capitalism and Its Ideological Defenses

In the last third of the nineteenth century, giant firms replaced small competitors in one industry after another. Three different ideologies grew up to defend the new order. They contradicted one another, and businessmen used all three.

1. The concentration of power

Railroads created national markets, new technology favored large plants, and the corporation made it possible to raise vast sums. Firms with heavy fixed costs fought price wars until they combined. Hunt compares three countries. In England, with its liberal tradition and free trade, concentration came more slowly but was heavy in railways, banking, thread, and soap. In Germany, liberalism had never taken deep root, and cartels were legal and respected: there were about 16 in 1879, 300 in 1900, and 2,100 in 1930. In the United States, the Civil War, tariffs, land grants, and friendly courts favored big business. By 1904 about 300 large combinations controlled over two-fifths of American manufacturing capital. Income was concentrated as well. In 1929 the top 5 percent received 34 percent of personal disposable income.

Law helped. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868), meant to protect freed slaves, was used by the courts mainly to strike down state regulation of corporations.

Measuring concentration

An industry has equal-sized firms. Some of them merge into one. The chart compares market shares before and after. The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) adds the squared shares; U.S. merger guidelines of 2023 call a market above 1,800 highly concentrated.

2. Neoclassical economics

Just as this was happening, Jevons, Menger, and Walras built a theory of an economy with many small firms, none able to affect prices. Consumers spread their spending until the last dollar on each good adds equal utility, and firms hire until each factor earns its marginal product. Under perfect competition no one can be made better off without harming someone else, given the existing distribution of income. J. B. Clark added that each factor is paid what it creates, which made distribution seem just.

Hunt calls this an impressive intellectual structure separated by a “giant chasm” from the world of trusts. Marshall’s and Pigou’s successors admitted flaws such as monopoly, public goods, external costs, instability, and inequality. Those who thought the flaws serious came to be called liberals, and those who thought them minor conservatives. Both, Hunt says, used the theory to defend capitalism.

3. Appendix: welfare economics

In a long appendix, Hunt presents the modern form of the argument. Each consumer chooses the point where the ratio of marginal utilities equals the price ratio; each firm hires until the ratio of marginal products equals the ratio of factor prices. Because everyone faces the same prices, no reallocation can make one person better off without harming another. This is a Pareto optimum. He then raises objections:

  • The theory needs given equilibrium prices, but cannot show that “groping” would lead to them.
  • It says little about production itself: strikes, effort, and authority disappear into a production function.
  • Preferences are taken as given, though society forms them.
  • There is a Pareto optimum for each distribution of wealth, and the theory cannot choose among them.
  • A rule that recommends only changes that harm no one is silent wherever interests conflict.

A defender would answer that the welfare theorems state exactly what must hold for markets to be efficient, and so show where they fail. Much of modern economics, from externalities to information, was built by relaxing the assumptions one at a time.

4. Social Darwinism

Herbert Spencer (and in America William Graham Sumner, whom Hunt does not discuss) defended laissez-faire by a different route. Competition is a struggle in which the fit survive; to relieve the poor is to preserve the unfit and corrupt the race. Hunt stresses how far this is from neoclassical theory. Social Darwinists accepted giant firms as evolution’s winners, while neoclassical economists, in principle, preferred many small competitors. Darwin disapproved of the analogy. Businessmen who wanted arguments against reform were not troubled by the inconsistency.

5. The new Christian paternalism

A third ideology admitted that the age of small competitors was over. Large firms and great fortunes were acceptable if the men who controlled them acted as trustees for society. Andrew Carnegie’s “Wealth” (1889) asked the millionaire to administer his surplus for the common good. Pope Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum (1891) condemned both the yoke laid on labor and socialism. Gustav Schmoller defended German cartels as schools of public responsibility.

Hunt gives special attention to Simon Patten of the University of Pennsylvania, on whom he had written earlier. Patten believed the economy was passing from scarcity to abundance, that competition was a survival from the age of scarcity, and that cooperation under enlightened business leaders would replace it. His students Rexford Tugwell and Frances Perkins served in Roosevelt’s administration, and Hunt traces the National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933, with its self-government of industry by trade associations, partly to these ideas. The Supreme Court struck it down in 1935.

6. Beyond Hunt

  • Why big business? Alfred Chandler (1977) explained it by economies of speed and scale and the visible hand of management. Naomi Lamoreaux (1985) stresses price wars among firms with high fixed costs, close to Hunt’s account. William Roy (1997) stresses law and power.
  • The Fourteenth Amendment. Hunt repeats the claim that its drafters deliberately meant to protect corporations. Historians since Howard Graham (1938) have mostly rejected this “conspiracy theory,” though Evelyn Atkinson (2025) argues that it had some basis in fact. The courts’ later use of the amendment for corporations is not in doubt.
  • “Social Darwinism.” Richard Hofstadter (1944) popularized the label. Robert Bannister (1979) and Thomas Leonard argue that few people used it of themselves, and that Progressive reformers also drew on evolutionary and eugenic ideas.
  • Neoclassical theory as ideology. Some neoclassicals were reformers: Walras called himself a socialist of a kind and wanted land nationalized, and Pigou’s externalities became a basis for regulation.

Slips to watch for in the book

Hunt names William Makepeace Thackeray, the English novelist, as a biographer of success. He must mean William Makepeace Thayer, the American author of From Log-Cabin to the White House. He also places Tugwell in Roosevelt’s original cabinet; Perkins was in the cabinet, and Tugwell was assistant secretary of agriculture.

See also the HET modules on marginal utility and Marshall and Clark.

Question to think about: Carnegie thought the rich should administer their wealth for the public. How does this differ from the duty of the rich in medieval paternalism? How does it compare with large foundations today?
