Marx’s Social and Economic Theories

Marx’s criticism of capitalism rested on more than poverty. He argued that it cuts people off from what is most human in them, that profit comes from unpaid labor, and that accumulation is driven by competition through recurring crises.

1. Alienation

Many socialists condemned capitalism for making workers poor. Hunt insists that this was not Marx’s main charge. A well-fed slave is still a slave. Human beings develop themselves through work: they change nature, and in doing so they develop their own senses, skills, and relations. Under capitalism the product, the process, and the purpose of work belong to someone else. Workers are estranged from what they make, from the activity of making it, from their own capacities, and from each other. The manuscript of 1844 says that the worker feels at home only when not working. Higher wages can ease the condition but cannot remove it.

2. Surplus value

If commodities exchange at their values, where does profit come from? Marx’s answer turns on one special commodity. The capitalist buys labor power, the capacity to work, at its value: the labor needed to produce the worker’s means of living. In use, labor power creates more value than it costs. In Hunt’s example, if six hours of an eight-hour day produce the value of the worker’s wage goods, the other two are surplus labor, the source of profit, interest, and rent.

value of output = c + v + s    rate of surplus value = s / v    rate of profit = s / (c + v)

3. Primitive accumulation and the drive to accumulate

Political economists explained the first capital as the savings of the frugal. Marx ridiculed this as a fable like original sin, and pointed instead to enclosure, colonial plunder, and slavery (Topic 2). Once capitalism exists, accumulation needs no greed. Competition forces each capitalist to expand and mechanize or be destroyed.

4. Wages, the reserve army, and crisis

Rapid accumulation absorbs unemployed workers, and wages rise. Higher wages cut profits and encourage labor-saving machines, so workers are laid off and the reserve army of labor fills again. Mechanization also creates a second problem: if output grows while wages are held down, who will buy the consumer goods? Hunt explains crises in Marx through the balance between two sectors, one producing consumer goods and the other producing capital goods. An imbalance in one spreads to the other in a cumulative collapse. In the slump wages fall, but output falls faster, so supply drops below consumer demand and recovery begins. Marx, Hunt adds, was no stagnationist: he expected growth in bursts, with periodic mass unemployment.

The reserve army cycle

Richard Goodwin (1967) turned Marx’s account into a model. Profits are invested. When employment is high, wages rise faster than productivity and squeeze profits; when profits fall, growth slows and employment drops. The chart follows the employment rate and the wage share over 60 years.

5. Concentration, immiserization, and the state

Marx expected capital to concentrate, because large firms beat small ones and because technology raises the smallest workable scale. He expected the working class to grow and its condition to worsen. Hunt reads this claim carefully. Marx wrote that the laborer’s lot grows worse “be his payment high or low,” so the claim is about relative position and degradation at work rather than falling wages. Marx also expected the rate of profit to fall in the long run, which Hunt mentions only briefly.

The state, in this view, protects the property relations on which class power rests, and settles rivalries among capitalists, so that it may act against some of them but not against all. Hence Marx’s skepticism that elections alone could bring socialism, which becomes the great dispute of Topics 10 and 11.

6. Beyond Hunt

  • Value and price. The “transformation problem” has been debated since 1894: Böhm-Bawerk called it a fatal contradiction, and solutions have been proposed by Bortkiewicz, Sraffian economists, the “New Interpretation,” and the temporal single-system school.
  • Crises. Marxists disagree over whether crises stem from underconsumption, disproportion between sectors, a profit squeeze, or a falling rate of profit. Hunt here stresses sectoral imbalance and demand.
  • Immiserization. Real wages in rich countries rose greatly after Marx. Defenders point to relative shares, conditions of work, and the world scale. Critics count it as a failed prediction.
  • Concentration is often counted as his most successful prediction (Topic 8).
Question to think about: Suppose wages rise steadily and workers live comfortably. What, if anything, is left of Marx’s criticism of capitalism?
