Mercantilism and the Physiocrats

Before economics had a name, merchants and officials wrote about trade as a contest between nations for gold, shipping, and manufactures. This topic covers the mercantilist doctrine of the balance of trade, the policies built on it, Hume’s argument that a permanent surplus is impossible, and the French physiocrats who first called for free trade.

1. The balance of trade

Mercantilism is the name Adam Smith gave, critically, to the “mercantile system”: the ideas and policies that guided European states from roughly 1500 to the late eighteenth century. It was never a single school. Its writers were merchants, company directors, and officials such as Thomas Mun of the East India Company, Josiah Child, and Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who wrote pamphlets on practical questions: why was silver flowing out of England, should the East India Company be allowed to export bullion, and how could the nation’s shipping be protected?

What united them was the idea of the balance of trade. A nation without gold or silver mines could increase its stock of precious metals only by selling more to foreigners than it bought from them. Mun put the rule plainly: a nation must “sell more to strangers yearly than wee consume of theirs in value” (England’s Treasure by Forraign Trade, written in the 1620s and published in 1664). Mun’s important step was to judge the balance for trade as a whole rather than for each bilateral exchange. The East India Company exported silver to Asia, but it re-exported Asian goods to Europe for more silver than it had spent, so the overall balance was favorable.

Behind the doctrine were several distinct ideas that are worth separating:

  • Bullion as wealth or as power. Gold and silver paid armies and fleets, so a state’s stock of money was a measure of its strength in war.
  • Money and circulation. Many writers worried less about hoarding than about a shortage of coin, which depressed trade and employment at home.
  • Employment. Exporting finished goods rather than raw wool kept the work of manufacturing at home. Imports of manufactures “exported” jobs.
  • Zero-sum competition. Because the world’s stock of trade and bullion was thought to be roughly fixed, one nation’s gain was another’s loss.

2. Mercantilist policy

The doctrine supported a coherent set of policies, which states pursued in different combinations:

PolicyExample
Chartered trading monopoliesThe English East India Company (1600) and the Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602) held exclusive rights to trade with Asia.
Reserving shipping for national fleetsEngland’s Navigation Act of 1651 and later acts required goods to be carried in English ships, aimed at Dutch dominance of the carrying trade.
Protecting manufacturesTariffs on imported manufactures, bans on exporting raw wool, and bounties on exports.
Colonies as captive marketsColonies supplied raw materials and bought the mother country’s manufactures, and were barred from trading directly with rivals.
State promotion of industryAs French finance minister (1665–83), Colbert founded royal manufactures, set quality regulations, built roads and canals, and raised tariffs in 1664 and 1667.

The Anglo-Dutch wars of the seventeenth century and the Anglo-French wars of the eighteenth were fought in large part over trade, colonies, and shipping. In the mercantilist world, trade policy and war were closely tied.

3. Mercantilism reconsidered

After Smith, economists treated mercantilism as a simple error: the confusion of money with wealth. Later scholars have read it more generously.

  • State-building. Gustav Schmoller (1884) of the German historical school (Topic 10) argued that mercantilism was at heart a policy of building a unified national state and economy out of a patchwork of local privileges. Eli Heckscher’s Mercantilism (1931) treated it as a system of power and unification, while remaining critical of its economics.
  • Productive capacity. Erik Reinert and others argue that the best mercantilist writers, including the Neapolitan Antonio Serra (1613), understood that a nation grows rich by moving into activities with increasing returns, such as manufacturing, rather than by hoarding gold. On this reading, mercantilism was the first theory of industrial policy (Topics 9 and 12).
  • Effective demand. In chapter 23 of the General Theory (1936), Keynes argued that the mercantilists had a point: when the domestic interest rate depends on the stock of money, a trade surplus that brings in bullion lowers interest rates and raises investment and employment (Topic 17).
  • The critics. Jacob Viner (1937) replied that the mercantilists were often confused, that their writings largely served merchants’ interests, and that they had no consistent theory.

4. Hume’s price–specie-flow mechanism

The most powerful early argument against the balance-of-trade doctrine came from David Hume’s essay “Of the Balance of Trade” (1752). Hume combined the quantity theory of money, the idea that prices rise and fall with the quantity of money in circulation, with the observation that buyers turn to whichever country is cheaper. Suppose a country runs a surplus and gold flows in. Its money stock rises, so its prices rise; the deficit country’s money stock and prices fall. The surplus country’s goods become dearer abroad and foreign goods become cheaper at home, so its exports fall and imports rise until the surplus disappears. Money, Hume concluded, finds its own level, like water. A policy of permanent surplus defeats itself.

The mechanism rests on two assumptions that later writers questioned. The incoming gold has to enter circulation and raise prices; if the state or banks hoard it, prices need not rise. And prices have to be flexible; if they are sticky, the adjustment comes through output and employment instead, which was Keynes’s point.

The price–specie-flow mechanism

Two countries of equal size, Britain and France, share 100 units of gold. Prices in each are proportional to its stock of gold in circulation, and each country’s exports rise when its prices are lower than its rival’s. Start Britain with little gold, so that it runs a surplus, and watch the surplus correct itself. Then let Britain hoard part of the gold it earns.

5. The physiocrats

In France, the reaction against mercantilism came from the physiocrats (“rule of nature”), led by François Quesnay, a physician at the court of Louis XV. Their starting point was agriculture, not trade. Only the land, they argued, produces a net product (produit net), a surplus over the costs of production. Manufacturing and commerce are “sterile”: useful, but they only transform or move what agriculture has produced. Quesnay’s Tableau économique (1758) traced how the net product circulates among farmers, landlords, and the sterile class, the first model of the economy as a circular flow.

The trade policy that followed was the reverse of Colbert’s. France’s grain trade was tightly controlled, with internal tolls and bans on exports, to keep bread cheap in the towns. The physiocrats argued that freeing the grain trade would raise grain prices, increase the farmers’ returns and the net product, and so make the whole kingdom richer. Their slogan, laissez faire, laissez passer, was first a demand to let grain move freely. The crown freed the grain trade in 1763–64, and A. R. J. Turgot, as controller-general, freed it again in 1774. Both experiments were reversed after bad harvests, high prices, and riots, most famously the “Flour War” of 1775.

MercantilistsTrade enriches a nation through a surplus of exports over imports. Protect manufactures, keep raw materials cheap, regulate trade in the national interest.
PhysiocratsTrade creates no new wealth; only land does. But freeing trade, especially in grain, raises the net product of agriculture.

6. Legacy

Smith learned from both groups. He took Hume’s argument against the balance-of-trade doctrine and the physiocrats’ case for natural liberty, but rejected the physiocrats’ view that manufacturing is sterile (Topic 2). The mercantilist idea that a trade surplus is a sign of national strength never disappeared. It returns in nineteenth-century arguments for protecting industry (Topic 9), in Keynes’s reconsideration of the mercantilists (Topic 17), and in debates over export-led growth and persistent surpluses in the world economy today.

Question to think about: Mercantilists equated wealth with a trade surplus. Is that idea dead, or does it survive in today’s politics of trade?
