Polanyi and the Double Movement

Writing during the Second World War, Karl Polanyi argued that the catastrophe of his time had a single root: the nineteenth-century attempt to organize society around a self-regulating market, held together internationally by free trade and the gold standard. His book The Great Transformation (1944) offers no theory of comparative advantage, but it has become one of the most cited works on the politics of globalization.

1. Polanyi and his time

Karl Polanyi was born in Vienna in 1886 and grew up in Budapest. He worked as a journalist in “Red Vienna” in the 1920s, where he took part in the debate with Ludwig von Mises over whether socialist economic calculation was possible. Forced to leave Austria as fascism rose, he moved to England, where he taught workers’ education classes, and then to Bennington College in Vermont, where he wrote The Great Transformation. After the war he taught at Columbia University and turned to economic anthropology, studying how ancient and non-Western societies organized their economies through reciprocity and redistribution rather than market exchange.

2. Embeddedness and fictitious commodities

For most of human history, Polanyi argued, the economy was embedded in society. Production and distribution were governed by kinship, religion, custom, and politics, and markets, where they existed, were limited and regulated. Only in nineteenth-century Britain was there an attempt to create a market society, in which the economy was disembedded and society itself was run as an adjunct to the market.

That attempt required treating three things as commodities that are not produced for sale: labor, which is human activity; land, which is nature; and money, which is a token of purchasing power created by banks and states. Polanyi called them fictitious commodities. Leaving their supply and price entirely to the market would, he argued, destroy the people, the natural environment, and the businesses that depended on them. Unemployment and destitution, the degradation of land and communities, and violent swings of credit and prices would follow.

The self-regulating market did not arise spontaneously. In Polanyi’s phrase, “laissez-faire was planned”: it was created by deliberate state action, such as the reform of the Poor Law in 1834 that forced workers into the labor market, the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846, and the adoption of the gold standard.

3. Free trade and the gold standard

Polanyi saw nineteenth-century civilization as resting on four institutions: the balance of power among the great states, the international gold standard, the self-regulating market, and the liberal state. Internationally, the self-regulating market meant free trade, the free movement of capital, and the gold standard, which tied each country’s money supply to its reserves of gold.

The gold standard made the international market the master of domestic policy. A country that lost gold through a trade deficit had to contract credit and let prices and wages fall until its exports recovered, which is Hume’s mechanism (Topic 1) seen from the point of view of the workers and farmers who bore the deflation. Societies resisted. Workers formed unions and demanded factory laws and social insurance; farmers demanded tariffs; businesses formed cartels; central banks tried to cushion the blows. Each protective measure made the market system more rigid and less able to adjust. In the 1920s and 1930s, the attempt to restore the gold standard after the First World War forced deflation on societies that would no longer accept it. The system broke down, and in several countries fascism offered a way out by destroying democracy.

4. The double movement

Polanyi described the history of market society as a double movement. One movement extends the market, pressing to turn more of life into commodities and to remove barriers to trade and capital. The other is the protective countermovement of society, a spontaneous and widely varied reaction, by groups of every political color, to protect people, nature, and productive organization from the market’s effects. 5. Embedded liberalism

The Great Transformation was published the year of the Bretton Woods conference. The postwar order that emerged answered many of Polanyi’s concerns, though not as he would have wished. John Gerard Ruggie, in an influential article of 1982, called it embedded liberalism. Unlike the liberalism of the nineteenth century, the postwar compromise allowed governments to pursue full employment and build welfare states while trade was gradually liberalized. The GATT reduced tariffs in stages but allowed safeguards, exceptions for agriculture, and escape clauses. The International Monetary Fund provided credit to countries in deficit so that they need not deflate, and permitted controls on capital movements. Multilateral openness was “embedded” in domestic social commitments.

From the 1980s, with the liberalization of capital flows, the WTO’s deeper rules, and the spread of free-market policies, many scholars argued that this compromise had been replaced by a new attempt to disembed the market, now on a global scale.

6. Polanyi today

Polanyi’s ideas were revived in the 1990s and again after 2008, as readers looked for ways to understand the backlash against globalization. Dani Rodrik’s analysis of the tensions between global markets and democratic politics (Topic 16) draws explicitly on Polanyi. Political scientists such as Mark Blyth have read the rise of populist movements since 2016 as a countermovement against hyperglobalization. Others warn that Polanyi’s framework can be stretched to explain almost any political reaction, and that the countermovement, as in the 1930s, can take nationalist and authoritarian forms as well as social-democratic ones.

Question to think about: Is the backlash against globalization since 2016 a Polanyian double movement?
