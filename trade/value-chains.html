Neo-Structuralism and Global Value Chains

After the debt crisis of the 1980s, Latin American structuralists accepted that their economies would be open to trade, but insisted that openness alone would not transform them. At the same time, production was being split across countries into global value chains. This topic follows both developments and the question that joins them: in a world where goods are made everywhere, which countries capture the value?

1. From structuralism to neo-structuralism

The debt crisis of 1982 ended the era of import substitution in Latin America (Topic 18). Under pressure from creditors and the IMF, most countries cut tariffs, privatized state enterprises, and opened to foreign capital, the program later called the Washington Consensus. Growth in the 1980s, Latin America’s “lost decade,” and much of the 1990s was disappointing.

ECLAC (the commission had added the Caribbean to its name in 1984) responded with a new program. Its report Changing Production Patterns with Social Equity (1990), largely the work of the Chilean economist Fernando Fajnzylber, accepted openness to trade and macroeconomic stability. But it argued that competitiveness in world markets should rest on rising productivity and the incorporation of technical progress, which Fajnzylber called authentic competitiveness, rather than on low wages, currency devaluation, and the depletion of natural resources, which he called spurious competitiveness. Fajnzylber also pointed to an “empty box”: not a single Latin American country had achieved both fast growth and reduced inequality. Neo-structuralism, developed by Fajnzylber, Osvaldo Sunkel, José Antonio Ocampo, and others, kept the structuralist focus on productive structure, technology, and the external constraint (Topic 19), while abandoning blanket protection in favor of policies to diversify exports, build technological capabilities, and spread the gains more widely.

2. From commodity chains to global value chains

The idea of tracing a product through every stage of production came from world-systems analysis (Topic 25). Terence Hopkins and Immanuel Wallerstein (1986) defined a commodity chain as the network of labor and production processes whose end result is a finished commodity, and used it to show how the world-economy distributes surplus among core and periphery. The sociologist Gary Gereffi adapted the concept in the 1990s to study how firms organize global production. In Commodity Chains and Global Capitalism (1994), edited with Miguel Korzeniewicz, he distinguished:

  • Producer-driven chains, such as automobiles, aircraft, and machinery, coordinated by large manufacturers that control technology and production networks.
  • Buyer-driven chains, such as clothing, footwear, and toys, coordinated by large retailers and brand-name companies, such as Walmart or Nike, that design and market products but own no factories.

By the 2000s, the term global value chain had replaced commodity chain. Trade in parts and components grew faster than trade in finished goods, and economists began to measure trade in value added rather than gross exports. The OECD–WTO Trade in Value Added database (2013) showed, for example, that a large part of the value of China’s exports of electronics consisted of imported components.

3. Governance of value chains

Gary Gereffi, John Humphrey, and Timothy Sturgeon (2005) proposed five types of governance, depending on how complex the information exchanged between firms is, how easily it can be codified, and how capable suppliers are:

TypeDescriptionPower of lead firm
MarketSimple products, arm’s-length transactions, price as the main signalLow
ModularSuppliers make products to a customer’s specification, using codified standardsRising ↓
RelationalComplex, tacit information; mutual dependence built on trust and repeated interaction
CaptiveSmall suppliers depend on a dominant buyer that closely monitors them
HierarchyProduction within a single multinational firmHigh

For a developing country, the type of governance matters because it shapes whether local firms can learn, and whether the lead firm will help them or block them from moving into more valuable activities.

4. Upgrading and the smile curve

John Humphrey and Hubert Schmitz (2002) identified four kinds of upgrading in value chains: process upgrading (producing more efficiently), product upgrading (moving into more sophisticated products), functional upgrading (taking on new functions, such as design or marketing, that add more value), and chain upgrading (moving into a different, more valuable chain). Firms in captive chains often find process and product upgrading encouraged but functional upgrading resisted.

Stan Shih, founder of the Taiwanese computer company Acer, illustrated the stakes in the early 1990s with the smile curve. Value added is high at the beginning of the chain (research, design, key components) and at the end (branding, marketing, services), and low in the middle (manufacturing and assembly). Many observers argue that the smile has deepened since the 1970s, as manufacturing was dispersed to many low-wage locations while knowledge-intensive activities stayed concentrated. 5. Diversification and economic complexity

A related line of research asks not which stage a country occupies but what range of products it can make. Ricardo Hausmann, César Hidalgo, and their colleagues argued that countries grow by accumulating productive capabilities, which they measure indirectly by the diversity and sophistication of the products a country exports. Their economic complexity index, published in The Atlas of Economic Complexity (2011), predicts future growth better than many standard variables. Because capabilities are hard to acquire, countries tend to move into products “close” to the ones they already make. The policy implication, which fits both neo-structuralism and Lin’s new structural economics (Topic 12), is that diversification is a central goal of development policy, and that the products a country exports matter for its future, as structuralists had always argued.

6. Who captures the value?

Value chains allowed many developing countries, above all in East Asia, to industrialize by joining a chain rather than building a whole industry. Critics point to the other side. In a widely cited study of Apple’s products, Kenneth Kraemer, Greg Linden, and Jason Dedrick (2011) estimated that only a small fraction of the iPhone’s retail price went to Chinese assembly labor, while Apple’s own profits took the largest share. William Milberg and Deborah Winkler (2013) argued that lead firms used global outsourcing to raise their markups and returns to shareholders, while labor’s share of income fell in both rich and developing countries. Countries that enter chains at the bottom risk being locked into low-value assembly, competing with one another on wages, a modern version of the problem Prebisch identified for primary commodities. The question for policy is how to move up the smile curve before wages rise and assembly moves elsewhere, and whether the governance of the chain allows it.

Question to think about: In a world of value chains, does it still matter which goods a country exports, or only which tasks it performs?
