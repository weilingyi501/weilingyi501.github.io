World-Systems Analysis

Most theories of trade begin with nations and ask how they trade with one another. World-systems analysis reverses the order. It begins with the capitalist world-economy as a whole, a single system with one division of labor, and asks how nations come to occupy different positions within it. Trade, in this view, is how the world-economy organizes a hierarchy among regions.

1. The world-system as the unit of analysis

Immanuel Wallerstein, an American sociologist who began his career studying postcolonial Africa, published the first volume of The Modern World-System in 1974. He argued that social change could not be understood country by country, as modernization theory and much of Marxism assumed. The proper unit of analysis was the world-system: a social system with a single division of labor and multiple cultures. Historically there had been two kinds: world-empires, unified politically, such as Rome or imperial China, and world-economies, which have one division of labor but many states. The modern world-system, which emerged in Europe during the “long sixteenth century” (about 1450–1640), is a capitalist world-economy. Its distinctive feature is that no single state controls it, so capital can move among states, playing them against one another.

Wallerstein drew on the French historian Fernand Braudel, on Marx, and on dependency theory (Topic 23). In 1976 he founded the Fernand Braudel Center at the State University of New York at Binghamton, which became the hub of world-systems research. Later volumes traced the system through mercantilism, the age of revolutions, and the nineteenth century.

2. Core, semi-periphery, and periphery

The world-economy has an axial division of labor between core-like and peripheral production processes. Wallerstein defined them not by the goods involved but by their degree of profitability and monopolization:

ZoneProductionLabor and statesExamples in the sixteenth century
CoreRelatively monopolized, high-profit, capital- and skill-intensive processesWage labor, relatively high wages; strong statesNorthwestern Europe: the Netherlands, England, northern France
Semi-peripheryA mix of core and peripheral processesIntermediate; often states with strong ambitionsMediterranean Europe: Spain, Portugal, northern Italy
PeripheryHighly competitive, low-profit processesCoerced labor (slavery, “second serfdom”); weak statesEastern Europe (grain) and the Americas (silver, sugar)

Trade between the zones transfers surplus from periphery to core. The semi-periphery is Wallerstein’s distinctive contribution: it is both exploited and exploiting, and it stabilizes the system politically by preventing a simple polarization between rich and poor. Countries can move between zones. The Netherlands and England rose to the core, Spain and Portugal fell to the semi-periphery, and in the twentieth century Japan and South Korea rose. But the hierarchy itself persists: not every country can be in the core at once, because the core is defined by its relation to the periphery.

3. Commodity chains and unequal exchange

To trace how surplus moves through the world-economy, Terence Hopkins and Wallerstein (1977, 1986) introduced the concept of the commodity chain: the network of labor and production processes whose end result is a finished commodity. Following a chain, such as the shipbuilding or flour chains of the sixteenth century, shows that core and periphery are linked within a single production process, with the most profitable nodes located in the core. The concept was later adapted by Gary Gereffi into the analysis of global value chains (Topic 20). Wallerstein also accepted Emmanuel’s theory of unequal exchange (Topic 24) as one of the mechanisms through which surplus is transferred from periphery to core.

4. Hegemony and its cycles

Because no state controls the world-economy, its political structure is an interstate system. At rare moments, one state achieves hegemony: a simultaneous lead in production, commerce, and finance, which lets it shape the rules of the system. Wallerstein identified three hegemonies: the United Provinces (the Netherlands) in the mid-seventeenth century, Britain in the mid-nineteenth century, and the United States after 1945. Each hegemony was brief. The hegemon’s lead in production erodes first, then in commerce, and last in finance. Each hegemonic power championed free trade at the height of its strength, since open markets favored its efficient producers, a pattern that echoes List’s “kicking away the ladder” (Topic 9).

The world-economy also moves in long waves of expansion and stagnation, which world-systems writers link to Kondratieff cycles of about fifty years.

5. Arrighi’s systemic cycles of accumulation

Giovanni Arrighi, an Italian economist who worked in Rhodesia and Tanzania before joining the Braudel Center, developed the theory of hegemony in The Long Twentieth Century (1994). Following Braudel, he identified four systemic cycles of accumulation, each led by a different agency. Each cycle has two phases, following Marx’s formula for capital, M–C–M′. In the first phase (M–C), capital invests in trade and production, and the leading agency expands material output. In the second phase (C–M′), when competition squeezes profits in trade and production, capital shifts to finance. These financial expansions are periods of apparent prosperity for the declining leader, but Arrighi called them “signals of autumn”: they announce the end of a cycle and the emergence of a new center. 6. The rise of China

In his last major book, Adam Smith in Beijing (2007), Arrighi argued that the center of the world-economy was shifting to East Asia, and that China’s rise followed a path of market-based development closer to Smith’s “natural” progress of opulence than to the capital-intensive Western model. He read the U.S. financial expansion since the 1970s, and its wars in the Middle East, as signs of the terminal crisis of American hegemony. Others in the tradition, including Christopher Chase-Dunn, have studied whether China is moving from the semi-periphery into the core, and whether a new hegemony, a multipolar order, or a prolonged period of systemic chaos lies ahead. Wallerstein himself argued that the capitalist world-system as a whole had entered a structural crisis from which it would not recover in its present form.

7. Critics

World-systems analysis has been criticized from several sides. Robert Brenner (1977) argued that it located the origins of capitalism in trade and exchange rather than in class relations of production, a “neo-Smithian” error that it shared with Frank. Theda Skocpol and others argued that it reduced states to their position in the world-economy and neglected their independent role. Economic historians questioned whether trade with the periphery was large enough to have financed European development, as Patrick O’Brien argued in 1982. Others noted that the theory’s categories are flexible enough to accommodate almost any outcome. Defenders respond that world-systems analysis is a perspective rather than a closed theory, and that its long-term, global view captures patterns, such as the rise and fall of hegemonic powers and the persistence of the core–periphery hierarchy, that country-by-country analysis misses.

Question to think about: Is China moving from the semi-periphery into the core, and what would that mean for the world-system?
