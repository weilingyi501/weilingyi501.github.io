Hamilton, List, and the Infant Industry

The first systematic challenge to free trade came from countries trying to catch up with industrial Britain. Alexander Hamilton in the young United States and Friedrich List in the divided German states argued that a latecomer must build its own industries behind protective walls before it can compete. Their arguments shaped the policies of almost every country that industrialized after Britain.

1. Hamilton’s Report on Manufactures

In 1791, Alexander Hamilton, the first U.S. secretary of the treasury, sent Congress his Report on the Subject of Manufactures. The United States was an agrarian economy that exported tobacco, grain, and timber and imported manufactures from Britain. Thomas Jefferson and many others thought it should stay that way: a republic of independent farmers. Hamilton disagreed. Manufacturing, he argued, would raise productivity through the division of labor and machinery, employ women and children, attract immigrants, and widen the market for farm produce. A nation that depended on foreign manufactures was also vulnerable in war.

Hamilton answered Smith directly. Left to themselves, Americans would not start new industries, because of habit, fear of failure, and above all the advantage of established foreign competitors, who might even sell below cost to destroy a new rival. The state had to help. Hamilton’s preferred tools were bounties (subsidies) and premiums for new industries, along with moderate protective duties, tariff exemptions for raw materials, and improvements in roads and canals. Congress adopted few of his specific proposals at the time, but the Report became the founding document of American protectionism.

2. The American System and Henry Carey

After the War of 1812 cut off British imports and American manufacturing grew behind that wall, Congress passed protective tariffs in 1816, 1824, and 1828. Senator Henry Clay called the combination of tariffs, a national bank, and federally funded roads and canals the American System. The tariff divided the country: Northern manufacturers favored it, while Southern planters who exported cotton and imported manufactures opposed it. South Carolina’s attempt to nullify the 1828 “Tariff of Abominations” brought a constitutional crisis in 1832–33.

Henry Carey, the leading American economist of the mid-nineteenth century, gave the protectionist case a broader theory. In The Harmony of Interests (1851), he argued that British free trade kept other countries as suppliers of raw materials, exhausted their soil, and concentrated manufacturing in England. Protection would bring the factory close to the farm, create a home market, and allow a balanced, diversified economy. After the Civil War, the Republican Party kept U.S. tariffs among the highest in the world until the 1930s.

3. Friedrich List

Friedrich List was a German economist and political activist who campaigned for a customs union among the German states, was imprisoned and exiled for his political views, and spent the years 1825–32 in the United States. There he read Hamilton, met the protectionists of Pennsylvania, and published Outlines of American Political Economy (1827). Back in Europe, he promoted railways and the Zollverein, the German customs union founded in 1834, which removed tariffs among the German states while maintaining a common tariff against outsiders. His major work, The National System of Political Economy, appeared in 1841.

List attacked what he called the “cosmopolitical economy” of Smith and his followers. Their theory, he said, assumed a world already at peace and united, in which nations did not matter. In the real world, nations were at different stages of development and competed with one another. Free trade between a developed and an undeveloped nation would keep the undeveloped one in a position of permanent inferiority. List noted that Britain itself had become great through centuries of protection and navigation laws, and only then preached free trade to others:

It is a very common clever device that when anyone has attained the summit of greatness, he kicks away the ladder by which he has climbed up.Friedrich List, The National System of Political Economy (1841), trans. Sampson Lloyd (1885)

Ha-Joon Chang took the phrase as the title of his history of trade policy (Topic 12).

4. Productive powers and stages of development

List’s central distinction was between exchange values and productive powers. Classical economics measured the gains from trade by the goods a country could obtain now. But the power of producing wealth, List argued, was infinitely more important than wealth itself. A tariff on manufactures imposes a cost today, as consumers pay more. It is an investment, however, if it builds the skills, machinery, institutions, and habits of industry that will make the nation richer in the future. Like an individual who sacrifices present income to learn a trade, a nation may sacrifice present consumption to acquire manufacturing power.

List placed nations in a sequence of stages: from barbarism through pastoral and agricultural stages, to an agricultural-manufacturing stage, and finally to an agricultural-manufacturing-commercial stage. Trade policy should depend on the stage:

  • A backward agricultural nation should trade freely with more advanced nations, to acquire their goods, techniques, and capital.
  • A nation with the population, resources, and institutions to industrialize, but facing a dominant foreign rival, should protect its manufactures.
  • Once its industries can compete, it should return to free trade.

List’s protection was thus temporary, selective (manufactures, not agriculture or raw materials), and conditional on a country’s capacity to develop industry. He thought only large nations in temperate climates were suited to it, a view colored by the prejudices of his time.

5. What a tariff does

Mainstream economics analyzes a tariff with a partial-equilibrium diagram that goes back to Marshall’s consumer surplus (Topic 5). For a small country that cannot affect world prices, a tariff raises the domestic price by the full amount of the tariff. Consumers lose, domestic producers and the government gain, and the losses exceed the gains: the difference is the deadweight loss from producing at too high a cost and consuming too little. This static loss is exactly the “sacrifice of exchange values” that List accepted as the price of building productive powers.

6. When does infant-industry protection pay?

The static loss is not the whole story if, as List argued, protection lets an industry learn. As firms gain experience, their costs fall. Protection is worth it if the future gains, once the industry can produce below the world price, outweigh the costs of protecting it while it learns. This is the test proposed by Mill and Bastable (Topic 4). The calculator below makes it concrete.

The infant-industry test

A new industry starts with costs 50% above the world price. Protected from imports, it learns, and its cost falls over time toward 80% of the world price. Each year of protection, consumers pay the difference between the industry’s cost and the world price. Once its cost falls below the world price, protection ends and the country gains the difference each year. Future costs and gains, counted over 80 years, are discounted to the present. 7. The historical record

The policy record is mixed and contested. In the late nineteenth century, the United States and Germany grew fastest while maintaining high tariffs, and Paul Bairoch argued that protection and growth went together in that era. Economic historians such as Douglas Irwin respond that U.S. growth came mainly from population, capital, and productivity in sectors that tariffs did not protect, and that some protected industries, such as tinplate in the 1890s, would probably have developed anyway. Studies of protection in the twentieth century find both cases that paid off and many where protected industries never grew up. The difficult questions are practical: which industries to choose, how to make protection temporary, and how to keep it from being captured by the interests it creates. The developmental states of East Asia offered one answer (Topic 11).

Question to think about: Mill accepted the infant-industry argument in principle. What separates List’s view from Mill’s?
