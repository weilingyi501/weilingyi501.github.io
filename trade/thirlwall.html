Post-Keynesian Trade: Cumulative Causation and Thirlwall’s Law

Mainstream trade theory explains which goods a country exports. Post-Keynesian economists asked a different question: how does trade affect how fast a country can grow? Their answer turned on demand rather than supply. Regions and nations that win markets grow faster, become more productive, and win more markets, while those that lose fall further behind. And in the long run, a country cannot grow faster than its exports allow it to pay for its imports.

1. Myrdal and cumulative causation

Gunnar Myrdal, the Swedish economist who later shared the Nobel Prize with Friedrich Hayek in 1974, set out the principle of circular and cumulative causation in Economic Theory and Underdeveloped Regions (1957). Standard theory assumed that markets tend toward equilibrium: if one region is richer, capital will flow to poorer regions where returns are higher, and labor will flow to richer ones, narrowing the gap. Myrdal argued that in reality the forces of the market tend to widen inequalities between regions and nations. A region that gains an initial advantage attracts capital, skilled workers, and trade, which raise its productivity and incomes further. The regions left behind suffer backwash effects: they lose their most enterprising people, their savings flow to the center, and their industries cannot compete. Expansion in the center may also generate spread effects, such as rising demand for the periphery’s products, but in poor countries these are usually weaker than the backwash. Trade, on this view, can reinforce inequality rather than reduce it, unless states intervene.

2. Kaldor’s growth laws

Nicholas Kaldor, a Hungarian-born Cambridge economist and adviser to British Labour governments, gave cumulative causation an empirical form. In his inaugural lecture, Causes of the Slow Rate of Economic Growth of the United Kingdom (1966), he proposed what became known as Kaldor’s growth laws:

  • The faster the growth of manufacturing output, the faster the growth of the whole economy. Manufacturing is the engine of growth.
  • The faster manufacturing output grows, the faster manufacturing productivity grows, because of increasing returns from specialization, learning, and new investment. This relationship, first found by P. J. Verdoorn (1949), is called Verdoorn’s law.
  • The faster manufacturing grows, the faster productivity rises in the rest of the economy, as labor moves out of low-productivity activities.

Since productivity growth depends on output growth, the key question becomes what determines the growth of demand for manufactures. For a mature open economy, Kaldor answered: exports.

3. Export-led growth

In “The Case for Regional Policies” (1970), Kaldor argued that the growth of a region is governed by the growth of demand for its exports. Fast export growth raises output, which, through Verdoorn’s law, raises productivity, which makes exports more competitive, which raises export growth further. Robert Dixon and Anthony Thirlwall (1975) formalized this cumulative export-led growth model. Once a region or country establishes a growth advantage, it tends to keep it. This is the same logic as Krugman’s later model of core and periphery (Topic 7), reached by a different route. It also helps explain why, in this tradition, the composition of exports matters: goods whose world demand grows fast, and whose production enjoys strong increasing returns, offer better prospects than others, an echo of Prebisch and Singer (Topic 18).

4. Thirlwall’s law

In 1979 Anthony Thirlwall published a short paper, “The Balance of Payments Constraint as an Explanation of International Growth Rate Differences,” which became one of the most tested results in post-Keynesian economics. His argument starts from a simple fact: in the long run, a country cannot run ever-larger trade deficits, because it cannot finance them indefinitely. Its imports must grow roughly in line with its exports.

Suppose relative prices do not change much over the long run, so that trade responds mainly to income. Exports grow with world income, at a rate set by the income elasticity of demand for exports, ε. Imports grow with domestic income, at a rate set by the income elasticity of demand for imports, π. If exports and imports must grow at the same rate, the fastest rate at which the economy can grow without a growing deficit is

yB = x / π = ε × z / π

where x is the growth of exports and z is the growth of world income. This is Thirlwall’s law. A country whose exports are in high demand as the world grows richer (high ε), and whose residents do not spend much of each extra unit of income on imports (low π), can grow fast. A country with the opposite characteristics will hit a balance-of-payments constraint: when it tries to grow faster, imports surge, the deficit widens, and it must slow down, long before it reaches full employment or the limits of its productive capacity. The income elasticities reflect the non-price characteristics of a country’s goods, such as quality, technology, and design, and so ultimately the structure of its economy.

5. Extensions and debates

  • Capital flows. Thirlwall and Mohammed Nureldin Hussain (1982) extended the law to allow for capital inflows, which let a country grow faster for a time. Later work added the requirement that foreign debt stay at a sustainable ratio to income, which brings back a constraint very close to the original law.
  • Many sectors. Ricardo Araujo and Gilberto Lima (2007) showed that a country’s income elasticities are weighted averages of the elasticities of the goods it trades, so structural change toward goods with high world demand can relax the constraint. This links Thirlwall’s law directly to industrial policy.
  • Testing. Many studies have found that growth rates predicted by the law come close to actual long-run growth for a wide range of countries. Critics such as John McCombie, a collaborator of Thirlwall’s, have discussed whether these tests can distinguish the law from an identity.
  • Which way does causation run? Paul Krugman (1989) noticed the same empirical regularity, which he called the 45-degree rule, but interpreted it the other way around: fast-growing countries expand the range of goods they produce, which raises the measured income elasticity of demand for their exports. On this view, growth determines elasticities, not the reverse.

6. The limits of export-led growth

If growth is constrained by the balance of payments, export success is the key to fast growth. But not every country can raise its exports faster than its imports at the same time; one country’s surplus is another’s deficit. This is the fallacy of composition in export-led growth, which Keynes had identified in the beggar-my-neighbour policies of the 1930s (Topic 17). If many developing countries pursue export-led strategies at once, they may flood world markets for the same products, depress their prices, and meet protection in rich countries. Post-Keynesians therefore emphasize policies that raise demand globally and that let deficit countries grow without being forced into austerity, as well as industrial policies that shift a country’s production toward goods with better demand prospects.

Question to think about: If growth is constrained by the balance of payments, what can a country do to relax the constraint?
