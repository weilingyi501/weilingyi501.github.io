The German Historical School

In nineteenth-century Germany, a school of economists rejected the idea that economics could discover universal laws by deduction. Economic life, they argued, had to be studied historically, nation by nation and stage by stage. Their approach led them to reread mercantilism as nation-building, to treat trade policy as a matter of time and place, and to support the turn to protection in the newly unified German Empire.

1. History against deduction

The “older” historical school began with Wilhelm Roscher’s Outline of Lectures on Political Economy According to the Historical Method (1843), followed by Bruno Hildebrand and Karl Knies. They did not reject classical economics outright, but they insisted that its propositions held only for particular societies at particular times. The economy was part of a nation’s whole life, bound up with its law, politics, customs, and religion, and it changed over time. Economics should therefore proceed by careful historical and statistical study, from which generalizations might eventually emerge.

The “younger” historical school, led from the 1870s by Gustav Schmoller, went further. Schmoller distrusted abstract theory and directed his students toward detailed archival studies of guilds, cities, industries, and administrative institutions. The school dominated German university economics for a generation. In 1872 its members founded the Verein für Socialpolitik (Association for Social Policy), which supported factory legislation, social insurance, and a strong state that would reconcile workers to the empire. Critics called them “socialists of the chair.”

For trade, the implication was clear. There was no single best trade policy. Free trade might suit Britain, the established industrial leader; it might not suit Germany, still catching up. Trade policy had to be judged by its effects on a particular nation at a particular stage of its development, the position Friedrich List had taken (Topic 9).

2. Stages of economic development

A characteristic product of the school was the theory of economic stages. Each writer divided history differently, depending on which feature of economic life he considered fundamental:

WriterStagesCriterion
Friedrich List (1841)Pastoral, agricultural, agricultural-manufacturing, agricultural-manufacturing-commercialMain branch of production
Bruno Hildebrand (1864)Natural (barter) economy, money economy, credit economyMeans of exchange
Karl Bücher (1893)Closed household economy, town economy, national economyDistance between producer and consumer
Gustav Schmoller (1884)Village, town, territory, state, and their economic policiesSize of the political unit that organizes the economy

Stage theories were later criticized as speculative and too neat. Yet their basic idea, that the appropriate economic policy depends on a country’s level of development, runs through later development economics, from Alexander Gerschenkron’s analysis of latecomers (Topic 13) to Justin Yifu Lin’s new structural economics (Topic 12).

3. Schmoller and the mercantile system

Schmoller’s essay The Mercantile System and Its Historical Significance (1884) offered a new interpretation of the doctrine Smith had attacked (Topic 1). Mercantilism, Schmoller argued, was not mainly a confusion of money with wealth. It was the economic side of state-building: the effort of early modern monarchs to replace the fragmented economies of towns and provinces, with their own tolls, privileges, and regulations, by a unified national economy under a single state. Tariffs, navigation laws, and industrial promotion were tools for creating a national market and national power. Schmoller had in mind especially the policies of the Prussian kings of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, which he saw as the foundation of Germany’s later rise.

This reading influenced generations of historians. Eli Heckscher’s Mercantilism (1931) accepted that mercantilism was a system of power and unification, while remaining critical of its economic ideas.

4. Iron and rye: the tariff of 1879

After unification in 1871, the German Empire at first followed a low-tariff policy, in line with the European wave of trade treaties that followed the Cobden–Chevalier treaty of 1860. Then came the depression that began in 1873. Cheap grain from the American Midwest and the Russian steppe, carried by railways and steamships, flooded into Europe and drove down farm prices. German heavy industry faced falling prices and British competition. In 1879 Chancellor Otto von Bismarck introduced a tariff that protected both industry and agriculture. The alliance it cemented, between Ruhr industrialists and the large estate owners (Junkers) east of the Elbe, became known as the “marriage of iron and rye.” Tariffs also gave the imperial government revenue independent of the states.

Most members of the historical school supported the turn to protection, though they disagreed about the agricultural tariffs that raised the price of workers’ bread. Germany’s turn was part of a broader retreat from free trade in continental Europe. France raised its tariffs in 1881 and again with the Méline tariff of 1892, and the United States kept high tariffs throughout. Only Britain and a few smaller countries remained committed to free trade before 1914.

5. The Methodenstreit

In 1883 the Austrian economist Carl Menger, one of the founders of marginal utility theory, published a defense of abstract theory that attacked the historical school. Schmoller replied with a dismissive review, and Menger answered with a pamphlet. The ensuing “battle over methods” (Methodenstreit) divided German-speaking economics for decades. Menger held that economics must start from a few simple assumptions about individual behavior and deduce exact laws; history and statistics could not replace theory. Schmoller held that such theory was empty unless grounded in the observation of real institutions.

Later economists, including Max Weber and Joseph Schumpeter, argued that both sides were partly right and that theory and history needed each other. But the dispute left a lasting split. Theoretical economics developed in Vienna, Lausanne, and Cambridge, while the historical approach moved into economic history, sociology, and, in the United States, institutional economics.

6. Legacy

In the late nineteenth century, many young American economists took doctorates in Germany. Richard Ely, Henry Carter Adams, and others returned to found the American Economic Association in 1885 on a program that echoed the Verein für Socialpolitik, rejecting laissez-faire and calling for historical and empirical study. Their students, together with Thorstein Veblen and John R. Commons, created American institutionalism (Topic 13). Today, Erik Reinert and others describe the historical school, together with the mercantilists, List, and the developmentalists, as an “Other Canon” of economics that emphasizes production, learning, and the role of the state, against the canon of Smith and Ricardo that emphasizes exchange.

Question to think about: Is the claim that trade policy should depend on a country’s stage of development a theory, or a refusal to theorize?
