Course in development

Theories of International Trade

From the mercantilists to global value chains: how economists have explained why nations trade, who gains from it, and whether trade helps poorer countries catch up or keeps them in their place.

About the course

Every theory of trade was written for a particular moment in the history of the world economy. The mercantilists wrote for monarchs competing for colonies and bullion; Smith and Ricardo wrote as Britain became the workshop of the world; Hamilton and List wrote for latecomers trying to catch up with Britain; Prebisch wrote for Latin American exporters of coffee, copper, and wheat; and Krugman wrote as most world trade came to flow between similar rich countries. This course takes each theory in turn, places it in the conditions that produced it, and follows the debates between traditions that still shape arguments over tariffs, trade agreements, and industrial policy.

Three questions run through the whole course:

GainsWhy do nations trade, and does trade make every country better off?
DistributionWho gains and who loses from trade, between countries and within them?
DevelopmentDoes trade help poorer countries catch up, or does it lock in a hierarchy among nations?

Learning goals

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Trace the major theories of international trade from mercantilism to the present and place each in the economic and political conditions that produced it.
  2. Work through key models by hand, including comparative advantage, the Heckscher–Ohlin model, the tariff in partial equilibrium, the Prebisch–Singer terms of trade, and balance-of-payments-constrained growth.
  3. Compare how different traditions answer the same questions about the gains from trade, their distribution, and their effect on development.
  4. Use these theories to analyze present-day debates over tariffs, trade agreements, industrial policy, and global value chains.

Readings

Main text

Douglas A. Irwin, Against the Tide: An Intellectual History of Free Trade (Princeton University Press, 1996).

Companion texts

Paul Krugman, Maurice Obstfeld, and Marc Melitz, International Economics: Theory and Policy (Pearson), the chapters on trade theory and trade policy.

Ha-Joon Chang, Kicking Away the Ladder: Development Strategy in Historical Perspective (Anthem, 2002).

Erik Reinert, How Rich Countries Got Rich … and Why Poor Countries Stay Poor (Constable, 2007).

Dani Rodrik, The Globalization Paradox: Democracy and the Future of the World Economy (Norton, 2011).

Anwar Shaikh, Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises (Oxford University Press, 2016), chapter 11.

Primary sources

Short selections from Mun, Hume, Smith, Ricardo, Mill, List, Ohlin, Keynes, Prebisch, Emmanuel, and Wallerstein.
