Unequal Exchange

In 1969, the Greek-born economist Arghiri Emmanuel published L’échange inégal (translated as Unequal Exchange, 1972). Applying Marx’s theory of prices of production to the world economy, he argued that trade between high-wage and low-wage countries transfers value from the poor to the rich even when goods exchange at their market prices and no one cheats. The theory provoked one of the most intense debates in radical economics.

1. Emmanuel and his question

Arghiri Emmanuel was born in Greece in 1911, worked as a merchant in the Belgian Congo, fought with the Greek forces in the Middle East during the Second World War, and later taught in Paris. His question was why poor countries remained poor despite decades of trade, and why the gap between rich and poor nations was so wide when the same goods, made with similar techniques, were produced in both. He rejected the explanation that poor countries simply specialized in the wrong products. Even when rich and poor countries made the same kinds of goods, he argued, trade between them was unequal because of the gap in wages.

2. Mobile capital, immobile labor

Emmanuel’s theory rests on Marx’s analysis of prices of production in volume 3 of Capital. Within a country, competition among capitals equalizes the rate of profit across industries, so goods do not sell at their labor values but at prices of production: cost of production plus the average rate of profit on the capital advanced. Industries with relatively more capital per worker sell above value, and those with less sell below value, so value is redistributed among capitals (see the History of Economic Thought course, Topic 7).

Emmanuel transferred this analysis to the world economy, with two premises:

  • Capital is internationally mobile, so rates of profit tend to equalize across countries. Ricardo’s theory of comparative advantage (Topic 3) had assumed the opposite.
  • Labor is internationally immobile, so wages do not equalize. Wages in rich countries are many times those in poor countries, a gap far larger than any gap in the rate of profit.

Under these conditions, international prices of production embody the wage gap. The high-wage country sells its goods at prices above their values, and the low-wage country sells below value. Exchange at these prices transfers value from the low-wage to the high-wage country. Emmanuel called this unequal exchange.

3. The strict and broad senses

Emmanuel distinguished two sources of non-equivalent exchange:

  • Unequal exchange in the broad sense arises from differences in the organic composition of capital (capital relative to labor) between countries, even when wages are equal. This is simply Marx’s transformation of values into prices of production, applied across borders. It happens within any country as well, and Emmanuel did not regard it as specific to international trade.
  • Unequal exchange in the strict sense arises from differences in wages, with equal organic compositions. This, Emmanuel argued, was the specifically international phenomenon, because only between nations do wages for comparable work differ so much and so persistently.

Emmanuel’s model of international prices of production

Each country employs 100 hours of labor, which creates 100 units of new value, and uses up some constant capital (machinery and materials). The value of each country’s output is its constant capital plus 100. Workers are paid a wage per hour, in units of value; the rest of the new value is surplus value. Capital moves freely, so both countries earn the same rate of profit on the capital they advance (constant capital plus wages). Compare each country’s price of production with the value of its output.

4. Wages as the independent variable

Emmanuel’s most controversial claim concerned causation. In classical and Marxian theory, the wage was usually treated as given by historical and institutional conditions, the “moral and historical element” Marx mentioned. Emmanuel took this seriously: wages are the independent variable of the system. They are set in each country by the history of class struggle, social norms, and institutions, and prices then adjust to them. It is not because their products are cheap that poor countries have low wages, he argued; it is because their wages are low that their products are cheap. Rich countries are not rich because they are productive; they became productive in part because high wages, by raising prices and incomes, created markets and induced investment.

A further implication caused bitter controversy on the left. If workers in rich countries receive wages inflated by unequal exchange, then they too share in the transfer from poor countries. Emmanuel concluded that international working-class solidarity faced a material obstacle, a conclusion that many Marxists rejected.

5. The Bettelheim debate

The French edition of Unequal Exchange was published together with a critique by the French Marxist economist Charles Bettelheim and Emmanuel’s reply. Bettelheim made several objections:

  • Wages reflect productivity. Higher wages in rich countries reflect higher productivity of labor and more developed productive forces, so they are not an independent variable. If productivity differences are taken into account, the “transfer” may disappear.
  • Exploitation happens in production. Workers in poor countries are exploited by capital in production, not by workers of rich countries through exchange. Emmanuel’s focus on exchange obscured relations of production.
  • Solidarity. Bettelheim rejected the idea that workers of rich countries exploit those of poor countries.

Emmanuel replied that, for goods produced with similar techniques, productivity differences cannot account for wage differences of ten or twenty to one, and that the question was what happens to the value created when goods are exchanged at prices that embody those wage differences.

6. Samir Amin

The Egyptian-French economist Samir Amin, in Accumulation on a World Scale (1970) and Unequal Development (1973), built unequal exchange into a broader theory of the world economy. Amin defined unequal exchange more narrowly than Emmanuel: it exists when the difference in wages between center and periphery is greater than the difference in productivity. He argued that modern export industries in the periphery, such as mines, plantations, and later factories, often have productivity close to that of the center, while wages are far lower. Unequal exchange was one mechanism of a wider process of accumulation on a world scale, in which the periphery’s economy is shaped to serve accumulation in the center. Amin’s answer was delinking (Topic 23): subordinating external relations to the requirements of self-centered national development.

7. Samuelson and other critics

Paul Samuelson, in “Illogic of Neo-Marxian Doctrine of Unequal Exchange” (1976), argued that Emmanuel’s results could be restated in ordinary price theory without reference to labor values. In a model with exogenously given wages, a country with lower wages simply has lower prices and a lower standard of living; calling this a “transfer of value” added nothing, and the poor country could still gain from trade relative to autarky. Other critics, from both Marxian and mainstream perspectives, questioned whether rates of profit really are equalized internationally, whether the labor values used to measure the transfer can be compared across countries with very different productivity, and whether the theory could explain change over time. Defenders responded that the theory’s point was not that the periphery loses compared with autarky, but that the international price system systematically distributes the gains in favor of high-wage countries.

8. Ecologically unequal exchange

From the 1980s, sociologists and anthropologists extended the idea to nature. Stephen Bunker’s study of the Amazon, Underdeveloping the Amazon (1985), argued that extractive economies export energy and matter that are lost to the region, while the industrial centers that receive them build up complexity. Alf Hornborg argued that the exchange of goods at market prices conceals an asymmetric flow of embodied land, energy, and materials from the periphery to the center, so that the technological progress of the core depends on appropriating the biophysical resources of the periphery. This ecologically unequal exchange connects the theory to ecological economics.

9. Unequal exchange and Prebisch–Singer

Unequal exchange is often confused with the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis (Topic 18), but the two are distinct:
