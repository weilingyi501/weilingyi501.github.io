Marshall, Haberler, and Opportunity Cost

When the marginal revolution replaced the labor theory of value, trade theory had to be rebuilt on new foundations. Marshall and Edgeworth turned Mill’s reciprocal demand into geometry, and Gottfried Haberler restated comparative advantage in terms of opportunity cost. This is the version of the theory taught in textbooks today.

1. The problem with labor costs

Ricardo’s theory (Topic 3) measured comparative costs in hours of labor. That worked if labor was the only input and all labor was alike. But production uses land and capital as well as labor, and labor comes in many skills and wages. After the marginal revolution of the 1870s (see the History of Economic Thought course, Topic 8), most economists no longer believed that labor time determined value. If the labor theory of value went, did comparative advantage go with it?

Two responses emerged. The British tradition of Marshall, Edgeworth, and Frank Taussig kept the classical framework but added a fuller account of demand and treated “real costs” as including the sacrifices of capital owners as well as workers. The Austrian economist Gottfried Haberler dropped real costs altogether and restated the theory in terms of opportunity cost.

2. Marshall, Edgeworth, and offer curves

Alfred Marshall worked out a diagrammatic theory of trade in the 1870s. His chapters on The Pure Theory of Foreign Trade were privately printed in 1879 and later formed appendices to Money, Credit and Commerce (1923). Marshall turned Mill’s reciprocal demand (Topic 4) into offer curves. Each country’s offer curve shows how much of its export good it is willing to give for each quantity of the import good. Where the two curves cross, both countries’ offers match, and the slope of a line from the origin to that point is the equilibrium terms of trade.

Francis Ysidro Edgeworth, in his “Theory of International Values” (1894), refined the analysis and tied it to his own work on exchange and indifference curves. Offer curves made it possible to analyze how changes in demand, growth, or tariffs shift the terms of trade. A tariff, for instance, shifts the tariff-imposing country’s offer curve inward: it offers less of its exports for any given amount of imports, and if its partner’s offer curve is not perfectly flat, its terms of trade improve.

3. Haberler’s opportunity cost

In a 1930 article and in The Theory of International Trade (1936), Haberler showed that comparative advantage does not need the labor theory of value. What matters is the opportunity cost of one good in terms of another: how much wine a country must give up to produce one more unit of cloth. This trade-off can be read from the country’s production possibility frontier, which shows the combinations of goods it can produce with all its resources, whatever those resources are. A country has a comparative advantage in cloth if its opportunity cost of cloth, before trade, is lower than its partner’s.

The restatement had a high price, as critics pointed out. Ricardo’s theory was a theory about the costs of production. Haberler’s opportunity costs are measured in other goods, and they reflect demand and prices as much as technology. Jacob Viner (1937) objected that opportunity costs could not tell us whether trade raised welfare without some measure of the real sacrifices behind production. Haberler’s version nonetheless prevailed, and it is the one taught in textbooks today.

4. Increasing costs and the gains from trade

In Ricardo’s example, costs were constant, so the production possibility frontier was a straight line and countries specialized completely. If some resources are better suited to one good than another, the frontier bows outward: each extra unit of cloth costs more wine than the last. With increasing opportunity costs, a country specializes only partly. It expands its export industry until its opportunity cost rises to the world price, and keeps producing some of the imported good.

The gains from trade then come in two parts. The gain from exchange arises because the country can trade at world prices even without changing what it produces. The gain from specialization arises because it shifts production toward the good in which it has a comparative advantage.

Increasing costs: production, consumption, and the gains from trade

A country can make at most 10 units of cloth or 10 units of wine; its frontier bows outward. Without trade, it produces and consumes at the point where its frontier is tangent to its own price line. Set the world price of cloth (in units of wine) to see where it produces, what it consumes, and how much better off it is.

5. Welfare and the optimum tariff

Marshall also introduced consumer surplus, the difference between what consumers would be willing to pay and what they actually pay. It made it possible to measure the gains from trade, and the losses from a tariff, as areas under demand and supply curves, a method that became standard in trade policy analysis (Topic 9).

The new tools gave a precise form to Torrens’s argument (Topic 4). In 1906, Charles Bickerdike showed that a country large enough to affect its terms of trade can gain from a small tariff, because the improvement in its terms of trade at first outweighs the loss from reduced trade. The optimum tariff is the tariff that maximizes this gain; it is higher the less elastic the foreign offer curve. Economists drew three caveats. The gain comes at the expense of trading partners and lowers world welfare. Small countries cannot use it. And partners can retaliate, possibly leaving everyone worse off, as in the tariff wars of the 1930s. In 1936 Abba Lerner added the symmetry theorem: a uniform tariff on imports has the same real effects as a uniform tax on exports.

6. Taussig and the test of experience

At Harvard, Frank Taussig spent decades studying the American tariff. His Tariff History of the United States (1888 and later editions) traced how tariffs had been made, and his International Trade (1927) tried to test classical theory against the evidence of prices, gold flows, and exchange rates. Taussig accepted comparative advantage but was cautious about its practical reach. He was open to the infant-industry case for some American industries in the early nineteenth century, while judging that most later tariffs had protected industries that no longer needed it. His student Jacob Viner’s Studies in the Theory of International Trade (1937) became the standard history of classical trade doctrine.

Question to think about: Does dropping the labor theory of value change what comparative advantage means, or only how it is proved?
