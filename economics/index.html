Introduction to Economics: History, Theory, and Policy

How economies came to be organized as they are, how economists explain them, and what the explanations mean for policy.

About the course

This is a first course in economics. It begins with the history of economic systems and economic ideas, and then turns to microeconomics, macroeconomics, and the world economy. On each main question it sets the standard model beside other explanations and asks students to compare them by evidence and argument.

The course follows the four parts and the chapter order of its main text, grouping fifty-six chapters into thirty-four topics. Parts I and II, and Parts III and IV, can be taught as two semesters. The text was published in 2008, so later events such as the financial crisis of 2008 enter through the discussion questions.

Three questions run through the whole course:

HistoryHow do economic systems arise and change?
DistributionWhat determines incomes, and how are they shared?
StabilityWhy do economies boom and slump, and what can policy do?

Learning goals

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Describe the main economic systems from early societies to the global economy.
  2. Use basic models, including supply and demand, costs, market structure, the multiplier, and comparative advantage.
  3. Compare how different traditions answer the same economic question.
  4. Apply these tools to public debates over inequality, employment, inflation, trade, and development.

Part I · History and Ideas
Topic 1Early EconomiesCommunal societies and ancient slavery▸
Context
For most of human history people lived in small bands that gathered, hunted, and shared. Farming and herding made a regular surplus possible, and with it private property, war captives, and the first class societies.

Core ideas

  • Four features describe any society: its technology, its economic institutions, its social institutions, and its ideology.
  • Change comes from tension among the four features.
  • A lasting surplus raises the question of who produces it and who controls it.

Topic 2Feudalism in Western EuropeLords, serfs, and the Christian paternalist ethic▸
Context
As Roman slavery declined, former slaves, free farmers, and members of the Germanic tribes became serfs bound to a manor. The medieval Church taught that rank carried obligations and that the pursuit of wealth was dangerous.

Core ideas

  • Serfs owed labor and produce; lords owed protection.
  • Technology improved only slowly, partly because those who worked gained little from improvement.
  • The Christian paternalist ethic condemned usury and unrestrained gain.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 3–4.

Question to think about: What holds an economy together when few things are bought and sold?

Topic 3From Feudalism to CapitalismEnclosure, revolution, and mercantilism in England▸
Context
Over about three centuries English peasants moved from labor services to leases, trade and towns grew, and political revolution limited the power of the crown. Mercantilist writers debated how far the state should direct trade, while a new individualism took hold.

Core ideas

  • Leases and commercial farming drew rural households into the market.
  • Protestantism and individualist philosophy gave profit seeking a moral defense.
  • The Industrial Revolution raised output and imposed heavy social costs.

Topic 4The United States, 1776–1865From farms and plantations to early industry▸
Context
In 1776 the United States was a country of independent farmers, merchants, and plantations worked by enslaved people. By 1860 the Northeast was industrializing, and the conflict over slavery led to civil war.

Core ideas

  • Several economic systems existed side by side in one country.
  • Law, land policy, and the dispossession of Native Americans shaped development.
  • Slavery was profitable for planters, and its effect on Southern growth has long been debated.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 7.

Question to think about: Can slavery be profitable and still hold back economic development?

Topic 5Classical LiberalismThe case for free markets▸
Context
Adam Smith and his successors wrote as the factory system spread in Britain. They argued that individuals pursuing their own interest in competitive markets would promote the general good, and that government should keep to a few tasks.

Core ideas

  • The invisible hand links self-interest to social benefit through competition.
  • Malthus added a theory of population that doubted the value of poor relief.
  • Government should provide defense, justice, and some public works.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 8.

Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), book I, chapters 1–3 and 7.

Question to think about: What conditions does the invisible hand need in order to work?

Topic 6Critics of Early IndustryHodgskin, Thompson, Owen, and Marx▸
Context
Industrial growth brought long hours, child labor, and insecurity. Reformers proposed cooperation and new forms of ownership, and Karl Marx developed a theory of how capitalism works and changes.

Core ideas

  • Hodgskin, Thompson, and Owen disagreed about whether competition itself was the problem.
  • Marx’s historical materialism traces social change to tension between productive forces and class relations.
  • Marx explained profit by the difference between the value of labor power and the value workers create.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 9–10.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto (1848), part I.

Question to think about: Which of these critics’ concerns have been answered since, and how?

Topic 7The Corporation and Neoclassical Economics1865–1900▸
Context
After the Civil War railroads, steel, and oil came under the control of giant firms, and antitrust law appeared. In the same decades economists rebuilt the case for markets on utility and marginal analysis.

Core ideas

  • Incorporation pooled capital and limited investors’ risk.
  • Marginal utility and marginal productivity became the basis of price and income theory.
  • Neoclassical economists differed over how much government should correct.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 11–12.

Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890), book V, chapter 3.

Question to think about: How well does a theory of many small firms describe an economy of large ones?

Topic 8Veblen and InstitutionsIndustry, business, and consumption▸
Context
Thorstein Veblen watched large corporations and their owners reshape American life. He studied the economy as a set of evolving habits and institutions.

Core ideas

  • Industry makes useful goods; business seeks profit, and the two can conflict.
  • Conspicuous consumption displays status and is imitated down the social scale.
  • Institutions change more slowly than technology.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 13.

Thorstein Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), chapter 4.

Topic 9Growth, Depression, and Keynes1900–1940▸
Context
Rapid growth, a world war, and the boom of the 1920s ended in the Great Depression. John Maynard Keynes argued that an economy could settle at high unemployment and that demand had to be managed.

Core ideas

  • Keynes rejected the claim that supply creates its own demand.
  • Aggregate demand has four parts: consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports.
  • Wartime spending ended mass unemployment in the United States.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 14–15.

John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), chapters 1–3.

Question to think about: Why did unemployment in the 1930s last so long?

Topic 10The United States in the Global Economy1940 to the present▸
Context
After 1945 the United States led the world economy. Later, rivals caught up, trade moved from surplus to deficit, and production spread across borders. The book’s account ends in 2006, just before the financial crisis.

Core ideas

  • American capitalism passed through industrial, corporate, and global stages.
  • Corporations, trade rules, and communication technology linked national economies.
  • National governments kept power but faced new limits.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 16.

Part II · Markets, Income, and Power
Topic 11Two Ways to Start EconomicsIndividuals or institutions▸
Context
The story of Robinson Crusoe is often used to introduce economics through one person’s choices. Others begin with the institutions in which people actually work and trade. This topic compares the two starting points.

Core ideas

  • One approach reasons from individual choice under scarcity.
  • The other reasons from institutions, history, and social groups.
  • Microeconomics studies parts of the economy, and macroeconomics studies the whole.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 17.

Question to think about: What does the Crusoe story leave out, and does it matter?
Topic 12Income, Wealth, and ClassMeasuring inequality▸
Context
Before theory come facts: how income and wealth are distributed in the United States, and how the distribution has changed.

Core ideas

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock, and wealth is more concentrated.
  • Quintile shares and the Gini coefficient summarize a distribution.
  • Income from work and income from property follow different paths.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 18.

Question to think about: Which measure of inequality tells you most, and what does each one hide?
Topic 13Labor, Prices, and ProfitsThe classical and Marxian view▸
Context
Smith and Ricardo linked prices to labor, and Marx used the idea to explain profit. The chapters set out that theory and the objections to it, including those of sympathetic economists.

Core ideas

  • The working day can be divided into necessary and surplus labor time.
  • Wages depend on productivity, bargaining, and social standards.
  • Later economists proposed other price theories, and the transformation problem remains debated.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 19–20.

Question to think about: Can a theory of profit stand without the labor theory of value?
Topic 14Market Power and the Large FirmMergers, concentration, and regulation▸
Context
Waves of mergers since the 1890s produced industries dominated by a few firms, and governments answered with antitrust law and regulation.

Core ideas

  • Concentration can come from scale, technology, finance, or collusion.
  • Firms with market power choose prices as well as output.
  • Antitrust and regulatory agencies have a mixed record.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 21.

Question to think about: When is a large firm efficient, and when is it merely powerful?
Topic 15DiscriminationRace, gender, and the labor market▸
Context
Gaps in pay, jobs, schooling, and housing persist between groups. This topic examines the evidence and the competing explanations.

Core ideas

  • Occupational segregation and unequal pay are measured in different ways.
  • Explanations range from individual prejudice to institutions and history.
  • Economists disagree about whether competition reduces discrimination.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 22.

Question to think about: Who gains from discrimination, and who loses?
Topic 16The EnvironmentPollution, prices, and limits▸
Context
Production uses up resources and creates waste that market prices often ignore. The topic compares proposals from tradable permits to regulation to deeper institutional change.

Core ideas

  • Pollution is a cost borne by people outside the transaction.
  • Market, regulatory, and institutional remedies rest on different assumptions.
  • The Malthusian argument about population has returned in new forms.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 23.

Question to think about: Should clean air have a price?
Topic 17Government, Democracy, and the EconomyInfluence, policy, and participation▸
Context
Economic resources affect elections, lobbying, law, and the media, and policy in turn affects the distribution of income. The topic ends with experiments in economic participation.

Core ideas

  • Taxes, education, and health policy can narrow or widen inequality.
  • Formal political rights and effective influence can differ.
  • Cooperatives and public enterprises are two forms of wider participation in economic decisions.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 24–25.

Question to think about: Where should democratic decisions end and market decisions begin?
Topic 18Scarcity, Choice, Supply, and DemandThe core market model▸
Context
The standard model begins with scarcity and opportunity cost and leads to supply and demand. The chapters present the model first and then its critics.

Core ideas

  • A production possibility curve shows opportunity cost.
  • Price changes move along a curve; other changes shift it.
  • Elasticity measures how strongly buyers and sellers respond.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 26–27.

Question to think about: What can supply and demand explain well, and what can it not?
Topic 19Consumer ChoiceUtility and demand▸
Context
Neoclassical theory derives demand from consumers who maximize utility within a budget. Critics ask where preferences come from.

Core ideas

  • Diminishing marginal utility helps explain downward-sloping demand.
  • A consumer maximizes utility when the last dollar spent on each good adds the same utility.
  • Critics argue that advertising and social setting shape preferences.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 28.

Question to think about: Are our wants our own?
Topic 20Production and CostsThe supply side of the firm▸
Context
A firm’s supply rests on its technology and its costs. The topic works from the production function to cost curves in the short and the long run.

Core ideas

  • In the short run at least one input is fixed, and returns to the others eventually diminish.
  • Marginal cost cuts average variable and average total cost at their lowest points.
  • Returns to scale shape long-run costs.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 29–30.

Question to think about: Do real firms face rising marginal costs?
Topic 21Work and WagesMarginal productivity and its critics▸
Context
In the neoclassical account each input earns the value of what it adds. The chapter sets this beside the view that wages also reflect bargaining and power.

Core ideas

  • Labor demand follows the marginal revenue product.
  • Substitution and income effects can make labor supply bend backward.
  • Human capital, working conditions, and discrimination help explain wage gaps.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 31.

Question to think about: Are people paid what they produce?
Topic 22Market StructuresFrom perfect competition to monopoly▸
Context
Four market structures organize the theory of the firm: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly.

Core ideas

  • Every firm maximizes profit where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.
  • A competitive firm takes price as given; a monopolist restricts output to raise it.
  • Oligopolists must anticipate one another, through price leadership, collusion, or rivalry.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 32–34.

Question to think about: Which market structure best describes the firms you deal with every day?
Topic 23Market FailurePublic goods, externalities, and taxes▸
Context
Even in standard theory markets can fail. The topic asks when they do and what governments can do about it.

Core ideas

  • Public goods are nonrival and nonexcludable, so free riding is a problem.
  • Taxes and subsidies can bring external costs and benefits into prices.
  • Tax systems balance efficiency, ability to pay, and benefits received.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 35.

Question to think about: Are market failures exceptions, or the normal case?
Part III · Growth and Stability
Topic 24Business Cycles and National AccountsRecording booms and busts▸
Context
Market economies have alternated between expansion and contraction since the early nineteenth century. National accounts let us measure what rises and falls.

Core ideas

  • Unemployment has private and social costs.
  • GDP can be added up from spending or from income, with the same total.
  • GDP leaves out household work and environmental damage.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 36–37.

Question to think about: What would a better measure than GDP include?
Topic 25Aggregate Supply and DemandSay, the neoclassicals, and Keynes▸
Context
Does a market economy return to full employment by itself? Say and the neoclassical economists gave three reasons to think so, and Keynes answered each one.

Core ideas

  • Say’s law holds that aggregate demand adjusts to aggregate supply.
  • The neoclassical argument runs through goods prices, wages, and interest rates.
  • Keynes replied that falling wages cut spending and that expected profit drives investment.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 38–40.

John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory (1936), chapter 18.

Question to think about: Which of Keynes’s three replies do you find strongest?
Topic 26Consumption and InvestmentWhat moves demand▸
Context
Consumption is the largest part of spending, and investment is the most volatile. The topic shows how each is measured over the cycle and what drives it.

Core ideas

  • Average and marginal propensities describe how households spend income.
  • Income distribution, debt, and wealth affect total consumption.
  • Investment follows profits with a lag.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 41–43.

Question to think about: How does rising household debt change the link between wages and spending?
Topic 27The Multiplier and Theories of the CycleWhy expansions end▸
Context
A change in spending sets off further rounds of spending. Economists disagree about whether downturns come from outside shocks or from within the expansion itself.

Core ideas

  • The simple multiplier equals 1/(1 − MPC).
  • The accelerator ties investment to changes in demand.
  • Underconsumption, wage-cost, and shock theories explain downturns differently.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 44–45.

Question to think about: Which theory fits the recession of 2008, and which fits 2020?
Topic 28Growth and Its CostsSources, sharing, and waste▸
Context
Growth depends on saving, investment, and productivity, and also on whether there is enough demand. This topic asks how growth is shared and what it costs.

Core ideas

  • The classical formula links growth to saving and the productivity of investment.
  • U.S. growth slowed after about 1970 while income grew more unequal.
  • Some growth reflects spending that adds little to well-being.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 46.

Question to think about: Is faster growth always better?
Topic 29Fiscal PolicySpending, taxes, and deficits▸
Context
Government spending and taxes affect demand, sometimes automatically and sometimes by choice. The debate concerns whether they should be used to stabilize the economy.

Core ideas

  • Automatic stabilizers raise the deficit in recessions and reduce it in expansions.
  • Critics point to crowding out, lags, and politics.
  • Wars and military budgets have been a major part of U.S. fiscal history.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 47–48.

Question to think about: Should the government balance its budget every year?
Topic 30Money, Banking, and CreditHow banks create money▸
Context
Money evolved from barter to coin, paper, credit, and electronic entries. Banks create money when they lend, and credit can amplify booms and busts.

Core ideas

  • Money serves as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value.
  • New loans create new deposits.
  • Credit rises in expansions and falls in recessions.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 49.

Question to think about: If banks create money, what limits how much they create?
Topic 31Inflation and Monetary PolicyPrices and the Federal Reserve▸
Context
Inflation has come from strong demand, rising costs, and wars. Central banks answer mainly through interest rates, and economists disagree about how well this works. The book predates quantitative easing and the inflation of 2021–23.

Core ideas

  • Demand-pull and cost-push inflation call for different remedies.
  • Stagflation combined rising prices with high unemployment.
  • The Fed has used reserve requirements, open market operations, and the discount rate.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 50–51.

Question to think about: Who gains and who loses when a central bank fights inflation?
Part IV · The World Economy
Topic 32Trade, Finance, and World CyclesHow instability travels▸
Context
Trade, investment, and finance link national cycles, so that booms and recessions pass from one country to another.

Core ideas

  • Imports rise and fall with national income.
  • A trade deficit reduces demand for domestic products.
  • Foreign investment and lending can speed growth and spread crises.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 52–53.

Question to think about: How did trouble in U.S. housing become a world crisis in 2008?
Topic 33Globalization and Free TradeTwo long debates▸
Context
Comparative advantage is the classic case for free trade, and infant industry is the classic reply. The globalization debate adds labor, environment, and distribution.

Core ideas

  • Comparative advantage depends on relative, not absolute, cost.
  • Defenders of infant industries favor temporary protection; Prebisch and Singer questioned who gains from specialization.
  • Globalization has brought growth and innovation, and also dislocation.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapters 54–55.

David Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), chapter 7.

Question to think about: Is there a fair way to share the gains and losses from trade?
Topic 34DevelopmentWhy some countries are poorer▸
Context
Income gaps between countries remain large. This topic compares explanations based on population, government, and markets with those based on colonial history and the use of the surplus.

Core ideas

  • Development means more than growth in income per person.
  • Colonial trade patterns left lasting effects.
  • The concept of the surplus asks who controls what a country produces beyond basic needs, and how it is used.

Readings

Sherman et al., chapter 56.

Question to think about: Which obstacles to development are internal, and which come from the world economy?
