Growth, Depression, and Keynes

Rapid growth, a world war, and the boom of the 1920s ended in the Great Depression. John Maynard Keynes argued that an economy could settle at high unemployment and that demand had to be managed.

In this topic

  3. Keynes against Say’s law
  4. Spending and income

1. The crash

The prosperity of the 1920s ended with the stock market fall of October 1929. Between 1929 and 1932, according to the text, more than 85,000 businesses failed, over 5,000 banks suspended operations, and the value of shares on the New York exchange fell from 87 to 19 billion dollars. About one worker in four was unemployed, and there was no unemployment insurance and no federal deposit insurance.

2. The New Deal

The programs of the 1930s created Social Security and unemployment insurance, set minimum wages and maximum hours, protected union elections, and regulated banks and securities. The text calls this the move from unregulated capitalism to a mixed economy, and notes that the New Deal did not by itself end the Depression.

3. Keynes against Say’s law

Say’s law holds that producing goods creates the income to buy them, so a general shortage of demand cannot last. Keynes answered in 1936 that a modern economy uses money, and money can be held. When the outlook for profit is poor, firms and households have good reason to hold savings back, so planned saving can exceed investment.

4. Spending and income

Keynes sorted spending into consumption, investment, government purchases, and exports, set against leakages into saving, taxes, and imports. Consumption rises with income, but usually by less. If investment is too small to fill the gap at full employment, output falls until income is low enough that saving equals investment. If spending is too large, prices rise.

5. Policy

Keynes preferred useful public spending such as schools and hospitals, and made his point with a joke: even paying people to dig up buried banknotes would beat doing nothing. Tax cuts and low interest rates could help too. War spending ended mass unemployment, and for about a quarter century some form of Keynesian policy was standard.

Note: Economists still debate why the Depression was so deep, including the roles of bank failures, the gold standard, and monetary policy. The text emphasizes demand and profit.

Question to think about: Why did unemployment in the 1930s last so long?

6. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapters 14–15.

Companion reading

John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), chapters 1–3.
