Early Economies

For most of human history people lived in small bands that gathered, hunted, and shared. Farming and herding made a regular surplus possible, and with it private property, war captives, and the first class societies.

1. How to describe a society

The book studies every society through four features: its technology, its economic institutions, its social institutions, and its ideology. Technology is more than machines: it includes skills, knowledge, land, and the labor force. Economic institutions are relations among people, not things. Each feature shapes the other three, so six pairs of interactions can be traced in any society.

2. Communal societies

For most of human history people lived in small bands, perhaps five to thirty-five people, who hunted, gathered, and shared what they found. With stone, bone, and wooden tools a person could produce little more than he or she consumed, so there was no market, almost no private wealth, and no separate government. The same poverty explains the stability of these societies. A band just above starvation cannot risk experiments, and a captive who can only feed himself is of no use as a slave.

3. The Neolithic revolution and the surplus

Beginning around 10,000 B.C.E. in the Middle East, and separately in China and the Americas, people developed better stone tools, farming and herding, and pottery. Women probably led the change from gathering to farming, and men the change from hunting to herding. Productivity rose, and for the first time there was a regular surplus above subsistence. The surplus fed specialists such as toolmakers, potters, priests, and soldiers, and their skills raised productivity again.

4. From equality to slavery

Once there was wealth to seize, war became profitable, and a prisoner who could produce a surplus could be kept as a slave. Chiefs became hereditary rulers and then kings, and ideas changed with institutions: priests called rulers divine, and Aristotle argued that slavery was natural. In Greece and Rome the ancient slave economy built cities and monuments, while free farmers were drafted into armies, taxed, and undersold by plantations.

Note: The book’s picture of early bands as equal and peaceful is one view. Archaeologists and anthropologists disagree about how equal and how peaceful they were, and about whether farming improved ordinary lives.

Question to think about: Why might a richer society be a less equal one?
