Money, Banking, and Credit

Money evolved from barter to coin, paper, credit, and electronic entries. Banks create money when they lend, and credit can amplify booms and busts.

In this topic

  3. The money multiplier
  4. Balance sheets and panics
  5. Credit and the cycle
  6. Readings

1. What money does

Money is a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. The text tells its history in stages: barter, goods used as money, metal coins, paper claims on metal, paper not convertible into metal, and bank deposits.

2. From goldsmith to bank

Goldsmiths stored coin and gave receipts, which began to pass as payment. Since few holders asked for coin on the same day, a goldsmith could lend by issuing more receipts than there was coin in the vault. Lending had created money. Modern banks do the same when they credit a borrower’s account.

3. The money multiplier

In the textbook account a bank keeps a required fraction of deposits as reserves and lends the rest, which is spent and deposited again. With a reserve ratio of 20 percent, a new deposit of 1,000 dollars can support deposits of 5,000. Banks that hold extra reserves, and people who hold cash, make the figure smaller.

4. Balance sheets and panics

A bank’s assets are reserves, securities, and loans, and its liabilities are mostly deposits. It earns by lending long and borrowing short, so it trades profit against liquidity. If depositors all ask for cash at once, a sound bank can fail, and failures spread. Panics were frequent in the nineteenth century; deposit insurance, begun in the 1930s, greatly reduced runs by small depositors.

5. Credit and the cycle

Borrowing grows in expansions as confidence rises, and debts grow faster than incomes. Interest rates rise with demand for loans, usually with a lag. When incomes fall, debts cannot be met, lenders withdraw, and a small downturn becomes a large one.

Note: Many economists and central banks now describe the order differently: banks lend first, creating deposits, and find reserves afterward, limited by profitability, capital, and the interest rate the central bank sets. The United States reduced reserve requirements to zero in March 2020.

Question to think about: If banks create money, what limits how much they create?
