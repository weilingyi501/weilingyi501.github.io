Government, Democracy, and the Economy

Economic resources affect elections, lobbying, law, and the media, and policy in turn affects the distribution of income. The topic ends with experiments in economic participation.

In this topic
  1. How resources become influence
  2. Taxes
  3. Spending, schools, and law
  4. Economic participation
  5. Readings

1. How resources become influence

The text asks how unequal wealth can shape policy where everyone has one vote. It points to turnout, which rises with income; campaign money; lobbying; ownership of the media; and the movement of people between business and office. It adds that control is far from total: business interests conflict, and organized voters sometimes prevail.

2. Taxes

A tax is progressive if its share of income rises with income, proportional if it stays the same, and regressive if it falls. The U.S. income tax is progressive in its rates, less so after deductions. Sales taxes are regressive, and so is a payroll tax that stops at an income ceiling.

3. Spending, schools, and law

Social Security and Medicare sharply reduced poverty among the elderly. The text judges other welfare spending too small to change the overall distribution and argues that schooling, though it raises individual incomes, also passes advantage from parents to children. It discusses differences in legal representation and the cost and coverage of health care.

4. Economic participation

The text distinguishes formal democracy, meaning rules that allow everyone to vote and speak, from effective democracy, in which influence is fairly equal. It then describes forms of participation in economic life: consumer and producer cooperatives, credit unions, employee stock ownership, and public enterprises such as city utilities, the Bank of North Dakota, and the more than 900 rural electric cooperatives. It also asks hard questions about them, such as financing, management, and whether unions are still needed.

Note: The chapter predates the Affordable Care Act and recent changes in campaign finance and tax law. Estimates of how far taxes and transfers reduce U.S. inequality vary with the method; most find a substantial reduction once transfers are counted.

Question to think about: Where should democratic decisions end and market decisions begin?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapters 24–25.

Companion reading

Robert Dahl, A Preface to Economic Democracy (University of California Press, 1985).
