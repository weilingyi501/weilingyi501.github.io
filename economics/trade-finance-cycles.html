Trade, Finance, and World Cycles

Trade, investment, and finance link national cycles, so that booms and recessions pass from one country to another.

In this topic

1. Exports, imports, and demand

Exports add to the demand for a country’s products and imports direct its spending abroad. Net exports are the difference: positive in a trade surplus, negative in a trade deficit. The United States ran surpluses in most years from the 1870s to the early 1970s and has run a deficit in goods and services every year since 1976. It reached 5.7 percent of GDP in 2005 and 2006 and was 3.0 percent in 2024.

2. Trade over the cycle

Imports follow home income, so they rise in expansions and fall in recessions. Exports follow income abroad, which moves with the home economy only loosely. In the U.S. data the text uses, the deficit therefore widens in expansions, draining some demand, and narrows in recessions, giving some support.

Raw materials add a pressure on costs. Mines, wells, and crops cannot expand as quickly as factories, so their prices tend to rise faster than those of finished goods late in a boom and to fall faster in a slump. Profit margins of manufacturers are squeezed and then relieved.

3. Three channels

The text names three ways in which booms and slumps cross borders. Through trade, lower income in one country means lower exports for others, and the effect returns in part. Through foreign investment, firms with lower profits at home and poorer prospects abroad cut spending in both places. Through finance, loans are called in, banks that share losses lend less, and stock markets fall together. On February 27, 2007, Shanghai shares fell almost 9 percent and the main U.S. indexes more than 3 percent.

4. Large and small countries

Size and specialization decide who feels the shock. A country that sells one crop or metal to one large buyer loses both volume and price when that buyer slows. After the Second World War the United States produced, by common estimates, about half of the world’s manufactures, and it was little affected by others. Trade has since become a larger share of its economy, so it is more exposed than it was.

National policy is limited too. When France tried to expand demand alone in 1981–82, part of the new spending went to imports, the deficit widened, and the government changed course.

5. Money across borders

The Bretton Woods agreement of 1944 made the dollar the main reserve currency. After the system ended in the early 1970s, controls on capital were relaxed and funds moved more freely. Such flows can finance growth, and they can also reverse: in 1997 the crisis that began in Thailand spread to its neighbors. Economists still dispute the causes, including weak banks, fixed exchange rates, short-term debt, and panic. The text stresses pressure to repay and the conditions set by the International Monetary Fund.

Note: The account ends in 2007. The financial crisis that followed passed through all three channels, and the volume of world merchandise trade fell by about 12 percent in 2009. A trade deficit also reflects national saving and investment and is matched by capital inflows, so many economists do not see it only as lost demand.

Question to think about: How did trouble in U.S. housing become a world crisis in 2008?
