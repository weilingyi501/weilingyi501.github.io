Discrimination

Gaps in pay, jobs, schooling, and housing persist between groups. This topic examines the evidence and the competing explanations.

In this topic
  1. Terms
  2. Evidence
  3. Does competition end discrimination?
  4. Crowding
  5. Readings

1. Terms

A stereotype is an oversimplified image of a group, prejudice a dislike based on it, and discrimination adverse treatment of a person because of group membership and not individual productivity. Ideologies such as racism and sexism claim that one group is naturally superior.

2. Evidence

In the text’s data for 2005 median household income was $50,784 for non-Hispanic white households, $35,967 for Hispanic, $30,954 for Black, and $61,048 for Asian households. Gaps in wealth were much larger. Women working full time all year earned about 60 percent as much as men through the 1960s and 1970s, and more later. Gaps alone do not measure discrimination: schooling, experience, occupation, and family roles differ too, and may themselves reflect earlier discrimination.

3. Does competition end discrimination?

Milton Friedman argued that an employer who refuses to hire capable people at a lower wage pays for the preference, so competition should drive such firms out. Others reply that with market power the cost is small and discrimination can last. The text’s own view is that divided workers bargain less effectively, so employers as a group may gain while workers of every group lose; Michael Reich’s work on racial inequality develops this claim.

4. Crowding

If a group is kept out of some occupations, its members crowd into the others, and supply there lowers pay. Barbara Bergmann and others used this idea to explain low wages in occupations where women predominate.

Note: Claudia Goldin and others have since given detailed evidence on gender gaps, including the role of inflexible hours, and field experiments have measured hiring discrimination directly.

Question to think about: Who gains from discrimination, and who loses?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 22.

Companion reading

Milton Friedman, Capitalism and Freedom (University of Chicago Press, 1962), chapter 7.

Michael Reich, Racial Inequality: A Political-Economic Analysis (Princeton University Press, 1981).
