The Corporation and Neoclassical Economics

After the Civil War railroads, steel, and oil came under the control of giant firms, and antitrust law appeared. In the same decades economists rebuilt the case for markets on utility and marginal analysis.

1. The rise of the giant firm

After 1865 the United States industrialized on a continental scale. Standard Oil used its volume to win railroad rebates, even on rivals’ shipments, and by 1879 controlled over 90 percent of the country’s refining. After years of price wars, firms turned to trusts and mergers. U.S. Steel, formed in 1901 by merging ten companies, controlled about two-thirds of the country’s steel output.

2. Regulation and labor

Railroad rates led to the Interstate Commerce Act (1887), and public anger at monopolies led to the Sherman Act (1890). The text argues that courts at first applied antitrust law more readily to unions than to firms, as in the rail strike of 1894, and that regulators often came to share the views of the industries they supervised. In the South, sharecropping left most freed people without land, in debt, and with little reason or means to invest.

3. The marginal revolution

Jevons and Menger (1871) and Walras (1874) rebuilt the theory of value on utility. A consumer with a limited income does best when the last dollar spent on each good brings the same extra satisfaction. Firms hire each input until the last dollar spent on each adds the same output. Alfred Marshall (1890) joined demand and supply in one framework that dominated teaching for decades.

4. What the theory claimed

With many small buyers and sellers, these choices lead to an efficient allocation, given the distribution of wealth. J. B. Clark went further and argued that each factor is paid what it adds to output. Critics answered that owning a productive factor is not the same as being productive. Neoclassical economists themselves listed exceptions: market power, public goods, social costs such as pollution, depressions, and great inequality. Liberals and conservatives still divide over how serious these are.

5. Other defenses of the new order

Social Darwinists such as Herbert Spencer treated competition as natural selection and opposed aid to the poor; the theory differs from neoclassical economics, though businessmen mixed the two. Carnegie called the rich trustees for the community. Simon Patten hoped that cooperation among large firms would replace wasteful competition, and through students such as Rexford Tugwell and Frances Perkins his ideas reached the early New Deal.

Question to think about: How well does a theory of many small firms describe an economy of large ones?

6. Readings

Companion reading

Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890), book V, chapter 3.

Herbert Spencer, The Man versus the State (1884).
