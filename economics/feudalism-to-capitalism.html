From Feudalism to Capitalism

Over about three centuries English peasants moved from labor services to leases, trade and towns grew, and political revolution limited the power of the crown. Mercantilist writers debated how far the state should direct trade, while a new individualism took hold.

1. Why change was slow

Feudal peasants and lords both farmed for their own use. A peasant who planted for an uncertain market risked starvation, and a lord who wanted more revenue could press for more services or take new land. Neither faced competition, so neither had to innovate. Long-distance merchants and towns grew inside feudalism and at first served the lords.

2. The Black Death and the lease

The plague of the fourteenth century made labor scarce. English lords tried to squeeze more from the peasants who remained and failed, because peasants resisted and lords competed for tenants. Peasants won personal freedom but not secure land. Lords enclosed the commons and turned customary holdings into leases. To pay a money rent, tenants had to sell crops and meet the market price, so they specialized, improved their methods, and bought goods they had once made. This is the explanation associated with Robert Brenner, and it is the one the book follows.

3. Political revolution and overseas empire

In the seventeenth century commercial farmers, merchants, and craftspeople fought the crown over taxes, monopolies, and religion. The civil wars of the 1640s and the Glorious Revolution of 1688 left a state friendlier to property and trade. Piracy, the slave trade, and colonies brought wealth to England. The book notes that Spain received even more treasure without industrializing, so the treasure alone cannot explain the outcome.

4. Mercantilism

Early mercantilists, the bullionists, wanted to keep gold and silver at home. Later writers aimed at a favorable balance of trade, supported by chartered monopolies, colonies, export subsidies, import bans, and the Navigation Acts. The state also regulated wages and work (the Statute of Artificers, 1563) and relieved the poor (the Poor Law of 1601), taking over duties once left to the Church. Employment was a constant concern.

5. A new individualism

Hobbes traced all motives to self-interest. Protestant teaching made private conscience the judge of conduct and praised hard work and thrift. Dudley North argued that merchants who sought special laws served themselves, and Bernard Mandeville claimed in The Fable of the Bees (1714) that private vices could produce public benefits. The ideas that Adam Smith would later systematize were already in circulation.

Question to think about: Which came first in the change to capitalism: new technology, new institutions, or new ideas?
