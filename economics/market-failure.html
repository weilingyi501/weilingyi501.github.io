Market Failure

Even in standard theory markets can fail. The topic asks when they do and what governments can do about it.

In this topic
  1. When markets fall short
  2. Public goods
  3. Externalities
  4. Principles of taxation
  5. Exception or rule?
  6. Readings

1. When markets fall short

In the standard theory, markets are efficient only under strict conditions. They can fail because of market power, poor information, economic fluctuations, public goods, and externalities.

2. Public goods

A public good is nonexcludable and nonrival: no one can be kept from it, and one person’s use leaves as much for others. National defense is the usual example. Because people can benefit without paying, markets provide too little, and the amount is decided through politics.

3. Externalities

Pollution is a cost that the price leaves out, so too much is produced. A. C. Pigou proposed a tax equal to the external cost. Education and research bring benefits to others, so too little is produced without a subsidy. The efficient amount of pollution is rarely zero, since the last units are costly to remove, and measuring external costs is hard.

4. Principles of taxation

An efficient tax changes behavior little and is cheap to collect. Two ideas of fairness compete: ability to pay and benefits received. Efficient taxes, such as a uniform head tax, are often the least fair by the first standard. Some taxes are earmarked, as gasoline taxes are for roads.

5. Exception or rule?

For many neoclassical economists failures are limited and can be repaired. The text’s progressive view is that pollution, market power, and neglected public goods run through the whole economy, and that governments open to private influence will not fully correct them. A third view, public choice theory, warns that governments fail too.

Question to think about: Are market failures exceptions, or the normal case?

6. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 35.

Companion reading

A. C. Pigou, The Economics of Welfare (1920), part II, chapter 9.

Ronald Coase, “The Problem of Social Cost,” Journal of Law and Economics 3 (1960).
