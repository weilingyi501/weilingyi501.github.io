The Multiplier and Theories of the Cycle

A change in spending sets off further rounds of spending. Economists disagree about whether downturns come from outside shocks or from within the expansion itself.

In this topic
  1. The multiplier
  2. The accelerator
  3. The two together
  4. Shocks from outside
  5. Forces from inside
  6. Readings

1. The multiplier

When the government hires a teacher, the teacher spends part of the pay, the seller spends part of that, and so on. If each person spends 80 percent of any extra income, 1,000 dollars of new spending raises income by 5,000 in the end: the multiplier is 1/(1 − MPC). New investment and new exports work in the same way. In practice the figure is smaller. Taxes, imports, and delays drain each round, and taxes raised to pay for the spending offset much of it.

2. The accelerator

Firms build capacity to meet growth in demand. If three dollars of capital are needed for each dollar of yearly output, demand must keep rising for net investment to stay positive; merely slowing the growth of sales makes investment fall. The accelerator helps explain why investment is so unstable. It works weakly when capacity is idle, and it ignores costs and finance.

3. The two together

Consumption drives investment and investment drives consumption, so an expansion feeds itself and a contraction does too. Paul Samuelson showed in 1939 that the two can produce smooth adjustment, cycles that die out, or cycles that grow, depending on their size.

4. Shocks from outside

For many neoclassical economists the economy returns quickly to equilibrium, so downturns must come from outside. Lucas stressed unexpected monetary and fiscal policy. Real business cycle theory stresses changes in technology and in prices such as that of oil, and holds that fluctuations can be efficient responses to them.

5. Forces from inside

The text’s own account centers on profit. Early in an expansion, sales rise while wages, interest, and materials are still cheap. Later, consumption lags because the labor share has fallen, while raw materials and interest grow dearer. Profit is squeezed from both sides, investment turns down, and a contraction feeds on itself until costs have fallen enough to restore profit. Underconsumption theories stress the demand side. Overinvestment theories stress costs, in some versions wages late in the boom.

Note: The crisis of 2008 brought new attention to a third line of thought, Hyman Minsky’s, in which stable times encourage risky borrowing. The 2020 recession is the clearest recent case of an outside shock.

Question to think about: Which theory fits the recession of 2008, and which fits 2020?

6. Readings

Core reading

Companion reading

Hyman P. Minsky, “The Financial Instability Hypothesis” (Levy Economics Institute Working Paper 74, 1992).
