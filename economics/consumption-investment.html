Consumption and Investment

Consumption is the largest part of spending, and investment is the most volatile. The topic shows how each is measured over the cycle and what drives it.

In this topic
  1. Measuring the cycle
  2. The consumption function
  3. Distribution and debt
  4. Investment
  5. Readings

1. Measuring the cycle

Mitchell dated peaks and troughs and divided each cycle into nine stages, from an initial trough (stage 1) through the peak (5) to the final trough (9). A cycle relative expresses each stage as a percentage of the cycle’s average, so that different cycles can be compared. Unemployment falls in expansions and rises in contractions, and the use of capacity does the opposite.

2. The consumption function

Keynes observed that consumption rises with income, but less. The average propensity to consume is consumption divided by income, and the marginal propensity is the share of an extra dollar that is spent. Poorer households spend all they receive or more, and rich households save a good part. Custom, advertising, and what others spend shape the desire to consume.

3. Distribution and debt

Because spending habits differ, distribution matters. If income shifts from wages to property, total consumption out of a given income tends to fall. The text reports that the labor share falls for most of an average expansion and rises for most of a contraction, and that the propensity to consume follows it. It also argues that rising household debt held up consumption after 1970 while wages grew slowly. Debt supports an expansion and deepens the contraction that follows. Wealth matters too, mainly for those who have it.

4. Investment

Gross investment includes replacement of worn-out capital; net investment adds to the stock. Investment swings far more than consumption. Firms need funds, from retained profit, loans, or new shares, and they need an expected profit. Buying existing land or shares is not investment in this sense, since nothing new is produced. In the text’s averages profits turn first and investment follows some quarters later, because information, planning, finance, and building take time. Moods swing with profits and then amplify the movement.

Note: Not all the evidence points one way. Surveys of investment find large roles for sales growth and credit conditions as well as profit, and the measured labor share depends on how the income of the self-employed is treated.

Question to think about: How does rising household debt change the link between wages and spending?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapters 41–43.

Companion reading

John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory (1936), chapters 8 and 12.

Wesley C. Mitchell, Business Cycles (University of California Press, 1913).
