Growth and Its Costs

Growth depends on saving, investment, and productivity, and also on whether there is enough demand. This topic asks how growth is shared and what it costs.

In this topic
  1. A simple formula
  2. Does saving become investment?
  3. The U.S. record
  4. Growth of what?
  5. Population
  6. Readings

1. A simple formula

If a tenth of output is saved and invested, and each 100 dollars of capital yields 30 dollars of output a year, output grows by 3 percent. In general the growth rate is the saving ratio times output per unit of capital. Saving and better technology raise growth.

2. Does saving become investment?

Here the two traditions part. One assumes that saving is invested and favors policies that raise saving and research, often through lower taxes on high incomes and business. The other follows Keynes: when buyers are few, more saving means less demand, and funds lie idle. Whether more saving, consumption, or military spending helps depends on whether the economy is at full employment.

3. The U.S. record

The table uses current Bureau of Economic Analysis data. The text explains the postwar boom by the American lead after the war, saved-up demand, strong unions, and high public spending, and the slowdown by foreign competition, trade deficits, debt, market power, and weaker unions. It emphasizes that after the early 1970s wages grew much more slowly than productivity.

4. Growth of what?

Some output adds little to well-being. The authors list much military production, most advertising, yearly style changes, planned obsolescence, and pollution. Others would draw the line elsewhere: advertising informs, variety has value, and defense protects.

5. Population

Malthus expected diminishing returns on limited land. Better technology, more capital per worker, and the fall in birth rates that has come with rising incomes and education have so far worked the other way.

Note: The formula here is close to the Harrod–Domar model. Robert Solow’s model (1956) lets capital and labor substitute for each other and traces most long-run growth in output per worker to technical change, which later work tries to explain.

Question to think about: Is faster growth always better?
