The Environment

Production uses up resources and creates waste that market prices often ignore. The topic compares proposals from tradable permits to regulation to deeper institutional change.

In this topic
  1. The problem
  2. Four kinds of answers
  3. Taxes and permits
  4. Growth and population
  5. Readings

1. The problem

Pollution includes solid waste, organic waste in water, local air pollution, greenhouse gases, persistent poisons, and radiation. Polluters do not bear most of these costs, so a firm that watches only its own costs will pollute more than is good for society.

2. Four kinds of answers

One group proposes to create property rights so that owners have a reason to protect resources; the “tragedy of the commons” describes what happens without them. A second favors taxes on pollution. A third favors direct regulation, since clean air cannot be divided and sold to some people and not others. The text’s progressive writers hold that the drive for profit and growth is the source of the problem and that deeper institutional change is needed.

3. Taxes and permits

A carbon tax sets a price for each unit of emissions. Cap and trade sets a total and lets firms trade permits. Either way, firms that can cut pollution cheaply cut more. That costs less in total than requiring every firm to make the same cut.

4. Growth and population

Malthus’s fear returned in arguments for zero growth. The text answers that technology and lower birth rates have eased the pressure, that different kinds of output pollute very differently, and that stopping growth without redistribution would fix present inequalities, within countries and between them.

Note: Planned economies polluted heavily too, which complicates explanations based on profit alone. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities have often managed common resources without either private owners or the state.

Question to think about: Should clean air have a price?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 23.

Companion reading

Garrett Hardin, “The Tragedy of the Commons,” Science 162 (1968).

Elinor Ostrom, Governing the Commons (Cambridge University Press, 1990), chapter 1.
