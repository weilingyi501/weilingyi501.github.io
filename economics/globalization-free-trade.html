Globalization and Free Trade

Comparative advantage is the classic case for free trade, and infant industry is the classic reply. The globalization debate adds labor, environment, and distribution.

1. Comparative advantage

In the early nineteenth century David Ricardo argued that trade benefits both sides even when one of them is better at producing everything. What matters is comparative advantage, the lower opportunity cost. In the text’s example an hour of labor makes 3 units of wine or 4 of cheese in the United States, and 1 of wine or 2 of cheese in Britain. A unit of wine costs 1.33 units of cheese in the first and 2 in the second, so the United States should make wine and Britain cheese.

The terms of trade, how much of one good exchanges for the other, must lie between the two opportunity costs, and their position divides the gain. The model assumes full employment, constant costs, competition, and given techniques.

2. Who gains within a country

A country can gain as a whole while some of its people lose, such as the dairy farmers in the example. The Heckscher–Ohlin model adds capital and land to labor: countries export goods that use their abundant factors, and the owners of scarce factors tend to lose. Leontief (1953) found that U.S. exports used less capital per worker than goods competing with imports, a puzzle often explained by skills and technology. Trade among similar countries in similar goods, scale economies, and the product cycle led to further theories.

3. The case for protection

Comparative advantage takes present costs as given, and costs change with experience. In the infant industry argument, made by Alexander Hamilton and Friedrich List, a new industry may need a tariff until it can compete. The northern United States and Germany protected their industries in the nineteenth century. In the American debate before the Civil War, manufacturers in the North favored tariffs and cotton exporters in the South opposed them. Critics reply that protection is costly, that governments may choose badly, and that temporary tariffs often stay.

Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer (both 1950) argued that the prices of primary products tend to fall relative to those of manufactures, so that specializing in them brings diminishing returns. Estimates differ by period, product, and price index; a well-known one by Grilli and Yang (1988) found a fall of about 0.6 percent a year for 1900–1986, while other studies find shifts at particular dates or no clear trend. The text contrasts South Korea, which supported selected industries, mainly from the 1960s, with Chile, which opened its markets in the 1970s and still relies heavily on copper. The comparison is debated, since Korea also stressed exports and schooling, and Chile later grew faster than most of Latin America.

4. The debate on globalization

Globalization is the closer linking of economies through trade, investment, finance, and communication. Its defenders, in the text’s account, argue that free movement of goods and capital, with few regulations, sends resources where they are most productive, spreads innovations such as the Internet, and lowers prices. Its critics argue that sudden capital flows cause instability, that governments compete by cutting taxes and labor protection, and that safety and environmental rules serve needs the market neglects. The authors favor the critics and ask who controls the process and who gains.

5. What the record shows

The two sides often cite different measures. Total world output can grow steadily while output per person slows. Growth per person did slow after 1970. The text reads the data to 2002 as a continuing decline; current World Bank data show a partial recovery after 1990, led by Asia.

The text also reports an estimate that the richest 1 percent of adults held about 40 percent of household wealth in 2000, and points to outsourcing, pollution, and falling trust. On climate, the United States signed the Kyoto Protocol in 1998 but never ratified it, and China passed it as the largest emitter of carbon dioxide in the mid-2000s.

6. Trade rules

The World Trade Organization, founded in 1995, sets rules meant to lower barriers. Poorer countries objected that rich ones asked for open markets in industry while subsidizing their own farmers. Unions and environmental groups, who protested at the Seattle meeting in 1999, wanted labor and environmental standards. Supporters of such standards call this fair trade; some poorer countries fear it could become protection under another name.

Note: Research since the text was written has measured the local costs of import competition more closely, and tariffs and industrial policy have returned to the center of policy in the United States, Europe, and China.

Question to think about: Is there a fair way to share the gains and losses from trade?
