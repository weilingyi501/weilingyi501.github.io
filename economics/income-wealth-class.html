Income, Wealth, and Class

Before theory come facts: how income and wealth are distributed in the United States, and how the distribution has changed.

In this topic
  1. Income
  2. The Gini coefficient
  3. Wealth and poverty
  4. Class as a relationship
  5. Readings

1. Income

Income is a flow received over a period: wages and salaries, profits, interest, rent, and government transfers. In the text’s figures for 2006, employee compensation made up about 69 percent of personal income. Ranked by income, the top fifth of U.S. households received 50.4 percent of household income in 2005, up from 43.6 percent in 1975, while the bottom fifth received 3.4 percent.

2. The Gini coefficient

The Gini coefficient runs from 0, when everyone has the same, to 1, when one person has everything. Around 2000 the World Bank figures quoted in the text ranged from about 0.25 in Denmark and Sweden to 0.41 in the United States and about 0.6 in Bolivia. Surveys, years, and definitions differ, so comparisons need care.

3. Wealth and poverty

Wealth is a stock: assets minus debts. It is far more concentrated than income. Median net worth in 2000 was $7,396 in the lowest income fifth and $185,500 in the highest, and much of the difference lies outside the home. The official poverty rate was 12.6 percent in 2005, and some of the poor worked full time all year. The text also reports falling real weekly wages between 1973 and 1998 while output grew.

4. Class as a relationship

“Middle class” usually refers to an income range. The text instead defines class by how people get their income and how that relates them to others: employees live on wages and salaries, and owners on profit. A well-paid athlete is an employee by this definition. Managers, professionals, and small proprietors fall in between, and many employees own some stock through pensions.

Note: The figures are the text’s and end around 2005. The slow growth of wages looks different when benefits, household size, and other price indexes are used, and economists disagree about how much.

Question to think about: Which measure of inequality tells you most, and what does each one hide?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 18.

Companion reading
