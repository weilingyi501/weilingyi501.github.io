Business Cycles and National Accounts

Market economies have alternated between expansion and contraction since the early nineteenth century. National accounts let us measure what rises and falls.

In this topic
  1. A history of booms and busts
  2. Counting the unemployed
  3. The costs
  4. The national accounts
  5. What GDP leaves out
  6. Readings

1. A history of booms and busts

Earlier societies suffered from war, drought, and epidemic. The regular swing of output and employment came with market economies: the text dates the modern cycle from 1793 in England, 1847 in France, and 1857 in Germany, and by the end of the nineteenth century downturns were international. In the United States the depression of the 1890s left between about 12 and 18 percent of the labor force unemployed at its worst, depending on the estimate, and that of the 1930s about a quarter.

2. Counting the unemployed

The official rate counts people without any work who looked for a job recently. Someone with an hour’s work a week is employed, and a person too discouraged to look is out of the labor force. Many more people are unemployed at some time in a year than in any one month. Broader measures that add discouraged and involuntary part-time workers are always higher.

3. The costs

A recession costs society the goods not made, the investment not undertaken, and the skills not used. Businesses fail. For people out of work the costs go beyond income: studies link rising unemployment to illness, divorce, and suicide.

4. The national accounts

Gross domestic product is the value of final goods and services produced in a country in a year. Added up by spending, it is C + I + G + NX. Added up by income, it equals what is paid to labor and property, with adjustments for depreciation and taxes on business. Disposable income is what households can spend or save after taxes and transfers. In the first figures published for 2005, consumption was close to 70 percent of GDP and personal saving was slightly negative. Such figures are revised for years: the saving rate for 2005 now stands at 2.2 percent of disposable income.

5. What GDP leaves out

GDP omits housework, which the text puts at about a quarter of measured output, and does not subtract pollution or the using up of resources. Accidents and divorce can raise it. The Genuine Progress Indicator and similar measures try to correct for these, at the price of many judgments about what to add and subtract.

Note: After the text was written, unemployment reached 10.0 percent in October 2009 and 14.8 percent in April 2020, the highest monthly rate since the official series began in 1948.

Question to think about: What would a better measure than GDP include?
