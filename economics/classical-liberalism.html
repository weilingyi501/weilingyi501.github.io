Classical Liberalism

Adam Smith and his successors wrote as the factory system spread in Britain. They argued that individuals pursuing their own interest in competitive markets would promote the general good, and that government should keep to a few tasks.

In this topic
  1. Four assumptions about people
  2. Smith and the market
  3. Growth
  4. Malthus on population
  5. The role of government

1. Four assumptions about people

The text finds four assumptions in classical liberal psychology. People are egoistic, moved by pleasure and pain, as Bentham put it; calculating, weighing choices by reason; inert, unwilling to work without reward or fear; and atomistic, more real than the groups they form. Each of these assumptions reversed part of the older view of society as a family.

2. Smith and the market

Hobbes thought that selfish people without a ruler would be at war. Smith’s Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that competition could turn self-interest to general benefit. Producers must please buyers and cut costs, and capital and labor move to where they are most valued. The search for private gain is guided, in his phrase, by an invisible hand.

3. Growth

Smith’s theory of growth begins with the division of labor. Specialization raises productivity but needs capital for tools and wages. Profits supply the capital, investment widens the division of labor, and output and wages rise. Smith’s followers concluded that regulation would shrink the market and slow the process.

4. Malthus on population

Malthus argued that population, unchecked, grows geometrically while food grows at best arithmetically. Preventive checks lower births, and positive checks such as famine and disease raise deaths. He concluded that poor relief would be self-defeating. Later experience went against the forecast where farm productivity rose and birth rates fell.

5. The role of government

Smith gave government three tasks: defense, justice, and public works that no private party would find profitable. The text stresses that he distrusted merchants and that later business writers used his argument more freely than he did, opposing regulation while welcoming roads, ports, and subsidies. The Poor Law of 1834, which cut back relief, put the liberal view into practice.

Question to think about: What conditions does the invisible hand need in order to work?
