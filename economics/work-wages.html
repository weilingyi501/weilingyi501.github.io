Work and Wages

In the neoclassical account each input earns the value of what it adds. The chapter sets this beside the view that wages also reflect bargaining and power.

In this topic
  1. The demand for labor
  2. The supply of labor
  3. Why wages differ
  4. Is it fair?
  5. Readings

1. The demand for labor

Firms hire workers for the output they add. Marginal revenue product is the extra revenue from one more worker: the extra output times the price in a competitive market. A firm hires until it equals the wage, so the marginal revenue product curve is the labor demand curve. A higher product price or better technology shifts it out.

2. The supply of labor

A higher wage makes leisure more costly, which encourages work (the substitution effect), and makes people richer, which lets them take more leisure (the income effect). Where the second is stronger, the supply curve bends backward. Population, participation, taxes, and benefits shift the curve.

3. Why wages differ

Differences arise from unpleasant or dangerous work (compensating differentials), from education and training (human capital), from obstacles to moving, and from discrimination.

4. Is it fair?

J. B. Clark presented marginal productivity as a principle of justice: each factor receives what it adds. The text’s criticism is that the theory credits owners with what machines add, although owning a machine is different from working. It also says that the theory leaves bargaining power out. Many economists now use marginal productivity as a description and not a defense, and add that employers with wage-setting power pay less than it predicts.

Note: Since the text was written, research on minimum wages and monopsony has moved many economists away from the simple competitive model of the labor market.

Question to think about: Are people paid what they produce?
