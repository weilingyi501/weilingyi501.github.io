Aggregate Supply and Demand

Does a market economy return to full employment by itself? Say and the neoclassical economists gave three reasons to think so, and Keynes answered each one.

In this topic
  1. Say’s law
  2. What changed
  3. Three neoclassical arguments
  4. Wages as cost and as demand
  5. Too much demand
  6. Readings

1. Say’s law

J. B. Say argued that producing goods creates the income to buy them. There can be too much of one good, as Ricardo put it, but not of all goods. In Crusoe’s economy, or on a self-sufficient manor, what is saved is put to use at once, and the conclusion holds.

2. What changed

Marx, Wesley Mitchell, and Keynes pointed to three features of a capitalist economy: production for the market, the use of money, and production for profit. Money can be held. Credit links debtors in chains that can break. Firms that expect no profit stop producing. Together these make a general shortfall of demand possible, though they do not by themselves explain when it happens.

3. Three neoclassical arguments

MarketNeoclassical argumentKeynes’s reply
GoodsFalling prices raise the quantity boughtFalling prices also cut incomes and profits
LaborFalling wages make hiring payWages are spending as well as cost
Saving and investmentA falling interest rate turns saving into investmentInvestment depends on expected profit, and rates have a floor

In this view only outside shocks disturb full employment. Robert Lucas and others built models in which unemployment reflects choices people make after a shock. Keynesians reply that depressions have been too long and too regular for that.

4. Wages as cost and as demand

The disagreement over wages can be put in a simple model. A lower wage share cuts workers’ spending, but may raise profits and so investment. Which effect is larger depends on how strongly investment responds.

5. Too much demand

The same framework explains inflation. If planned spending exceeds what can be produced at full employment, prices rise. Wartime is the clearest case.

Note: Most economists now combine parts of both views: prices and wages adjust slowly, so demand matters for years, while supply matters more over the long run. How slowly is still debated.

Question to think about: Which of Keynes’s three replies do you find strongest?
