Consumer Choice

Neoclassical theory derives demand from consumers who maximize utility within a budget. Critics ask where preferences come from.

In this topic
  1. The assumptions
  2. From utility to demand
  3. Criticism
  4. Readings

1. The assumptions

Consumer theory assumes that people know their preferences and all prices, choose what makes them best off, have limited incomes, always value more, and get less extra satisfaction from each further unit. Utility is the name for satisfaction. Early writers treated it as measurable (cardinal); later ones needed only a ranking (ordinal).

2. From utility to demand

A consumer does best when the last dollar spent on each good adds the same utility. If a price falls, that good now gives more utility per dollar, so more of it is bought. Diminishing marginal utility therefore leads to a downward-sloping demand curve.

3. Criticism

The text raises three objections. Preferences come from family, culture, and experience, so they are not simply given. Advertising tries to change them, and Galbraith spoke of a “revised sequence” in which producers shape wants as well as serve them. People also buy for status, as Veblen said, and act from habit and impulse. Finally, poor consumers may lack money in a society with plenty of goods.

Note: Behavioral economics, associated with Daniel Kahneman and Richard Thaler, has made departures from the rational model a research field of its own.

Question to think about: Are our wants our own?

4. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 28.

Companion reading

William Stanley Jevons, The Theory of Political Economy (1871), chapter 3.
