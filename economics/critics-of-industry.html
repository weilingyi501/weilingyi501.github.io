Critics of Early Industry

Industrial growth brought long hours, child labor, and insecurity. Reformers proposed cooperation and new forms of ownership, and Karl Marx developed a theory of how capitalism works and changes.

In this topic
  1. The setting
  2. Hodgskin and Thompson
  3. Owen and others
  4. Marx’s method
  5. Surplus value
  6. Alienation and crises
  7. Readings

1. The setting

The text describes the early factory towns as places of long hours, child labor, crowding, and epidemics, with unions banned in Britain by the Combination Act of 1799. Critics asked whether these costs were necessary or came from the way ownership was organized.

2. Hodgskin and Thompson

Thomas Hodgskin (1825) asked who produces what is credited to capital. A fisher’s net is the work of net makers, so the output belongs to cooperating workers and not to the owner of the net. He kept the market as the way to coordinate them. William Thompson thought competition itself was at fault: it bred selfishness, subordinated women, made the economy unstable, and held back the spread of knowledge. He favored planned cooperation.

3. Owen and others

Robert Owen ran a profitable mill at New Lanark with decent conditions and schools, and later proposed self-governing cooperative communities. Others took different paths. Saint-Simon wanted large-scale industry directed for the general good, Fourier designed cooperative communities, Godwin trusted reason and education, and Proudhon defended small producers against both property owners and the state.

4. Marx’s method

Marx began from the mode of production: the forces of production (tools, skills, resources) together with the relations of production (who owns and who works). When the forces outgrow the relations, conflict between classes brings change. In capitalism, two classes matter most: those who own means of production and those who must sell their labor power, the capacity to work.

5. Surplus value

Marx followed Smith and Ricardo in relating exchange value to labor time. The value of labor power is the labor needed to produce the worker’s customary living. If that takes six hours and the working day is eight, two hours create surplus value, the source of profit, interest, and rent.

6. Alienation and crises

For Marx, poverty was not the deepest problem. Workers do not control their work, its product, or often whether they work at all; he called this alienation. He also studied crises. Market exchange, money that can be held back, and production for profit make general overproduction possible. In an expansion, wages lag behind output, which limits consumer demand, while interest and raw material costs rise. Profit is squeezed and investment falls.

Note: Marx’s forecasts of steadily worsening conditions and of revolution in the most advanced economies are widely disputed. His analyses of concentration, instability, and work remain in use. Later topics take up the objections to the labor theory of value.

Question to think about: Which of these critics’ concerns have been answered since, and how?

7. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapters 9–10.

Companion reading

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto (1848), part I.
