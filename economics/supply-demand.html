Scarcity, Choice, Supply, and Demand

The standard model begins with scarcity and opportunity cost and leads to supply and demand. The chapters present the model first and then its critics.

In this topic
  1. Scarcity and opportunity cost
  2. Supply, demand, and equilibrium
  3. Elasticity
  4. Criticism
  5. Readings

1. Scarcity and opportunity cost

The standard model assumes that people compare costs and benefits and that wants exceed resources. The production possibility curve shows the most of one good that can be made for each amount of another. On the curve, more of one means less of the other; what is given up is the opportunity cost. An economy with idle workers and factories is inside the curve and can have more of both.

2. Supply, demand, and equilibrium

Demand is the amount people are willing and able to buy at each price, so need without money does not count. Supply is the amount sellers offer at each price. At the equilibrium price the two are equal. A change in the good’s own price moves buyers and sellers along their curves. A change in incomes, tastes, or other prices shifts demand, and a change in costs or technology shifts supply.

3. Elasticity

Price elasticity is the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic when there are good substitutes, when the good takes a large share of income, and when buyers have time to adjust. When demand is inelastic, a rise in price raises sellers’ revenue.

4. Criticism

Critics accept the everyday usefulness of the model and note its limits. Curves are never observed directly; only prices and quantities are, and either curve may have moved. The exact model assumes many small traders with full information. And for many firms, the text argues, the problem is finding enough buyers more than overcoming scarcity.

Question to think about: What can supply and demand explain well, and what can it not?

5. Readings
