Inflation and Monetary Policy

Inflation has come from strong demand, rising costs, and wars. Central banks answer mainly through interest rates, and economists disagree about how well this works. The book predates quantitative easing and the inflation of 2021–23.

1. Measuring inflation

Inflation is a rise in the general price level. The consumer price index follows the cost of a basket of goods; the GDP deflator covers everything produced at home. If an index goes from 107 to 111, inflation is 4/107, or about 3.7 percent.

2. Who is affected

People whose incomes are fixed in money lose, and so do lenders when prices rise faster than expected; borrowers gain. Wages may lag or keep pace. Very rapid inflation makes planning impossible and can destroy the use of money, as in Germany in 1923.

3. Why prices rise

Demand-pull inflation comes when spending outruns production, as near the top of booms and in wars. Cost-push inflation comes from dearer inputs, such as oil in the 1970s, or from wages that rise faster than productivity. The text adds profit-push inflation by firms with market power, and cites Gardiner Means’s finding that concentrated industries cut output more than prices in the 1930s. When prices rise amid high unemployment, as in the 1970s, the result is called stagflation.

4. The Federal Reserve

The Fed was created in 1913 after the panic of 1907. It has a board in Washington appointed by the president and twelve regional banks, whose boards are largely chosen by member banks, an arrangement the authors criticize. The textbook tools are reserve requirements, the discount rate, and open market operations. To slow spending the Fed raises interest rates; to speed it up it lowers them.

5. How well does it work?

Tight money can stop inflation, at the cost of a recession, as in 1981–82. It is weaker against slumps, when firms see no profit in borrowing and rates cannot fall much below zero. Monetarists led by Friedman blamed instability on erratic money growth and wanted a fixed rule. Critics answer that credit follows activity as much as it leads, and that rules do not prevent slumps. Price controls have been used too, in the Second World War and in 1971–74, with disputed results.

Note: Since 2008 the Fed has also bought long-term assets and paid interest on reserves, which is now its main way of setting rates. Consumer prices rose 9.1 percent in the year to June 2022, and economists still divide that rise among supply disruptions, demand, and profit margins.

Question to think about: Who gains and who loses when a central bank fights inflation?
