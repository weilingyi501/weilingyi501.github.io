Production and Costs

A firm’s supply rests on its technology and its costs. The topic works from the production function to cost curves in the short and the long run.

In this topic
  1. The production function
  2. Costs
  3. Making cars
  4. Criticism
  5. Readings

1. The production function

A production function relates inputs, such as labor and capital, to output with a given technology. In the short run at least one input is fixed. As more workers share the same equipment, output per extra worker may rise at first and eventually falls; this is the law of diminishing returns. In the long run all inputs can change, and doubling them may double output or yield more or less: constant, increasing, or decreasing returns to scale.

2. Costs

Total cost is fixed cost plus variable cost. Marginal cost is the cost of one more unit. When it is below average cost it pulls the average down, and when above it pulls it up, so it cuts average cost at the lowest point. Economists count opportunity costs, including what owners could earn elsewhere, so zero economic profit still means a normal return.

3. Making cars

Ford’s Model T joined a new product to a new process: standard parts, special machines, and unskilled labor at large scale. The text contrasts product innovation, which rivals can copy, with process innovation, which it credits for much of the success of Japanese and German firms after the war.

4. Criticism

Critics say that labor is unlike other inputs, because how hard people work depends on relations at the workplace. They add that technology depends on institutions and does not simply arrive, that many firms work with roughly constant unit costs over a wide range, and that large firms often face increasing returns. They also ask whether normal profit should count as a cost: capital goods are needed, but that does not settle who should own them.

Question to think about: Do real firms face rising marginal costs?
