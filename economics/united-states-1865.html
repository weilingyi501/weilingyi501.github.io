The United States, 1776–1865

In 1776 the United States was a country of independent farmers, merchants, and plantations worked by enslaved people. By 1860 the Northeast was industrializing, and the conflict over slavery led to civil war.

1. A colonial economy

Over 80 percent of colonial Americans worked in agriculture. England bought food, lumber, and raw materials cheaply, sold finished goods dear, and restricted the export of new machines. These limits were one cause of the Revolution, and trade with England kept much of the same pattern for decades after independence.

2. Several economies in one country

In 1810 about 81 percent of the labor force was in farming. Northern and western families mostly worked their own land and sold little. City craftspeople made goods to order with a few apprentices. The South depended on plantations worked by enslaved people. Over the century farmers bought machinery, paid railroad rates, borrowed from banks, and became tied to the market.

3. Railroads, corporations, and law

Railroad mileage grew from under 3,000 in 1840 to over 30,000 in 1860, and government land grants paid for much of the building of later lines. The corporation, whose owners have limited liability, let many investors pool funds. Courts changed too: judges moved from the community’s idea of a just price toward enforcing whatever price was agreed, and property law came to favor industry over farming.

4. Work, women, and reform

Early unions were weak. Married women could not hold property in their own name until state laws began to change around mid-century. Many women learned to organize in the movement against slavery, and the 1848 meeting at Seneca Falls called for equal rights, including the vote.

5. The economics of slavery

Plantations sought profit and, after the cotton gin, often found it: the South grew quickly in the fifteen years before the war. But wealth was concentrated. Small farmers without slaves were poor by Northern standards, and little industry developed. Cotton planted year after year wore out soil, which helped push slavery westward. The struggle over its extension, with disputes over tariffs and fugitive slaves, ended in the Civil War.

Note: Historians still debate how far slavery contributed to American growth and whether slave labor was incompatible with factories; enslaved people did work in some Southern industry.

Question to think about: Can slavery be profitable and still hold back economic development?
