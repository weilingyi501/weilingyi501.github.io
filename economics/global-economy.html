The United States in the Global Economy

After 1945 the United States led the world economy. Later, rivals caught up, trade moved from surplus to deficit, and production spread across borders. The book’s account ends in 2006, just before the financial crisis.

In this topic

  2. The slowdown, 1970–1990
  3. What global capitalism means here
  4. Deficits and government

1. The long boom, 1945–1970

The United States came out of the Second World War with its factories intact. Pent-up savings, a technological lead, and foreign demand supported growth; the text puts U.S. growth at 3.7 percent a year from 1947 to 1970. Real wages rose, and the country exported more than it imported and lent to the rest of the world.

2. The slowdown, 1970–1990

Growth fell to about 2.5 percent a year. Japan and Europe had rebuilt and competed strongly, the postwar stimulus had faded, and the technological gap had narrowed. Real wages stagnated for most workers. The 1990s brought faster growth, followed by a stock market fall and recession in the early 2000s.

3. What global capitalism means here

Better transport and communication, freer trade and capital flows, and the spread of markets after 1990 tied national economies together. The text reports that annual foreign direct investment flows rose from 57 billion dollars in 1982 to 1,271 billion in 2000. It divides U.S. history into three stages: industrial capitalism (about 1840–1870), corporate capitalism (1870–1970), and global capitalism (from 1970).

4. Deficits and government

For most of a century before 1970 the United States ran trade surpluses; afterward it ran deficits and borrowed from abroad. The government budget was also often in deficit. A government that borrows in its own currency can always pay, but doing so may bring inflation, and interest moves income from taxpayers to bondholders, some of them foreign. At full employment, extra deficit spending tends to raise prices.

5. Decolonization and world institutions

Between 1945 and about 1970 most colonies became independent, yet many kept exporting raw materials and importing manufactures. The text calls this neocolonialism, a contested term. The IMF and World Bank lend to countries in difficulty; their conditions, such as spending cuts, privatization, and open capital markets, were criticized by Joseph Stiglitz among others, while defenders say that they restored stability and growth.

Note: The account stops in 2006. The financial crisis of 2008, the pandemic recession, the inflation of 2021–23, and new tariffs all came later, and they are good tests of its argument.

Question to think about: Does the period since 2008 belong to the same stage, or to a new one?

6. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 16.

Companion reading

Joseph Stiglitz, Globalization and Its Discontents (Norton, 2002).
