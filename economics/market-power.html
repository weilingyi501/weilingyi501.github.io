Market Power and the Large Firm

Waves of mergers since the 1890s produced industries dominated by a few firms, and governments answered with antitrust law and regulation.

In this topic
  1. Waves of mergers
  2. Why firms grow
  3. Effects
  4. Policy
  5. Readings

1. Waves of mergers

The text describes four waves in the United States. From the 1890s to 1914 firms absorbed direct rivals (horizontal mergers). In the 1920s they absorbed suppliers and outlets (vertical). In the 1950s and 1960s they bought unrelated businesses (conglomerate). Since the 1980s mergers have crossed borders.

2. Why firms grow

Economies of scale lower unit costs. Firms also grow to gain control: over rivals, suppliers, outlets, finance, and risk. The first reason can benefit buyers through lower prices, and the second can raise prices, so it matters which one is at work.

3. Effects

Large firms do much research and can plan for the long run, but a firm with little competition may shelve innovations that would make its own equipment obsolete. Rivalry often takes the form of advertising and product differences in place of price cuts. Critics such as Galbraith argued that large firms shape what consumers want. Defenders reply that size usually reflects efficiency and that new rivals keep appearing.

4. Policy

Four laws are the core of U.S. antitrust: the Sherman Act (1890), the Clayton and Federal Trade Commission Acts (1914), and the Celler–Kefauver Act (1950). Natural monopolies such as power lines may be regulated instead. Regulators can come to serve the firms they oversee, which is called regulatory capture. Conservatives tend to favor leaving firms alone, liberals breaking them up or regulating them, and some critics public ownership.

Note: The data in the chapter stop before the rise of large technology platforms, where network effects give a new reason for concentration.

Question to think about: When is a large firm efficient, and when is it merely powerful?

5. Readings

Core reading

Sherman et al., chapter 21.

Companion reading

John Kenneth Galbraith, The New Industrial State (Houghton Mifflin, 1967).
