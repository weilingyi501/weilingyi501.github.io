Arghiri Emmanuel

Emmanuel came to economics late, after years in business in Africa. His one great book, Unequal Exchange, started one of the most heated debates of the period: whether workers in rich countries share in the gains from the exploitation of poor ones.

Born
1911, Patras, Greece
Died
14 December 2001, Paris, France
Education
Studies in Athens; doctorate in Paris
Worked in
Belgian Congo, France
Key works
Unequal Exchange: A Study of the Imperialism of Trade (1969; English 1972); Appropriate or Underdeveloped Technology? (1982)

Life

Emmanuel was born in Patras, Greece, in 1911 and studied in Athens. He spent years in the Belgian Congo working in his family’s textile business, an experience that gave him a close view of a colonial economy. He was older than most of the people in this column: he was nearly sixty when his main book appeared, and the turn to academic economics came only after he settled in Paris following the Second World War.

In Paris he worked with Charles Bettelheim, the French Marxist economist, and wrote a doctoral thesis on unequal exchange. The two published an early statement of the idea together in 1962. The thesis appeared as a book, L’échange inégal (Unequal Exchange), in 1969, with a critical response by Bettelheim and a reply by Emmanuel bound into the volume. He taught in Paris and continued to write on technology, development, and colonialism until his death in 2001.

Work

Emmanuel started from Marx’s theory of prices of production, in which competition equalizes the rate of profit. He applied it to the world economy. Capital, he assumed, moves between countries, so profit rates tend to equalize internationally. Labor does not move, so wages stay national, and after the late nineteenth century wages in the industrial countries rose far above those in the colonies. When goods made with low-wage labor exchange for goods made with high-wage labor at prices that equalize profit, value is transferred from the low-wage country to the high-wage one. Emmanuel called this unequal exchange. It did not depend on monopoly or on the type of goods traded. It followed from the wage gap itself.

He drew two controversial conclusions. The first was that wage differences were the cause, not just the consequence, of unequal development. The second was that workers in the rich countries had come to share in the gains from the exploitation of poor ones, which weakened the basis for international working-class solidarity. Bettelheim, Amin, and many others disputed parts of the argument, and Amin reworked it into his own account of accumulation on a world scale. In the essay “White-Settler Colonialism and the Myth of Investment Imperialism” (1972) Emmanuel also argued that colonial settlers, not metropolitan capital, had often been the driving force of colonial expansion.
