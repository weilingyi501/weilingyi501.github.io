Andre Gunder Frank

Frank turned a single phrase, “the development of underdevelopment,” into the starting point of dependency theory. Few scholars of his generation moved so often, or between so many continents, and few were as consistently on the side of the people their theories described.

Born
24 February 1929, Berlin, Germany
Died
23 April 2005, Luxembourg
Education
Swarthmore College (BA 1950); University of Chicago (PhD 1957)
Worked in
United States, Brazil, Mexico, Chile, Germany, Britain, the Netherlands, Canada
Key works
Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America (1967); Latin America: Underdevelopment or Revolution (1969); World Accumulation, 1492–1789 (1978); ReOrient (1998)

Life

Frank was born in Berlin in 1929. His family left Germany when the Nazis came to power in 1933, lived in Switzerland, and reached the United States in 1941. He studied economics at Swarthmore and then at the University of Chicago, where he wrote a dissertation on Soviet agriculture in 1957, in the department that was then becoming the center of free-market economics.

After a few years of teaching in the United States he left for Latin America in 1962. He taught in Brazil until the military coup of 1964, worked in Mexico, and from 1968 taught at the University of Chile, where he joined Theotonio dos Santos, Ruy Mauro Marini, and others at the Center for Socio-Economic Studies. He married the Chilean scholar Marta Fuentes, his lifelong collaborator. The coup of September 1973 ended his Chilean years, as it did for most of the group. He moved to the Max Planck Institute in Starnberg, then to the University of East Anglia, and from 1981 to 1994 to the University of Amsterdam. He spent his last years in North America and Luxembourg, working through a long illness until his death in 2005.

Work

In 1966 Frank published “The Development of Underdevelopment” in Monthly Review. Modernization theory held that poor countries were traditional societies that had not yet entered capitalism. The Latin American Communist parties, for their part, described their countries as semi-feudal. Frank rejected both views. Latin America, he argued, had been part of the capitalist world economy since the sixteenth century. Its poverty was not an original condition but the result of centuries in which surplus flowed from satellite to metropolis: from the countryside to the cities, from the cities to the capitals, and from the capitals to Europe and North America. The regions that were most tightly tied to the world market, such as the sugar zones of northeastern Brazil, were among the poorest. Regions grew most when their ties to the metropolis weakened, as during the world wars and the Depression.

Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America (1967) made this case with the histories of Chile and Brazil, and Latin America: Underdevelopment or Revolution (1969) drew the political conclusion that the national bourgeoisie could not lead a path out of dependence. After the Chilean coup, Frank wrote two open letters to Milton Friedman and Arnold Harberger, his former Chicago teachers, about the human cost of the military government’s monetarist program.

In later work Frank pushed the world-system further back in time. World Accumulation, 1492–1789 (1978) traced the cycles of the early modern world economy. With Barry Gills he argued in The World System: Five Hundred Years or Five Thousand? (1993) that a single world system was much older than capitalism. ReOrient (1998) claimed that Asia, and above all China, stood at the center of the world economy until about 1800, and that Europe rose by using American silver to buy its way into Asian trade. The book broke with his old allies, including Amin and Wallerstein, and won the World History Association’s book prize.

Remembered

Samir Amin met Frank and Marta Fuentes in 1967 and found that they were working on parallel problems in different continents. In a short note written after Frank’s death, Amin recalled their friendship and their joint books with Arrighi and Wallerstein. He wrote that Frank was driven by a single wish, to serve the working classes and subordinated peoples, and added:

Spontaneously, unconditionally, he was always on their side.Samir Amin, “A Note on the Death of Andre Gunder Frank (1929–2005),” Monthly Review 57, no. 2 (June 2005)

Amin added that this quality is not always found even among the best intellectuals.
