Paul Baran

Baran was for years nearly the only Marxist economist with tenure at an American university. His Political Economy of Growth gave the next generation its starting point: underdevelopment is not a lack of capitalism, but one of its results.

Born
1909, Mykolaiv, Russian Empire (now Ukraine)
Died
26 March 1964, Palo Alto, California
Education
Studies in Germany; Harvard University (MA)
Worked in
Germany, Poland, United States (wartime government service, Stanford University)
Key works
“On the Political Economy of Backwardness” (1952); The Political Economy of Growth (1957); Monopoly Capital (1966, with Sweezy)

Life

Baran was born in 1909 in Mykolaiv, on the Black Sea, then part of the Russian Empire. His father was a doctor and a Menshevik, and the family moved between Russia, Poland, and Germany during his youth. He studied economics in Germany and was drawn into the debates of the European left. When the Nazis came to power in 1933 he left Germany, spent several years in Poland, and reached the United States in 1939.

He completed a master’s degree at Harvard and during the Second World War worked for the US government, including for the Strategic Bombing Survey, which assessed the effects of the bombing of Germany and Japan. After the war he worked at the Department of Commerce and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. In 1949 Stanford University hired him, and in 1951 it gave him tenure. Through the McCarthy years and after, he was nearly alone as an openly Marxist economist with a permanent post at an American university. He supported the Cuban Revolution after visiting the island in 1960. He died of a heart attack in 1964, before he and Sweezy could finish Monopoly Capital.

Work

Baran’s central concept was the economic surplus: the difference between what a society produces and what it must consume to keep going. He distinguished the actual surplus from the potential surplus, the amount that could be available if waste, luxury consumption, and unemployment were eliminated. The question for development was not only how much surplus a country produced, but who controlled it and how it was used.

In “On the Political Economy of Backwardness” (1952) and The Political Economy of Growth (1957) he argued that in the poor countries the surplus was large but wasted. Part was sent abroad as profits by foreign firms. Part was consumed by landlords and merchants, or spent on the military and the state, and little went into productive investment. The colonial powers and the local classes allied with them had an interest in keeping things this way. Capitalism, in his view, had spread to the periphery in a form that blocked development instead of promoting it. Frank took this argument as his starting point, and the Latin American dependency theorists built on it.

With Sweezy he applied the concept of surplus to the United States. Monopoly Capital (1966), published after his death, argued that giant corporations generate a rising surplus that the economy struggles to absorb. Advertising, military spending, and the sales effort absorb part of it, and stagnation is the normal tendency of the system. The book became a key text for the young radical economists who founded URPE.
