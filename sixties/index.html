The Sixties Generation

Development, empire, and liberation: profiles of the economists and social scientists who came of age with decolonization and the revolutions of the 1960s, and who rethought the world economy from the standpoint of the people at its bottom.

Who they were

Between the Bandung Conference of 1955 and the oil shock of 1973, most of Africa and much of Asia won formal independence, Cuba and Algeria had revolutions, Vietnam fought the United States, and students and workers rose in Paris, Mexico City, Prague, and on American campuses. A cohort of young scholars, most born between 1929 and 1942, did their formative work in the middle of these events. Many of them did not watch from a distance. They worked as planners for newly independent governments, taught at new universities in Dar es Salaam, Santiago, and Dakar, were deported or exiled after coups, and wrote for the movements they belonged to.

They came from different countries and disciplines. Some were economists, others sociologists or historians, and they disagreed with one another often and in public. What they shared was a question: why did the end of colonial rule not bring the development that modernization theory had promised? Their answers took different names, among them dependency, unequal exchange, the world-system, and the multinational corporation. All of them treated the world economy as one hierarchical whole, in which the wealth of some regions and the poverty of others were produced by the same process.

These pages introduce the people behind those ideas: where they came from, what they lived through, what they wrote, and what became of their work. The theories themselves are taught in more detail in the History of Economic Thought course (Topic 11) and in the Theories of International Trade course (Topics 22–25).

Forerunners: the Monthly Review circle

The generation had teachers. Paul Baran, Paul Sweezy, and Harry Magdoff were born between 1909 and 1913 and formed by the Depression of the 1930s rather than by decolonization. Through the magazine Monthly Review, founded in 1949, and its press, they kept Marxist economics alive in the United States during the McCarthy years. They supplied the younger generation with key ideas: Baran’s economic surplus, and the monopoly capital of Baran and Sweezy. They also published much of its work in English, including Frank’s “The Development of Underdevelopment” and the English editions of Emmanuel and Amin. In Latin America, Raúl Prebisch and the structuralists of the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (see Topic 20 of the History of Economic Thought course) had already described the world economy as a center and a periphery.

Paul Baran1909–1964The Stanford economist whose theory of the economic surplus explained why capitalism did not develop the poor countries. Dependency theory grew from his work. Paul Sweezy1910–2004A Harvard economist who left academia, founded Monthly Review, and became the leading Marxist economist and publisher in the United States. Harry Magdoff1913–2006A New Deal statistician who became co-editor of Monthly Review and showed, during the Vietnam War, that imperialism was central to the American economy.

The world economy seen from the periphery

Frank, dos Santos, and their colleagues in Santiago built dependency theory out of Latin American experience. Amin, Emmanuel, and Rodney did the same for Africa, and Wallerstein and Arrighi turned these insights into the study of the capitalist world-system as a whole. Amin, Arrighi, Frank, and Wallerstein later wrote two books together and called themselves, half in jest, the “gang of four.”

Andre Gunder Frank1929–2005A Chicago-trained economist who moved to Latin America and argued that underdevelopment is produced by capitalism, not left behind by it. Samir Amin1931–2018An Egyptian economist in Dakar who analyzed accumulation on a world scale and called on the South to delink from it. Arghiri Emmanuel1911–2001A Greek-born economist in Paris whose theory of unequal exchange located the gap between rich and poor nations in the gap between their wages. Immanuel Wallerstein1930–2019A sociologist of African independence who built world-systems analysis, the study of capitalism as a single historical system since the sixteenth century. Giovanni Arrighi1937–2009An Italian economist radicalized in Rhodesia and Tanzania who traced the long cycles of capitalist hegemony from Genoa to China. Theotonio dos Santos1936–2018A Brazilian sociologist and economist, exiled twice, who gave dependency theory its best-known definition. Walter Rodney1942–1980A Guyanese historian whose How Europe Underdeveloped Africa made underdevelopment a story that ordinary readers could follow. He was assassinated at 38.

The American wing: radical economics in the United States

In the United States the same years produced a revolt inside the economics profession. In 1968 graduate students and young faculty from Michigan, Harvard, and Radcliffe founded the Union for Radical Political Economics (URPE), and in 1969 they launched the Review of Radical Political Economics. Many had been drawn in by the civil rights movement and the war in Vietnam, and several had worked in the Third World before they joined. Their textbook The Capitalist System (1972), edited by Richard Edwards, Michael Reich, and Thomas Weisskopf, put imperialism, racism, and sexism on the syllabus next to markets and prices. Others of this cohort, including David Gordon (1944–1996), went on to study labor-market segmentation and the long swings of American capitalism.

Stephen Hymer1934–1974The economist who founded the theory of the multinational corporation and then turned it into a critique of uneven development. He died at 39. Samuel Bowlesb. 1939A Harvard economist who left for UMass Amherst, showed with Herbert Gintis how schooling reproduces class, and built a theory of power inside competitive markets. Herbert Gintis1940–2023A mathematician turned radical economist who wrote on alienation and schooling, then helped rebuild economics on game theory and the evidence that people cooperate. Thomas Weisskopfb. 1940An American development economist trained in India who helped found radical economics and later compared inequality and affirmative action in India and the United States.

A short chronology

1955Bandung Conference of Asian and African states
1957Ghana becomes independent; Baran publishes The Political Economy of Growth
1959Cuban Revolution
1960Seventeen African countries become independent
1962Algeria wins independence from France
1964Military coup in Brazil; the first UN Conference on Trade and Development
1966Tricontinental Conference in Havana; Frank publishes “The Development of Underdevelopment”
1968Student and worker revolts worldwide; URPE founded; Rodney banned from Jamaica
1969Emmanuel publishes L’échange inégal (Unequal Exchange)
1970Allende elected in Chile; Amin publishes Accumulation on a World Scale
1972Rodney publishes How Europe Underdeveloped Africa
1973Military coup in Chile ends the Santiago circle of dependency theorists
1974UN declaration on a New International Economic Order; Wallerstein publishes The Modern World-System, vol. 1
