Samuel Bowles

Bowles began as an economist of education in Nigeria and became one of the main figures of American radical economics. Over six decades he carried the questions of 1968, about power, class, and inequality, into the core of economic theory and into the way economics is taught.

Born
1939, New Haven, Connecticut
Education
Yale University (BA 1960); Harvard University (PhD 1965)
Worked in
Nigeria, Harvard, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Santa Fe Institute
Key works
Planning Educational Systems for Economic Growth (1969); Schooling in Capitalist America (1976, with Gintis); Beyond the Waste Land (1983, with Gordon and Weisskopf); Microeconomics: Behavior, Institutions, and Evolution (2004); The Moral Economy (2016)

Life

Bowles was born in New Haven in 1939 and spent part of his youth in India, where his father, Chester Bowles, served as US ambassador. After Yale he taught secondary school in northern Nigeria, and his doctoral research at Harvard was on educational planning for economic growth, drawing on the Nigerian case. He joined the Harvard faculty and was drawn into the civil rights and antiwar movements. In 1968 he was among the young economists at Harvard who helped found the Union for Radical Political Economics.

Harvard declined to promote him in the early 1970s. In 1973–74 the University of Massachusetts Amherst hired Bowles, Herbert Gintis, Richard Edwards, and other radical economists, and the department became one of the main homes of political economy in the United States. Bowles later also joined the Santa Fe Institute, where he directed its program on behavioral sciences. He has continued to write on inequality and on how economics should be taught, above all as a leader of the CORE project, whose open-access textbook The Economy is used around the world.

Work

His first book, Planning Educational Systems for Economic Growth (1969), used the tools of development planning. His experience in the movements of the 1960s led him to ask a different question: why had expanding education done so little to equalize income or opportunity? With Gintis he answered it in Schooling in Capitalist America (1976). Schools, they argued, prepare young people for the social relations of work, not only for its technical demands, and the hierarchy of the classroom mirrors the hierarchy of the workplace. This became known as the correspondence principle.

In the 1980s, with David Gordon and Thomas Weisskopf, he explained the slowdown of the American economy after the late 1960s as the breakdown of the postwar “social structure of accumulation” (Beyond the Waste Land, 1983). With Gintis he developed the theory of contested exchange. Because labor and credit contracts cannot specify everything, the side on the short end of the market, such as an employer facing a worker who might shirk, exercises power through the threat of dismissal. Power is therefore part of competitive markets, not a departure from them.

His later work combined this tradition with game theory, experiments, and evolutionary models. Microeconomics: Behavior, Institutions, and Evolution (2004) rebuilt the graduate microeconomics curriculum around incomplete contracts, power, and social preferences. The Moral Economy (2016) argued that incentives built on pure self-interest can crowd out the ethical and civic motives that good economic institutions depend on.
