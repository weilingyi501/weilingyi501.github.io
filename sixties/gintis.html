Herbert Gintis

Gintis came to economics from mathematics and to radical economics from the movements of the 1960s. He spent the second half of his career using the tools of game theory to show that people are not the purely selfish agents of textbook economics.

Born

Died

Life

Gintis was born in Philadelphia in 1940. He trained first as a mathematician and moved into economics at Harvard during the 1960s, as the civil rights and antiwar movements were reshaping the university. His doctoral dissertation (1969) was a critique of welfare economics from the standpoint of alienation and power. He was among the young Harvard economists who founded the Union for Radical Political Economics in 1968.

In the mid-1970s he moved with Samuel Bowles and others to the University of Massachusetts Amherst, where he taught for most of his career. Later he was an external faculty member of the Santa Fe Institute and taught at the Central European University in Budapest. He wrote prolifically until his death in January 2023.

Work

Gintis’s early work asked what standard welfare economics left out. It treated preferences as given, but if work and schooling shape what people want and who they become, then the economy produces people as well as goods. That question ran through his collaboration with Bowles. In Schooling in Capitalist America (1976) they argued that schools reproduce the class structure by preparing young people for the hierarchy of the workplace. In Democracy and Capitalism (1986) they made the case for extending democratic rights from politics to the economy, and in the theory of contested exchange they showed how power operates within competitive markets.

From the 1990s Gintis turned to game theory and behavioral science. Game Theory Evolving (2000) became a widely used problem-centered textbook. With Bowles, Ernst Fehr, and others he developed the idea of strong reciprocity: a tendency, observed in experiments across many societies, to cooperate with others who cooperate and to punish those who do not, even at a cost to oneself. The Bounds of Reason (2009) argued for game theory as a common language for the behavioral sciences. A Cooperative Species (2011), written with Bowles, explained how human cooperation could have evolved. In his view, the evidence that people are not purely self-interested was both good science and a reason for hope about democratic economic institutions.
