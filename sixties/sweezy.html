Paul Sweezy

Sweezy gave up a promising Harvard career to found a small socialist magazine. For more than half a century Monthly Review was where the ideas of the sixties generation reached readers in English, and Sweezy was its editor, mentor, and host.

Born
10 April 1910, New York City
Died
February 2004, Larchmont, New York
Education
Harvard University (BA 1931, PhD 1937); London School of Economics (1932–33)
Worked in
Harvard University; Office of Strategic Services; Monthly Review (1949–2004)
Key works
The Theory of Capitalist Development (1942); Monopoly Capital (1966, with Baran); The End of Prosperity (1977) and Stagnation and the Financial Explosion (1987), with Magdoff

Life

Sweezy was born in New York in 1910 into a wealthy family; his father was a bank executive. He studied at Phillips Exeter and Harvard, and in 1932–33 spent a year at the London School of Economics, where the debates of the British left and the crisis of the Depression turned him toward Marxism. Back at Harvard he wrote a dissertation on monopoly in the English coal trade, became a protégé of Joseph Schumpeter, and published a well-known article on the “kinked” demand curve of oligopoly (1939). During the Second World War he served in the Office of Strategic Services.

After the war he left Harvard, where his politics made a permanent post unlikely. In 1949, with the labor journalist Leo Huberman, he founded Monthly Review, an independent socialist magazine whose first issue carried Albert Einstein’s essay “Why Socialism?” In the early 1950s the attorney general of New Hampshire questioned him about a lecture he had given at the state university and about his political associations. Sweezy refused to answer, was held in contempt, and took the case to the Supreme Court, which ruled in his favor in Sweezy v. New Hampshire (1957), a landmark decision for academic freedom. He edited the magazine until his death in 2004, from 1969 together with Harry Magdoff.

Work

The Theory of Capitalist Development (1942) was for decades the standard English-language account of Marxian economics. In the debate with Maurice Dobb on the transition from feudalism to capitalism, which began in 1950, Sweezy emphasized the disruptive effect of long-distance trade and towns on feudal Europe. Later writers such as Frank and Wallerstein built on this emphasis, and in 1977 Robert Brenner criticized all three as “neo-Smithian” Marxists.

With Baran he wrote Monopoly Capital (1966), which argued that the giant corporation had replaced competition as the central institution of American capitalism, and that the system tended toward stagnation because it generated more surplus than it could absorb. With Magdoff, in a long series of Monthly Review essays, he traced the slowdown of the American economy after the 1960s. In Stagnation and the Financial Explosion (1987) they argued that the growth of debt and finance was a response to stagnation in production, an early analysis of what is now called financialization.

Through the magazine and its press, Sweezy published and corresponded with much of the sixties generation. Frank, Amin, Emmanuel, and many others reached American readers through Monthly Review.
