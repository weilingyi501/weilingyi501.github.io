Immanuel Wallerstein

Wallerstein began as a scholar of African independence and ended as the author of one of the largest projects in modern social science: a history of the capitalist world-economy from its origins in the sixteenth century to its expected crisis in the twenty-first.

Born
28 September 1930, New York City
Died
31 August 2019, Connecticut
Education
Columbia University (BA 1951, MA 1954, PhD 1959)
Worked in
Columbia, McGill, Binghamton, Yale; research in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire
Key works
Africa: The Politics of Independence (1961); The Modern World-System, 4 vols. (1974–2011); Historical Capitalism (1983); World-Systems Analysis: An Introduction (2004)

Life

Wallerstein was born in New York in 1930 and spent his whole education at Columbia. His dissertation compared the nationalist movements of the Gold Coast (Ghana) and the Ivory Coast, and his early books, such as Africa: The Politics of Independence (1961), made him a leading figure in African studies. He traveled widely in Africa in the years of independence and knew many of its political leaders and intellectuals.

The student occupation of Columbia in 1968 was a turning point. Wallerstein worked among the faculty trying to negotiate a settlement and afterwards wrote University in Turmoil (1969). He later called 1968 a “world revolution” that ended the dominance of the old left as well as of liberalism. He moved to McGill in 1971 and in 1976 to the State University of New York at Binghamton, where he founded the Fernand Braudel Center, named after the French historian whose work on the long sixteenth century inspired him. He was president of the International Sociological Association from 1994 to 1998. From 1998 until shortly before his death in 2019 he wrote a twice-monthly commentary on world affairs.

Work

African independence raised a puzzle for Wallerstein: the new states had political sovereignty but little control over their economic fate. He concluded that the nation-state was the wrong unit of analysis. The proper unit was the world-system, and the modern one was a capitalist world-economy: a single division of labor that crossed many states, held together by the market rather than by one empire. It took shape in Europe and the Americas in the “long sixteenth century” (about 1450–1640).

The world-economy is divided into core, periphery, and semi-periphery. The core specializes in high-profit, high-wage production with strong states. The periphery specializes in low-wage production with weak states, and the semi-periphery stands in between, both exploited and exploiting, which he argued helps to keep the system politically stable. States can move between zones, but the hierarchy itself persists. The four volumes of The Modern World-System traced this structure from its origins to 1914, and Historical Capitalism (1983) gave a short overview.

With Giovanni Arrighi and Terence Hopkins he wrote Antisystemic Movements (1989) on the labor, nationalist, and new social movements that opposed the system. In his last decades he argued that the capitalist world-economy had entered a structural crisis, and that its successor would be decided by political struggle in the coming decades.
