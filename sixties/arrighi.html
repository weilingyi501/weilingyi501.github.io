Giovanni Arrighi

Arrighi went to Africa as a young economist trained in neoclassical theory and came back convinced that it could not explain what he saw there. He spent the rest of his life on the history of capitalism as a world system, and ended it with a study of the rise of China.

Born
7 July 1937, Milan, Italy
Died
18 June 2009, Baltimore, Maryland
Education
Bocconi University, Milan (laurea in economics, 1960)
Worked in
Rhodesia (Zimbabwe), Tanzania, Italy, United States
Key works
The Geometry of Imperialism (1978); The Long Twentieth Century (1994); Chaos and Governance in the Modern World System (1999, with Beverly Silver); Adam Smith in Beijing (2007)

Life

Arrighi was born in Milan in 1937 and studied economics at Bocconi University. After a short period in business he went to Africa in 1963 to teach at the University College of Rhodesia and Nyasaland in Salisbury (now Harare). He became involved with the African nationalist movement and in 1966 was deported from Rhodesia by the white-settler government for his political activities. He moved to the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania, then a meeting place for scholars and liberation movements from across Africa, where he worked with the Canadian political scientist John Saul.

Back in Italy in the late 1960s, he taught in Trento and Calabria and was active in the radical workers’ movement of those years. In 1979 he joined Wallerstein at the Fernand Braudel Center in Binghamton, and in 1998 he moved to Johns Hopkins University, where he taught until his death in 2009.

Work

Arrighi’s African work challenged W. Arthur Lewis’s model of development with unlimited supplies of labor. In “Labour Supplies in Historical Perspective” (1970) he showed that in Rhodesia the supply of cheap African labor was not a natural fact. It was created by land policy, taxation, and the destruction of a prosperous African peasant agriculture, which forced Africans into wage work. With Saul he wrote Essays on the Political Economy of Africa (1973). The Geometry of Imperialism (1978) sorted out the many meanings of imperialism in Hobson and Lenin.

The Long Twentieth Century (1994) is his best-known book. It traces four “systemic cycles of accumulation,” each led by a hegemonic center: Genoa, the Netherlands, Britain, and the United States. Each cycle begins with a phase of material expansion in trade and production and ends with a phase of financial expansion, when capital leaves production for finance. Financial expansion marks the “autumn” of a hegemony and the beginning of a transition to the next. Read in 1994, the argument implied that the financialization of the American economy since the 1970s was a sign of declining US hegemony.

Adam Smith in Beijing (2007) argued that East Asia’s rise, and China’s above all, followed a path of “natural” market-based development that Smith had described. This path was based on labor-intensive growth and a strong home market, and differed from the Western path of capitalist and military expansion. It was a characteristic Arrighi move: to read a classic against the way it is usually read, and to look for the future of the world system outside the West.
