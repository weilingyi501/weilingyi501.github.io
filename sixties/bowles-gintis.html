Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis

Bowles and Gintis wrote as a pair for more than four decades. Their Schooling in Capitalist America remains a classic, and their later work on power, incentives, and cooperation carried the questions of 1968 into the center of economic theory.

Bowles
Born 1939, New Haven, Connecticut. Yale (BA 1960), Harvard (PhD 1965). Harvard, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Santa Fe Institute
Gintis
Born 1940, Philadelphia; died 5 January 2023. Harvard (PhD 1969). Harvard, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Santa Fe Institute
Joint works
Schooling in Capitalist America (1976); Democracy and Capitalism (1986); A Cooperative Species (2011)

Lives

Bowles spent part of his youth in India, where his father, Chester Bowles, served as US ambassador. After Yale he taught secondary school in northern Nigeria, and his early research at Harvard was on educational planning for economic growth, drawing on the Nigerian case. Gintis came to economics from mathematics and wrote his Harvard dissertation on alienation and consumer welfare. Both were drawn into the civil rights and antiwar movements, and both were among the young economists at Harvard who helped found the Union for Radical Political Economics in 1968.

Harvard declined to promote Bowles in the early 1970s. In 1973–74 the University of Massachusetts Amherst hired Bowles, Gintis, Richard Edwards, and other radical economists, and the department became one of the main homes of political economy in the United States. Both men later joined the Santa Fe Institute. Gintis died in 2023. Bowles has continued to write on inequality and on how economics should be taught, including through the open-access CORE textbook The Economy.

Work

Human capital theory explained schooling as an investment that raises productivity and wages. Schooling in Capitalist America (1976) proposed a different account. Schools, they argued, prepare young people for the social relations of work, not only for its technical demands. The hierarchy of the classroom mirrors the hierarchy of the workplace. Working-class schools reward obedience and punctuality, while elite schools reward initiative and independence. This correspondence principle helped explain why expanding education had done so little to equalize income or opportunity.

In the 1980s and 1990s they developed the theory of contested exchange. In labor and credit markets, contracts cannot specify everything, so the side on the short end of the market, such as the employer facing a worker who might shirk, can exercise power through the threat of dismissal. Power is therefore part of competitive markets themselves, not a departure from them. Democracy and Capitalism (1986) set this alongside a case for extending democratic rights to the economy. In their last joint work, A Cooperative Species (2011), they used game theory, experiments, and evolutionary models to argue that people are not purely self-interested and that cooperation and fairness are part of human nature.
