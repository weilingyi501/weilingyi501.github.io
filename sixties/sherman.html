Howard Sherman

Sherman wrote the first general treatise of the new radical economics and one of its most widely used textbooks. He spent his career explaining why capitalist economies rise and fall, and arguing that socialism had to be democratic.

Born
1931, Chicago, Illinois
Education
UCLA (BA); University of Chicago (law degree, 1953); University of Southern California (MA 1957); University of California, Berkeley (PhD 1960)
Worked in
Brookings Institution, Wayne State, Caltech, Berkeley, University of California, Riverside
Key works
The Soviet Economy (1969); Radical Political Economy (1972); Economics: An Introduction to Traditional and Radical Views (1972, with E. K. Hunt); The Business Cycle (1991); Reinventing Marxism (1995)

Life

Sherman was born in Chicago in 1931, the son of an engineer and a secretary who later became an artist. His politics formed early: he was a delegate to the Progressive Party convention of 1948 and a vice president of the NAACP chapter at the University of Chicago. He served as a private in the U.S. Army from 1953 to 1955, in the McCarthy years, and later wrote about that struggle in “The Making of a Radical Economist.” He had sympathized with the Soviet Union until Khrushchev’s revelations about Stalin, after which he called himself a democratic socialist.

He took a law degree before turning to economics. At Berkeley he studied with Joseph Bain, Robert A. Gordon, and Andreas Papandreou, later prime minister of Greece, and wrote his dissertation on economic concentration and business cycles. It took him seven years after the doctorate to find a tenured post. He found it at the University of California, Riverside, where he chaired the department and helped build one of the country’s leading programs in political economy; his colleagues there included E. K. Hunt, Victor Lippit, Keith Griffin, and Robert Pollin. He was a founding member of the Union for Radical Political Economics and led the local of the American Federation of Teachers at Riverside.

Work

Radical Political Economy: Capitalism and Socialism from a Marxist-Humanist Perspective (1972) set out the field as a whole, only a few years after it had reappeared in American universities. In the same year came the introductory textbook with Hunt. It placed the standard theory beside its critics and opened with history, and in its first six editions it sold around 300,000 copies.

Business cycles were his lifelong subject. He drew on Marx, Wesley Mitchell, Keynes, and Kalecki to argue that each phase of the cycle produces the next. In what he called the nutcracker theory, profits near the top of a boom are squeezed from two sides: demand weakens as consumption fails to keep up with output, while costs rise. Investment falls, and a recession follows. The Business Cycle: Growth and Crisis under Capitalism (1991) tested the argument against the American record and added credit, monopoly, the world economy, and government.

Sherman was as critical of the Soviet Union as of capitalism. From The Soviet Economy (1969) onward he studied a system that lacked political democracy and was run by a small group from above, and he came to call it statism, neither capitalist nor socialist. Reinventing Marxism (1995), written after the Soviet collapse, defended a Marxism without dogma, used as a method for studying how the parts of a society are related and how they change.

The text Sherman wrote with Hunt guides the course Introduction to Economics: History, Theory, and Policy.
