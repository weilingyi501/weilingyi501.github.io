Walter Rodney

Rodney was a historian rather than an economist, but no book of the period did more to spread the idea of underdevelopment than his How Europe Underdeveloped Africa. He also lived his argument, as a teacher in Tanzania and a political organizer in the Caribbean.

Born
23 March 1942, Georgetown, British Guiana
Died
13 June 1980, Georgetown, Guyana
Education
University of the West Indies, Mona (BA 1963); School of Oriental and African Studies, London (PhD 1966)
Worked in
Tanzania, Jamaica, Guyana
Key works
A History of the Upper Guinea Coast, 1545–1800 (1970); The Groundings with My Brothers (1969); How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972); A History of the Guyanese Working People, 1881–1905 (1981)

Life

Rodney was born into a working-class family in Georgetown, British Guiana, in 1942. He won scholarships to the University of the West Indies in Jamaica and to the School of Oriental and African Studies in London, where at 24 he completed a doctorate on the Upper Guinea Coast and the Atlantic slave trade. He then taught at the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania, where Julius Nyerere’s government was building a program of African socialism.

In 1968 he returned to the University of the West Indies in Jamaica. Besides teaching, he held informal sessions, which he called “groundings,” with poor people in Kingston on African history and Black liberation. In October 1968, while he was abroad at a writers’ congress in Montreal, the Jamaican government banned him from returning. Student protests followed and turned into riots in Kingston. His talks from those months were published as The Groundings with My Brothers (1969).

Rodney went back to Dar es Salaam from 1969 to 1974, the years in which he wrote his best-known book. In 1974 he returned to Guyana to take up a professorship, but the government of Forbes Burnham blocked the appointment. He helped build the Working People’s Alliance, a party that tried to unite Guyanese of African and Indian descent against an increasingly authoritarian state. On 13 June 1980 he was killed in Georgetown by a bomb concealed in a radio transmitter. He was 38. A commission of inquiry in Guyana reported in 2016 that the state had been involved in his killing.

Work

How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972) was written for a broad audience, and it has been read by students, activists, and general readers ever since. Its argument was that Africa’s poverty was not an original condition. Before the European slave trade, African societies had developed at their own pace in agriculture, crafts, trade, and state formation. Four centuries of the slave trade and then colonial rule drained labor and surplus, turned economies toward a few exports, and blocked the growth of local industry and skills. Rodney insisted that development and underdevelopment were two sides of one historical process, so that Europe’s development was tied to Africa’s underdevelopment. He also stressed that the colonial period left little of value behind, a claim that set him against colonial apologists.

His historical research was just as important. A History of the Upper Guinea Coast (1970) showed how the slave trade reshaped African societies from within, and his posthumous History of the Guyanese Working People (1981) studied the making of the working class in his own country.
