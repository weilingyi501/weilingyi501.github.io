E. K. Hunt

Hunt taught generations of students to read economic theories as part of the history of the societies that produced them. He criticized the foundations of welfare economics and helped shape the economics department at the University of Utah.

Born
1937, Blanding, Utah
Education
University of Utah (BA 1961, PhD 1968)
Worked in
University of California, Riverside (1969–78); University of Utah (from 1978)
Key works
Property and Prophets (1972); Economics: An Introduction to Traditional and Radical Views (1972, with Howard Sherman); History of Economic Thought: A Critical Perspective (1979); “A Radical Critique of Welfare Economics” (1980)

Life

Emery Kay Hunt was born in Blanding, in the southeast corner of Utah, in 1937. He took both his degrees at the University of Utah, where Lawrence Nabers awakened his interest in the history of economic thought. Besides Nabers he named Marx, John Dewey, Thorstein Veblen, Leo Rogin, and Maurice Dobb as the writers who shaped him, a list that joins Marxism to American pragmatism and institutionalism.

From 1969 to 1978 he taught at the University of California, Riverside, where he and Howard Sherman were colleagues and co-authors. He then returned to the University of Utah, chaired its economics department from 1983 to 1989, and became professor emeritus. With Allen Sievers he later wrote a short account of heterodox economics at Utah.

Work

Two books that appeared in 1972 made his name with students. Property and Prophets: The Evolution of Economic Institutions and Ideologies tells the history of capitalism together with the ideas used to defend and attack it, from the paternalism of the Middle Ages through classical liberalism, socialism, and the New Deal. The introductory text with Sherman brought the same approach to the first course in economics.

His essays went after the foundations of the standard theory. “A Radical Critique of Welfare Economics” (1980) called welfare economics the heart of neoclassical theory and argued that it rests on hedonism twice over, as an account of how people behave and as a measure of what is good. It takes individual desires as given, though they are formed by society. Its weakest point, Hunt held, is its treatment of externalities as isolated exceptions, when the actions of people in society affect one another everywhere. With Ralph d’Arge he had applied this to pollution in 1971. He wrote that in such a world people are led by an “invisible foot” to promote public misery. Other essays examined the labor theory of value, property, and alienation in Marx.

History of Economic Thought: A Critical Perspective (1979, with later editions by Hunt and Mark Lautzenheiser) is his major work of synthesis. It denies that the history of economics can be told without values and follows long-running disputes, above all whether value comes from labor or from utility and whether the distribution of income determines prices or follows from them. It uses Piero Sraffa’s work to reread the classical economists and Marx.

Hunt’s books guide two courses on this site: the course on capitalism, based on Property and Prophets, and Introduction to Economics: History, Theory, and Policy, based on the text with Sherman.
