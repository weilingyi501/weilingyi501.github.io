Stephen Hymer

Hymer wrote the dissertation that founded the economic theory of the multinational firm. In a career of only fifteen years he moved from that technical contribution to one of the sharpest critiques of how multinationals shape the world economy.

Born
1934, Montreal, Canada
Died
February 1974, New York State
Education
McGill University; Massachusetts Institute of Technology (PhD 1960)
Worked in
Yale University, Ghana, the New School for Social Research
Key works
The International Operations of National Firms (thesis 1960, published 1976); “The Multinational Corporation and the Law of Uneven Development” (1972)

Life

Hymer was born in Montreal in 1934 and studied at McGill and then at MIT, where Charles Kindleberger supervised his doctoral thesis, completed in 1960. He taught at Yale and spent time in Ghana in the mid-1960s, studying the economic history of the Gold Coast and the effects of colonial rule. His contact with Africa, the war in Vietnam, and the student movement changed his views. By the late 1960s he was part of the new radical economics. In 1970 he taught his first course on Marx at Yale, and soon afterwards he moved to the New School for Social Research in New York. He died in a car accident in February 1974, at the age of 39.

Work

Before Hymer, economists treated foreign direct investment as a movement of capital from where it was abundant to where it was scarce. His thesis argued that this could not explain why firms owned and controlled factories abroad. A foreign firm faces disadvantages in an unfamiliar country, so it invests there only when it has some advantage of its own, such as technology, brands, or organization, and wants to control how that advantage is used. Direct investment is therefore a matter of market power and control, a part of industrial organization rather than of capital flows. MIT did not publish the thesis until 1976, after his death, but it became the foundation of the field of international business.

His later essays drew radical conclusions from the same analysis. In “The Multinational Corporation and the Law of Uneven Development” (1972) he argued that the multinational firm reproduces its internal hierarchy across the map of the world. Top-level planning and strategy gather in a few great cities of the core. Middle management spreads to regional centers, and routine production is located in the periphery. This geography of power and income, he wrote, would tend to make the world economy as unequal as the corporation itself. In “Robinson Crusoe and the Secret of Primitive Accumulation” (1971) he reread the famous economic parable as a story of colonial labor.
