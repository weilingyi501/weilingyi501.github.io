Thomas Weisskopf

Weisskopf’s path ran from planning research in India to the founding of radical economics in the United States. Throughout his career he connected the economics of the Third World with that of the rich countries.

Born
1940, Rochester, New York
Education
Massachusetts Institute of Technology (PhD 1966)
Worked in
Indian Statistical Institute (Kolkata and New Delhi), Harvard, University of Michigan
Key works
The Capitalist System (1972, co-editor); Beyond the Waste Land (1983, with Bowles and Gordon); Affirmative Action in the United States and India (2004)

Life

Weisskopf was born in Rochester, New York, in 1940, the son of the physicist Victor Weisskopf. Between 1961 and 1968 he spent about four years teaching and doing research at the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata and New Delhi, in the years when India’s five-year plans made it a laboratory for development economics. He completed his doctorate in economics at MIT in 1966.

He was an assistant professor at Harvard from 1968 to 1972 and one of the young economists there who helped found the Union for Radical Political Economics. In 1972 he moved to the University of Michigan, where he taught economics and in the Residential College until his retirement in 2010.

Work

With Richard Edwards and Michael Reich, Weisskopf edited The Capitalist System (1972), the textbook of the new radical economics. It covered alienation, inequality, racism, sexism, and imperialism alongside markets and growth. His own essays of these years examined how capitalism in the rich countries affected the development of the poor ones. He argued that the obstacles to Third World development lay less in a shortage of capital than in class structures and international relations that left the surplus with local elites and foreign firms.

In the 1980s, with Samuel Bowles and David Gordon, he turned to the crisis of the American economy. Beyond the Waste Land (1983) and After the Waste Land (1990) explained the slowdown of productivity and profits after the late 1960s as the breakdown of the postwar “social structure of accumulation,” the set of institutions that had governed relations between capital and labor, between the United States and the world, and between business and citizens. His later work returned to India, comparing affirmative action and the growth of inequality in India and the United States.
