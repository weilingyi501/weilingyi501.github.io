Harry Magdoff

Magdoff spent his early career measuring productivity for the New Deal and his later career as co-editor of Monthly Review. His Age of Imperialism, written during the Vietnam War, was one of the books most read by the young radicals of 1968.

Born
21 August 1913, New York City
Died
1 January 2006, Burlington, Vermont
Education
City College of New York; New York University
Worked in
New Deal agencies and the Department of Commerce; business; Monthly Review (co-editor 1969–2006)
Key works
The Age of Imperialism (1969); Imperialism: From the Colonial Age to the Present (1978); with Sweezy, The Dynamics of U.S. Capitalism (1970) and Stagnation and the Financial Explosion (1987)

Life

Magdoff was born in the Bronx in 1913 to a family of Russian Jewish immigrants and came to socialism as a teenager. He studied at City College and New York University during the Depression. In the late 1930s and the war years he worked as an economist and statistician in New Deal and wartime agencies, including studies of productivity and employment and work on war production, and then in the Department of Commerce, where Henry Wallace was secretary.

He left government in 1946. In the McCarthy era he was investigated, and for about two decades he worked outside academia and government, in business and finance. In the 1960s he returned to public writing. When Leo Huberman died in 1968, Magdoff joined Sweezy as co-editor of Monthly Review in 1969 and remained an editor until his death in 2006, at 92.

Work

During the Vietnam War many liberal economists argued that foreign trade and investment were too small a share of the American economy to explain US foreign policy. In The Age of Imperialism: The Economics of U.S. Foreign Policy (1969) Magdoff answered them with data. He showed how much American corporate profit came from abroad, how dependent American industry was on imported raw materials, how US banks had spread overseas, and how military spending and foreign aid supported this position. Imperialism, he argued, was not a policy that could be dropped at will but a way of life of the American economy. The book was widely read in the antiwar movement and among the founders of URPE.

Imperialism: From the Colonial Age to the Present (1978) traced the longer history, arguing that formal colonialism had given way to new forms of domination through finance, corporations, and military power. With Sweezy, in essays collected in The Dynamics of U.S. Capitalism (1970), The End of Prosperity (1977), and Stagnation and the Financial Explosion (1987), he analyzed the slowdown of American capitalism and the growing weight of debt and finance.
