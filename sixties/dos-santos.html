Theotonio dos Santos

Dos Santos, with Ruy Mauro Marini and Vânia Bambirra, built the Marxist current of dependency theory. Their work was shaped by two military coups, in Brazil in 1964 and in Chile in 1973, and by the exile that followed each.

Born
11 November 1936, Carangola, Minas Gerais, Brazil
Died
27 February 2018, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
Education
Federal University of Minas Gerais; University of Brasília
Worked in
Brazil, Chile, Mexico
Key works
“The Structure of Dependence” (1970); Imperialismo y dependencia (Imperialism and Dependence, 1978)

Life

Dos Santos was born in Minas Gerais in 1936. He studied sociology and politics in Belo Horizonte and then went to the new University of Brasília, where he worked with Ruy Mauro Marini (1932–1997) and Vânia Bambirra (1940–2015), his wife and collaborator. All three belonged to the small revolutionary organization known as POLOP (Workers’ Politics), founded in 1961.

The military coup of 1964 drove the group from the university and, in time, from Brazil. Dos Santos went into exile in Chile in 1966, where he led research on dependency at the University of Chile’s Center for Socio-Economic Studies. Marini, Bambirra, and Andre Gunder Frank were there too. Santiago, home to the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and, after 1970, to Allende’s socialist government, was then the intellectual capital of the continent. The coup of 1973 scattered the group again. Dos Santos moved to Mexico and taught at the National Autonomous University (UNAM) until Brazil’s amnesty allowed him to return in 1979. He later taught at the Fluminense Federal University in Niterói and remained active in Latin American debates until his death in 2018.

Work

Dos Santos’s 1970 article “The Structure of Dependence,” published in the American Economic Review, gave the theory its most quoted definition: dependence is a situation in which the economy of some countries is conditioned by the development and expansion of another economy to which they are subjected. He distinguished historical forms of dependence. Colonial dependence was based on trade monopolies. Financial-industrial dependence, from the late nineteenth century, was based on the export of raw materials. The new technological-industrial dependence after 1945 came with multinational corporations that invested in the industries of the periphery itself.

This last point mattered politically. Industrialization in Brazil or Mexico did not end dependence, because the periphery still relied on imported machinery, foreign technology, and foreign capital, and its industries served a narrow, high-income market. Marini’s Dialéctica de la dependencia (The Dialectics of Dependency, 1973) added the concept of superexploitation: capitalists in the periphery made up for the value they lost in the world market by paying workers less than the value of their labor power. Bambirra’s El capitalismo dependiente latinoamericano (Latin American Dependent Capitalism, 1974) classified the region’s economies by the timing and form of their industrialization. All three concluded that the way out lay in socialism, not in reformed capitalism, which set them apart from both the structuralists and the more reformist dependency writers such as Fernando Henrique Cardoso.
