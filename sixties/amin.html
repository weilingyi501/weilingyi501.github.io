Samir Amin

For half a century Amin worked from Dakar on one problem: how capitalism, as a world system, produces wealth at its center and polarization at its edges, and how the peoples of the South might break out of it.

Born
3 September 1931, Cairo, Egypt
Died
12 August 2018, Paris, France
Education
Paris: political science (1952), statistics (1956), doctorate in economics (1957)
Worked in
Egypt, Mali, France, Senegal
Key works
Accumulation on a World Scale (1970); Unequal Development (1973); Eurocentrism (1988); Delinking (1985)

Life

Amin was born in Cairo in 1931 to an Egyptian father and a French mother, both doctors, and grew up in Port Said. He went to Paris in 1947, studied political science, statistics, and economics, and joined the French Communist Party as a student, though he later moved away from its Soviet line. His 1957 doctoral thesis, on how precapitalist economies are reshaped when they are integrated into the world market, became the basis of his first major book.

He returned to Egypt to work in the state’s economic development organization during the Nasser years, and from 1960 to 1963 he advised the planning ministry of newly independent Mali. After teaching in France and Senegal, he directed the UN African Institute for Economic Development and Planning (IDEP) in Dakar from 1970 to 1980. In 1980 he founded the Third World Forum in Dakar, a network of researchers across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, and later the World Forum for Alternatives. He remained a public figure in African and world debates until his death in Paris in 2018.

Work

Accumulation on a World Scale (1970) argued that capital accumulation should be studied at the level of the world economy, not the nation. In the center, accumulation is autocentric: the production of capital goods and of mass consumer goods support each other, and wages can rise with productivity. In the periphery, accumulation is extraverted: production is organized around exports and the luxury consumption of a small elite, so growth does not spread to the rest of society. The outcome is a polarization between center and periphery that capitalist expansion reproduces rather than closes.

In Unequal Development (1973) and Imperialism and Unequal Development (1976), Amin built on Arghiri Emmanuel’s theory of unequal exchange and placed it inside this broader account. Because wages in the periphery are held far below those in the center for comparable productivity, trade transfers value from South to North. He later developed this into what he called the law of worldwide value: in a world where goods and capital move freely but labor does not, the price of labor power differs across countries far more than productivity does.

His political conclusion was delinking (Delinking, 1985). He did not mean autarky. He meant that a country should subordinate its external relations to the logic of its own popular, national development, instead of adjusting to the demands of the world market. In Eurocentrism (1988) he criticized the belief that Europe’s path was a universal model, and the habit of reading world history as if it led necessarily to the West.

Friends and allies

Amin met Andre Gunder Frank in 1967 and recognized in his work on Latin America a parallel to his own on Africa and Asia. With Frank, Giovanni Arrighi, and Immanuel Wallerstein he wrote Dynamics of Global Crisis (1982) and Transforming the Revolution (1990). The four often disagreed. Amin, for example, rejected Frank’s later claim that the world system was five thousand years old, insisting that capitalism was qualitatively new. He wrote a short and moving tribute to Frank after his death in 2005 (see the profile of Frank).
